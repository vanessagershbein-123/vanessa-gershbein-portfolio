Diagnosing a business: distribution, velocity, price and promotion

BrandLesson 2.214 min

Goal: Break a sales change into its parts, from currency and acquisitions down to volume, price, promotion and what shoppers actually bought.

Skills: Data & chartsBusiness math

In the same summer quarter of 2026, two snack and nutrition companies reported sales bridges that were mirror images. Hershey's organic salesSales growth after removing currency moves and businesses bought or sold, so you see what the existing brands did. rose 0.8%: net price added about 11 points, while volume fell about 10 points, which Hershey put down to elasticityHow much the quantity sold changes when price changes. An elasticity of −1.5 means a 10% price rise cuts units about 15%., shipment timing and planned portfolio changes1. BellRing's net salesSales after discounts, returns and trade promotions: the revenue a company reports. rose 6.8% on volume up 14.9% and price/mixThe part of a sales change that comes from price changes and from shifts toward pricier or cheaper items. down 8.1%. For its Premier Protein brand, BellRing said the volume came from extra promotion and new distribution, and the extra promotional spending weighed on price/mix2.

Hershey raised prices and sold less. BellRing cut its effective prices and sold more. Neither headline number, 0.8% or 6.8%, tells you whether the business is healthy. The bridge underneath does.

Words to know

A sales bridge (some teams call it a walk) splits a change in sales into pieces that add up to the total. These are the pieces you'll see:

Term What it means A quick example
Net sales What the company books: its shipments to retailers, after trade discounts The revenue line in an earnings release
Organic sales Sales growth after removing currency swings and businesses bought or sold Reported +5%, minus 3 points from an acquisition = +2% organic
FX Foreign exchange: currency moves that change what overseas sales are worth in dollars A stronger euro makes European sales worth more
M&A Mergers and acquisitions: businesses bought or sold Buying a coconut water producer
Volume How many units, cases or pounds moved 1 million cases
Price/mix Sales change from price, plus shifts toward pricier or cheaper items Selling more large bags raises your average price
Net price realization How much of a price change actually reached net sales, after discounts List price up 10%, deeper deals give back 2 points
Elasticity How much volume changes when price changes −1.5 means a 10% price rise cuts units about 15%
Base volume What you'd sell at regular price, with no promotion running 1,000 units a week
Incremental volume The extra units a promotion adds on top of base 800 more units during the deal
Trade promotion Temporary price cuts, displays and features at a retailer, paid for by the brand "2 for $5"
Retail takeaway What shoppers bought, from scanner data The same thing as consumption
Lapping Comparing against the same period a year earlier Lapping a big launch makes this year look weak

Step 1: Take out what isn't the brand

Start by removing currency and acquisitions, because neither tells you anything about shoppers. Conagra's net sales fell 1.4% in the quarter ended August 2026: currency added 0.2%, acquisitions and divestitures took away 0.5%, and organic sales fell 1.1%. Inside that organic number, price/mix added 1.0% and volume fell 2.1%, yet Conagra gained dollar shareYour dollar sales divided by the whole category's dollar sales. in categories from frozen vegetables to hot dogs3.

Volume down with share up usually means the category is shrinking faster than you are. That's a category problem first and a brand problem second.

Step 2: Split volume from price and mix

Price/mix bundles two things: changes in what you charge, and shifts in what people buy, such as more of a cheaper size. Volume is how many units moved. When price is up and volume is down, the question is elasticity: did you lose more units than the price increase was worth? When volume is up and price is down, the question is what you paid for it.

Here's a bridge in miniature. For example, say last year you sold 100 units at $2.00, which is $200. This year you sold 95 units at $2.20, which is $209. Sales grew 4.5%, but volume fell 5% and price rose 10%. The pieces multiply: 0.95 × 1.10 = 1.045. In quarterly results, companies show them as points that roughly add up, the way Hershey showed about 11 points of price against about 10 points of lost volume.

Step 3: Split base from promoted volume

Base volumeWhat a brand would sell at its regular price, with no promotion running. is what you'd sell at your regular price. Incremental volumeThe extra units a promotion adds on top of base volume. is what a promotion adds on top. The catch is that every promotion also discounts sales you'd have made anyway. Circana calls that subsidizationPromoted units you'd have sold anyway at full price, so the discount bought nothing extra.: the share of promoted volume you likely would have sold at base price4.

For example, say you normally sell 1,000 units a week at full price. During a two-week promotion, you sell 1,800 a week. About 1,000 of those you'd have sold anyway, so they're subsidized, and only about 800 are incremental. You gave the discount on all 1,800 units to win roughly 800 extra. Whether that pays depends on the margin you gave up on the units you'd have sold anyway, which is exactly the math in a promotion breakeven.

Watch how much of your category sells on deal. In Premier Protein's ready-to-drink shake category, 25% of volume sold on a price reduction in the quarter, up 5 points from a year earlier2. When that number climbs, shoppers learn to wait for the deal, and your base gets harder to read.

Step 4: Check shipments against what shoppers bought

Your net sales are shipments to retailers. Scanner dataStore checkout scans that firms like Circana and NielsenIQ collect from retailers and sell to many brands. is what shoppers bought. Simply Good Foods' OWYN shakes saw net sales fall 3.3% in the quarter it reported in January 2026, while retail takeawayWhat shoppers actually bought at the register, as measured by scanner data. Also called consumption. rose 17.8%. Retailers had started the quarter holding too much OWYN inventory, a lingering effect of an earlier product quality issue5. Shoppers were buying more. Retailers were ordering less.

Step 5: Check what you're lapping

Every comparison has a base. Hershey's candy retail takeaway grew 0.8% in its third quarter, but because it was lappingComparing against the same period a year earlier, especially when last year had something unusual, like a launch. a big Reese launch from the year before, its share fell1. A launch, a stock-up or an extra week last year can make an ordinary month look like a slide.

Currency or acquisitions Organic sales
Price or mix Net price per unit, and which sizes and items grew
Volume Distribution × velocity, and base vs. promoted
Retailer ordering Scanner sales vs. net sales, and retailer inventory
The comparison What happened in the same period last year

In an interview

When a case says "sales fell 8%, what's going on?", walk the bridge out loud: organic or not, volume or price, base or promoted, shipments or consumption, and what you're lapping. Say which branch you'd check first and why, then follow the data rather than your first guess.

Self-check: Your snack brand's net sales are up 6%: price/mix +9%, volume −3%. Scanner sales are up 2%. Write a one-line diagnosis.

Something like: "Our growth is all price, our volume is shrinking, and net sales are running 4 points ahead of scanner sales. Before we call it a good quarter, compare the units we shipped with the units shoppers bought, to see whether retailers built inventory we'll give back, and check elasticity by retailer".

Check your understanding

4 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. Net sales grew 4% on volume up 12% and price/mix down 7%, and the share of volume sold on promotion doubled. What's the main risk?

    Show answer

    You're buying volume with discounts that also subsidize sales you'd have made at full price. Volume bought on deal often includes shoppers who'd have paid full price. Check how much of the promoted volume was truly incremental before you repeat the plan.

  2. Your shipments fell 7% this quarter, but scanner sales rose 4%. What do you check first?

    Show answer

    Whether retailers cut their inventory of your products. When shoppers buy more and retailers order less, the gap is usually retailer inventory. Find out why before you plan a fix for a demand problem you don't have.

  3. Sales are flat and share is down a point. Last year in the same period, you launched a big new item. What do you tell your director?

    Show answer

    We're lapping last year's launch, so compare the base business and the two-year trend before deciding anything. A big launch inflates last year's base. The two-year trend shows whether the brand is really slipping.

  4. Reported sales grew 11%, and 8 points came from a company you acquired. How do you describe the brand's growth?

    Show answer

    About 3% organic, which is the number to judge the brand on. Strip out acquisitions and currency first. What's left is what your brands actually did.
