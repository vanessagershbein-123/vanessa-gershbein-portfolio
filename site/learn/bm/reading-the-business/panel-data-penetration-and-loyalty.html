Panel data: penetration, buy rate and loyalty

BrandLesson 2.313 min

Goal: Split sales into how many households buy and how much each one buys, and use that to judge where growth will come from.

Skills: Data & chartsResearch & insight

Freshpet's sales grew 10% in the third quarter of 2026, yet it added few new buyers. Its third-quarter report showed household penetrationThe share of households that bought you at least once in a period. up 1%, while the total buy rateWhat each buying household spends on you in a period., what each buying household spends, was up 9%. A group of households it calls Most Valuable Pet Parents made up 72% of its sales, and that group grew 8%1.

Freshpet is the exception rather than the rule. Across the brands Worldpanel by Numerator tracks, 68% of the brands that grew in 2025 added households2. Both things are true, and panel dataA large sample of households that record what they buy, so you can follow the same buyers over time. is how you find out which story your brand is in.

What a panel measures

Scanner dataStore checkout scans that firms like Circana and NielsenIQ collect from retailers and sell to many brands. tells you what sold. A household panel tells you who bought it: a large sample of homes that record what they buy, so you can follow the same people over time. Penetration is the number of buying households divided by all households, times 1003.

From there, the math is simple: sales = households in the market × penetration × buy rate, and buy rate = trips per buyer × spend per trip.

For example, say a town has 10,000 households, and 2,000 of them bought your salsa this year: 20% penetration. Each buyer bought it 4 times at $3 a trip, so the buy rate is $12, and sales are 10,000 × 20% × $12 = $24,000. To grow 10%, you could add 200 buying households, or get each buyer to spend $1.20 more. The panel tells you which of those actually happened.

Number What it tells you What usually moves it
Penetration How many households bought you at least once Distribution, visibility, advertising that reaches light buyers, trial sizes
Purchase frequency How often buyers come back Habit, being easy to find, being remembered

Why most growth comes from more buyers

Researchers at the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute call it double jeopardyThe pattern that smaller brands have fewer buyers, and those buyers are also slightly less loyal.: small brands have fewer buyers, and those buyers are slightly less loyal. The pattern was first spotted in the 1960s and shows up in many categories, from packaged goods to retail banking. Writing about business markets in 2021, the institute's conclusion is blunt: you can't grow sustainably by focusing on loyalty alone, because a bigger brand has many more buyers who are only slightly more loyal4.

For example, say Brand A reaches 24% of households and its buyers buy it 4 times a year, while Brand B reaches 8% and its buyers buy it 3 times. A sells four times as much as B, and almost all of that gap is the number of buyers, not how loyal they are. That's the typical shape of double jeopardy.

Light buyers also matter more than the old 80/20 rule suggests. Byron Sharp and Charles Graham found the real split is closer to 60/20 over five years, about 50/20 over a year and about 40/20 in a quarter. So the light 80% of buyers deliver almost half of a brand's sales5.

That doesn't mean loyalty is irrelevant. Sharp himself writes that some growth comes from existing heavier buyers becoming a little more frequent and loyal. He also notes that in categories where people rotate between brands, penetration grows two ways: recruiting new buyers, and getting the many very light buyers who don't buy you every period to buy a little more often6.

Recruiting isn't enough

The same Brand Footprint report has a second finding. Of the brands that recruited new households in 2024, only 22% kept them and were chosen more often a year later. What set those brands apart was a habit forming early: two in three were being bought more often inside their first year2.

So the panel question is never just "did we add buyers?" It's "did we add buyers, and did they come back?" Freshpet sits at the other end for now: a small brand in a fresh pet food category it's still converting shoppers into, with a heavy-buyer group that already drives most of its sales. Even there, part of the buy-rate rise came from having more of those heavy-buying households, the mirror image of the mix trap.

How to read a panel report

  • Read penetration and buy rate together. Either one alone can mislead.
  • Watch for the mix trap. When light buyers drift away, the average buy rate rises even if nobody changed their habits.
  • Check the time window. Over a longer window, more light buyers show up, so penetration rises and buy rate grows less than the window does. Only compare a 52-week number with another 52-week number.
  • Ask where lapsed buyers went. A rival brand, the store brandProducts sold under a retailer's own brand name, like Kirkland Signature at Costco. or out of the category each calls for a different fix.

Byron Sharp revisits the laws of brand growth in this conversation, including penetration, double jeopardy and why heavy buyers of a brand often buy it less than you'd think.

Self-check: Your brand's sales are flat. Penetration fell from 20% to 18%, and buy rate rose 11%. Your director says loyalty is up. What do you say?

The math checks out: 18 × 1.11 is about 20, so flat. But you lost a tenth of your buyers, and part of the buy-rate rise may just be light buyers leaving, which lifts the average. Pull the panel's buyer flows to see who left and where they went, and put the plan behind winning households back, not only rewarding the ones you kept.

Check your understanding

4 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. A mid-size brand wants to grow by launching a rewards program for its heaviest buyers. What's the main risk?

    Show answer

    Its heavy buyers are few and already buy a lot, so most of the growth has to come from reaching more households. Double jeopardy says bigger brands mainly have more buyers, not much more loyal ones. A plan aimed only at people already buying you a lot has little room to grow.

  2. Penetration rose 15% and buy rate fell 5%. What happened to sales?

    Show answer

    Up about 9%. Sales = penetration × buy rate, so 1.15 × 0.95 ≈ 1.09. Adding buyers usually lowers the average buy rate, because new buyers tend to be light.

  3. A panel report shows "new buyers" up 30% this year. What do you check before celebrating?

    Show answer

    Whether they come back for another purchase, and whether they're first-time buyers or light buyers returning. Both count as penetration growth, but they tell different stories. A good early test is whether they buy again: Brand Footprint found the brands that kept their recruits had a buying habit forming in the first year.

  4. Which plan is most likely to grow a mid-size brand's penetration?

    Show answer

    Wider distribution plus advertising that reaches light buyers of the category. Penetration grows when more households can find you and think of you. Multipacks and points mostly sell more to people who already buy.
