The monthly business review: from data to a recommendation

BrandLesson 2.412 min

Goal: Turn a month of data into one headline, the evidence behind it, and a decision someone can make.

Skills: Writing & storytellingData & charts

Read two earnings headlines from the same season. Vita Coco's third-quarter 2026 release led with net salesSales after discounts, returns and trade promotions: the revenue a company reports. up 34%, driven by coconut water growth of 15%, with organic growthSales growth after removing currency moves and businesses bought or sold, so you see what the existing brands did. of 18% right there in the same line. Read the tables and you find why both numbers are needed: about 16 points of the 34% came from buying Copra, a coconut water producer1.

Vital Farms' third-quarter headline led with revenue up 9.7% from the second quarter and a record share of shell eggs. Its revenue was down 8.4% from the year before, a number that appears further down the release2.

Both headlines are accurate, and each one chose its numbers. In your monthly review, your director will read past your headline the way an analyst reads past these, so write one that survives the second read.

Words to know

Term What it means
Monthly business review A regular meeting where the brand team reports how the business did and what should happen next. Some teams call it a business update or performance review
Scorecard The same set of measures shown every month, so changes stand out
Headline One sentence that says what happened, why, and what you want
Driver The cause behind a number moving, like distribution, price or a competitor's promotion
The ask The decision you want leadership to make
Appendix Backup detail you don't present but can pull up if asked

Names and formats vary by company; the parts above are common to most.

Lead with the answer

Barbara Minto's Pyramid Principle puts it simply: your thinking is easy for a reader to grasp when you present your ideas organized as a pyramid under a single point3. McKinsey's alumni profile of Minto describes the problem she kept finding in reports: findings and conclusions jumbled together, with neither leading clearly to a recommendation. In her words, "The problem was the thinking, not the language"4.

A list isn't a point. Minto's own course warns that saying there are three reasons for a change is not an insight in itself. The point at the top has to say what the reasons add up to3.

From numbers to a headline in three steps

  1. List what moved, and by how much. For example: sales down 3.5%, units down 9%, our price up 6% since March, distribution flat, and our main competitor held its price.
  2. Group the facts by cause. Units fell right after our price rose, distribution didn't change, and the competitor didn't follow. That's one story, not five numbers.
  3. Say what they add up to, then what you want. "Sales fell 3.5% because shoppers bought 9% fewer pizzas after our March price increase, which our main competitor didn't match. We recommend testing a smaller price gap at our two largest retailers before deciding whether to keep it."

Check the math before you present it: 1.06 × 0.91 = 0.965, so a 6% price rise and 9% fewer units do give about 3.5% lower sales. The headline should survive that check.

What, so what, now what

Stanford's Matt Abrahams calls "What? So what? Now what?" the most widely useful structure he knows: what's happening, why it matters to this audience, and what should happen next5. Here's how it maps onto a monthly review:

Part In your review For example
What The month in one line, with the driver "Share slipped as a rival's new item took display space at our biggest retailer"
So what What it means for the plan "Our base velocity elsewhere is holding, so this is one account, not the brand"
Now what The decision you need "Approve a shelf-and-display plan with that retailer for next quarter"

When the numbers go off track

OpenStax describes the loop every review runs on. If the numbers stay within the forecast range, move on. When they fall outside it, find the root cause and adjust6. Your review is where that root cause gets named, with a number behind it.

A one-page review that works

  1. The headline: what happened, why, and what you want.
  2. The same scorecard every month: sales, share, distribution, velocityHow fast you sell where you're stocked: sales per point of distribution, per store or per TDP., price, the share of volume sold on promotion, and consumption against shipments.
  3. Two or three drivers, each tied to a number from the scorecard.
  4. Timing vs. real change: what you're lappingComparing against the same period a year earlier, especially when last year had something unusual, like a launch., retailer inventory moves, calendar shifts.
  5. Decisions, each with an owner and a date.
  6. An appendix for everything that doesn't support the headline.

That structure is a working habit rather than a rule, so adapt it to your team.

Common traps

  • Walking the scorecard in order. Leadership has to do the synthesis for you.
  • "A mixed month." That says you haven't decided what the month meant.
  • Explaining timing as performance. A shipment shift or a lapped launch isn't a trend.
  • Burying the bad number. It will come up anyway, so put it where you can frame it.

Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic turns a cluttered data presentation into a single "big idea" sentence and a specific recommendation, with a full before-and-after makeover.

Self-check: Dollar sales +3%, category +6%, share down 0.4 points, %ACV up 5%, velocity down 2%. Write the headline.

Something like: "We grew 3% only because we're in more stores. Each store is selling 2% less of us while the category grows 6%, so we're losing share, and we need to find out why shoppers are choosing us less before we add more distribution." It names the result, the driver and the next step.

Check your understanding

4 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. Which headline is strongest?

    Show answer

    "Share fell 0.4 points because velocity dropped at our top two retailers after a competitor's price cut; we recommend a targeted response in those two accounts.". It says what happened, why and what to do, in one sentence. The others describe the deck instead of the business.

  2. At the end of your review, your director asks for "more data." What most likely went wrong?

    Show answer

    You didn't state a clear point and recommendation, so there was nothing to decide. A request for more data often means the audience couldn't see the conclusion. Lead with the answer and the decision you need.

  3. Sales fell 4% this month, but the same month last year had an extra week of shipments. How do you handle it?

    Show answer

    Say so up front, adjust for the calendar, and show the underlying trend. Calendar effects aren't performance. Clear them first, so the discussion is about the real trend.

  4. You have six findings from this month's data. What goes first?

    Show answer

    The one-line conclusion they add up to, then the two or three findings that support it. The point at the top has to summarize what's below it. Findings that don't support it go to the appendix.
