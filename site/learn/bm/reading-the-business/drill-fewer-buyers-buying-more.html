Fewer buyers, buying more

BrandDrill 2.212 minIntermediate

Skills: Data & chartsResearch & insight

The prompt

Hypothetical scenario. The brand and all numbers are made up to show the work.

The facts. Coastal Crisp is a kettle chip brand. Household panelA large sample of households that record what they buy, so you can follow the same buyers over time. data for the past 52 weeks:

Year ago This year
Household penetration (share of U.S. households buying at least once) 12.0% 10.5%
Purchase trips per buyer 4.0 4.4
Bags per trip 1.5 1.6
Average price per bag $4.00 $4.10

The brand team is pleased: "Sales are up and loyalty is rising. Let's launch a rewards program for our heavy buyers."

Your task: Work out what drove sales, then say whether you'd back the rewards program and what you'd do instead or as well.

0:00 target 12:00

Your earlier answer

  1. Hint 1 of 3 Split sales into two parts: how many households buy, and what each one spends. Work out both for each year.
  2. Hint 2 of 3 Which part grew and which shrank, and by how much?
  3. Hint 3 of 3 Before you accept "loyalty is rising," ask who left. If the households that left were light buyersPeople who buy a brand rarely. They make up most of any brand's buyers., what happens to the average spend of the ones who stayed?

Do the math first

Sales = households buying × what each buyer spends.

Year ago This year Change
Spend per buyer (trips × bags × price) 4.0 × 1.5 × $4.00 = $24.00 4.4 × 1.6 × $4.10 = $28.86 +20%
Penetration 12.0% 10.5% −12.5%
Sales index (penetration × spend) 288 303 +5%

Sales grew 5%, but the brand lost one in eight of its buyers. A shrinking group spending 20% more covered the gap.

Why "loyalty is rising" may be an illusion

Some of that higher spend per buyer can be pure arithmetic. Light buyers are the easiest to lose, and when they leave, the average across the buyers who remain goes up even if nobody changed their habits.

Say Coastal Crisp had 1,200 buyers in a sample: 400 heavy buyers spending $48 a year and 800 light buyers spending $12. That's an average of $24. If 150 light buyers drift away and nobody else changes, the remaining 1,050 average about $25.71, up 7%, with no one buying a single extra bag.

A strong answer

"Sales are up 5%, but we lost 12.5% of our buyers, and part of the jump in spend per buyer is just light buyers leaving. A rewards program would pay the people already buying the most. I'd first pull buyer flows from the panel: how many buyers we lost and gained, and which brands the lapsed buyers went to. Then I'd put money into reaching more households: distribution where we're thin, visibility in store, and advertising that reaches light category buyers, not only our fans."

What interviewers push on

  • "Isn't a loyal core a good thing?" Yes, but a brand that only grows with fewer buyers is narrowing. Eventually the heavy buyers can't buy more chips, and there's no new base to grow from.
  • "How would you know whether heavy buyers really are buying more?" Track the same households over time. If the people who bought last year are each buying more, it's real. If the average only rose because light buyers left, it isn't.

Practice twist

Next year, penetrationThe share of households that bought you at least once in a period. recovers to 11.5% and spend per buyer falls back to $26.00. Are sales up or down from this year?

Answer: 11.5 × $26.00 = 299, down about 1% from 303. Even with 10% more buyers, sales slip, because spend per buyer fell almost 10%. Recovery of buyers is the right direction, but you'd want to know why each buyer is spending less.
