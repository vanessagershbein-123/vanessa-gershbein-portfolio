Share, distribution and velocity

BrandLesson 2.113 min

Goal: Read the three numbers every brand team checks first: your slice of the category, how many shelves you're on, and how fast you sell from them.

Skills: Data & charts

In the quarter that ended in September 2026, two brands both added a lot of shelf space. BellRing's Premier Protein shakes grew their total distribution pointsTotal distribution points: %ACV added up across every item in your line. It grows when more stores carry you or when stores carry more of your items., a measure of how much shelf presence a brand has, 36% over the year before. Their consumption grew 9%, by a measure that includes BellRing's own estimates for channels the data doesn't track1. Freshpet grew its distribution points 14% and its Nielsen-measured sales 12.7%2.

Same move, very different payoff, at least on the headline numbers. Freshpet's extra shelf space turned into sales almost point for point, while Premier Protein's turned into about a quarter as much. To see what's going on, you need three numbers, and the third one does most of the explaining.

First, where the numbers come from

Every time a product is scanned at a store checkout, the sale is recorded. Firms like Circana and NielsenIQ collect those scans from retailers, combine them and sell the result to brands, so you can see your own sales and your competitors' week by week. People call it scanner dataStore checkout scans that firms like Circana and NielsenIQ collect from retailers and sell to many brands., or syndicated data, because the same dataset is sold to many companies. Not every retailer shares its scans, which is why companies sometimes add their own estimates for "untracked channels".

Here are the words you'll hear most, in plain English:

Term What it means A quick example
Category The set of products shoppers treat as alternatives, as the data provider defines it All ready-to-drink protein shakes
Item (or SKU) One specific product: one flavor in one size Chocolate, 11-ounce, 4-pack
Dollar sales What shoppers spent on you at the register $2 million last quarter
Unit sales How many packs shoppers bought 500,000 packs
Dollar share Your dollar sales ÷ the category's dollar sales $2M of a $20M category = 10%
Unit share Your unit sales ÷ the category's unit sales 500,000 of 4 million packs = 12.5%
ACV (all commodity volume) A store's total sales of everything it sells, used to measure how big the store is A supercenter's ACV is many times a corner store's
%ACV distribution How much of the market's total store sales happen in stores that carry your item 80% ACV: the stores carrying you do 80% of all sales
TDPs (total distribution points) %ACV added up across all your items 3 items at 50% ACV each = 150 TDPs
Velocity How fast you sell where you're stocked: sales per point of distribution, or per store per week $1,500 per point of ACV a week
Consumption (or takeaway) What shoppers bought, from scanner data, as opposed to what you shipped to retailers Consumption up 9%, shipments up 15%

Share: your slice of the category

Share is your sales as a percentage of the whole category's sales3. Run it on dollars and you get dollar shareYour dollar sales divided by the whole category's dollar sales.. Run it on units and you get unit shareYour unit sales divided by the whole category's unit sales.. A premium brand's dollar share sits above its unit share, because each unit costs more, and a value brand's sits below. When the two move in opposite directions, price or mix is doing the work: a higher price, or more sales of pricier items.

Share also answers a different question from sales. In the third quarter of 2026, Vital Farms reached a record dollar and volume share of shell eggs, while its net revenue fell 8.4%, mostly from lower price and mix4. Share is relative: if the category's dollars fall faster than yours, you gain share while selling less. The reverse happens too, so never report one without the other.

Distribution: how many shelves you're on

Store count treats a corner store the same as a supercenter. Percent ACV distributionPercent ACV distribution: how much of a market's total store sales (its all commodity volume, or ACV) happen in stores that carry your item. At 80% ACV, the stores carrying you do 80% of all sales. fixes that by weighting each store by its total sales. If the stores that carry your product account for $800 million of a $1 billion market's sales, you're at 80% ACVAll commodity volume: a store's total sales of everything it sells. Data firms use it to weight how big each store is.5.

Here's why the weighting matters. For example, say a small town has three stores: a supercenter that sells $8 million a year of everything, a grocery store that sells $1.5 million and a corner store that sells $0.5 million. If only the supercenter carries you, you're in one store out of three, but you're at 80% ACV, because that store does $8 million of the town's $10 million in sales. Shoppers can find you where most of the shopping happens.

Total distribution points (TDPs) add up the %ACV of every item in your line. For example, five items each at 60% ACV give you 300 TDPs. TDPs grow when more stores carry you, or when the stores that already carry you stock more of your items. For example, say two brands are both at 70% ACV. One has six items in every one of those stores (6 × 70 = 420 TDPs), and the other has two (140 TDPs). Same stores, but the first has three times the presence on shelf, and usually far more facings for shoppers to notice.

Freshpet's numbers show why the second part matters. Its store count rose 5% to more than 31,000 stores, but its distribution points rose 14%, because more stores put in larger and double fridges. Three-quarters of those stores still have only one fridge, and when one club retailer installed very large fridges, the company said its velocitiesHow fast you sell where you're stocked: sales per point of distribution, per store or per TDP. jumped too2.

Velocity: how fast each shelf sells

Velocity is sales divided by distribution: per store, per TDP or per million dollars of ACV. It lets you compare items fairly, whatever their footprint5.

For example, say your granola sells $90,000 a week at 60% ACV. That's $90,000 ÷ 60 = $1,500 per point of distribution. A rival sells $60,000 a week at only 30% ACV, which is $2,000 per point. The rival sells less in total, but where shoppers can see both brands, they pick the rival faster. With your distribution, and if its velocity held, the rival would outsell you.

Put the three together and you get the line every brand manager carries around: sales = distribution × velocity. Check it on the granola, for example: 60 points × $1,500 = $90,000 a week.

Back to the opening puzzle. On the headline numbers, Freshpet's sales per distribution point held roughly steady. Most of its new points were bigger fridges in stores that already sold it, since its store count rose only 5%. Premier Protein's sales per point fell by roughly a fifth. Treat that as rough, because BellRing's consumption figure includes its own estimates for channels where distribution isn't measured. A lower average also can't tell you whether the new points sell slowly or the old ones slipped; only store- and item-level data can. When distribution grows much faster than sales, that's the question to answer before you celebrate the shelf space.

The question The number that answers it
Are we winning against competitors? Dollar share and unit share, read together
Can shoppers find us? %ACV and TDPs
Do shoppers choose us when they see us? Velocity
Why did sales move? Distribution × velocity first, then price

In an interview

When a case says sales are up, ask whether distribution grew or velocity did. Sales up on wider distribution with falling velocity is a warning dressed as a win. Sales up on rising velocity in the same stores means shoppers are choosing you more often.

Self-check: A new flavor reached 60% ACV in its first quarter. The original flavor sits at 95% ACV and sells three times as much. Is the new flavor underperforming?

Compare velocity, not sales. The new flavor sells a third as much on about 63% as much distribution, so each point of its distribution sells roughly half as fast as the original's. Distribution isn't the whole gap. Check where it sits on shelf, whether shoppers are still trying it for the first time, and how many come back.

Check your understanding

4 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. Your brand's sales grew 8% this year while its TDPs grew 25%. What's the most useful next question?

    Show answer

    Why is each distribution point selling less, and is it the new points or the old ones?. Sales = distribution × velocity, so 1.08 ÷ 1.25 means each point now sells about 14% less. Find out whether the new stores and items sell slowly or the existing ones slipped.

  2. Your dollar share rose a point this year, but your unit share fell. What most likely happened?

    Show answer

    You sold relatively fewer units, at prices that rose faster than the category's. Dollar share up with unit share down means price did the work. That can be fine, but it can also mean shoppers are drifting to cheaper options.

  3. Two items each sell $50,000 a week. Item A is at 90% ACV and Item B is at 45%. Which one do you push for more distribution?

    Show answer

    Item B, because it sells twice as fast per point, so it has the stronger case for new stores. Same sales on half the distribution means double the velocity, so Item B has earned a shot at more stores. Expect the new stores to sell below its current rate, though, since it's probably already in the stores that suit it best.

  4. A retailer will add your brand to 500 small convenience stores. Your store count rises 20%, but your %ACV rises only 2 points. How do you describe the win?

    Show answer

    Real but small: the new stores account for little of the market's sales, so expect a modest lift. %ACV weights each store by its total sales. Many small stores can add a lot of locations and very little sales potential.
