Build the quarter

BrandDrill 2.315 minIntermediate

Skills: Sizing & forecastingBusiness math

The prompt

Hypothetical scenario. The brand and all numbers are made up to show the work.

The facts. You own the second-quarter forecast (13 weeks) for Summit Oats granola bars, in units sold to shoppers:

  • Today: Summit Oats is in 10,000 stores and sells a steady 12 units per store per week.
  • New distribution: A grocery chain adds the brand to 2,000 more stores starting in week 5. New stores usually sell at half the normal rate for their first four weeks, then 80% after that.
  • One promotion: A two-week price cut in weeks 7 and 8 at a different chain, where Summit Oats is already sold in 4,000 stores. Past events at that chain lifted units 60% in those stores during the promotion, followed by a 15% dip for the next two weeks while shoppers worked through their pantries.

Your task: Build the quarter's forecast by component. Then name the two biggest risks and what you'd hand to the demand planning team.

Your earlier answer

  1. Hint 1 of 3 Don't start with a total. Build it line by line: the base first, then each change on top of it.
  2. Hint 2 of 3 For the new stores, count the weeks at half speed and the weeks at 80% separately.
  3. Hint 3 of 3 The promotion needs two lines: the lift during the event and the dip after it. Then ask how shipments will differ from what shoppers buy.

The forecast, one driver at a time

Component Math Units
Base 10,000 stores × 12 × 13 weeks 1,560,000
New stores, weeks 5 to 8 2,000 × 6 (half rate) × 4 weeks 48,000
New stores, weeks 9 to 13 2,000 × 9.6 (80% rate) × 5 weeks 96,000
Promotion lift, weeks 7 and 8 4,000 × 7.2 extra (60% of 12) × 2 weeks 57,600
Post-promotion dip, weeks 9 and 10 4,000 × −1.8 (15% of 12) × 2 weeks −14,400
Total 1,747,200

Why build it this way

  • Each line has an owner and a reason. If the quarter misses, you can see which assumption broke. A single "up 12%" number can't tell you that.
  • The base and the events stay separate. Promotions and new distribution come and go. The base is what the brand does on its own, so keep it clean.
  • Include the dip when your history shows one. Past events at this chain show a 15% dip, which gives back a quarter of the lift (14,400 of 57,600 units). Leave it out and you overstate the event's net gain by a third.

The two biggest risks

  1. The new stores ramp more slowly or start later. It's the biggest addition after the base, 144,000 units, and the timing is the retailer's call, not yours.
  2. The promotion lift is borrowed from past events. If a competitor promotes the same weeks, or the display doesn't go up, the 60% lift could be half that.

What you hand to demand planning

The components, not just the total: the base rate, the new-store timing and ramp, the event dates, lift and dip. Also flag that shipments will run ahead of this number in weeks 4 and 5, because the chain needs to stock 2,000 new stores before a single shopper buys a bar. Expect the promoting chain to buy ahead of weeks 7 and 8, then order less for a few weeks after.

Practice twist

The chain pushes the new stores back to week 9. What's the new forecast?

Answer: new stores now sell at half rate in weeks 9 to 12 (2,000 × 6 × 4 = 48,000) and at 80% in week 13 (2,000 × 9.6 = 19,200). Total = 1,560,000 + 67,200 + 57,600 − 14,400 = 1,670,400 units, about 76,800 fewer. Tell Operations and Finance the week the date moves, not at quarter end.

How did it go?

Be honest. Missed comes back tomorrow, Partial in 3 days, Nailed it in a week.
