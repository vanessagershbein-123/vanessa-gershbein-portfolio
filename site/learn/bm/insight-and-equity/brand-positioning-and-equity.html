Brand positioning and equity

BrandLesson 4.215 min

Goal: Choose a frame of reference, points of parity and a point of difference, and see brand equity show up where it counts: in price.

Skills: Positioning & strategyPricing & promotion

Anheuser-Busch InBev sells two big light beers in the U.S. Its annual report describes one with a specific promise: Michelob ULTRA has just 95 calories and 2.5 carbs. It describes the other with a category: Bud Light is a premium American light lager. Michelob ULTRA, rolled out nationally in 2002, has grown into the leading U.S. beer brand by volume, according to Circana data the report cites1. In the third quarter of 2026, AB InBev said Michelob Ultra had been the industry's #1 volume share gainer for eight straight quarters2.

Same company, same category, two light beers. One gives drinkers a concrete reason to pick it. The other names the shelf it sits on. Bud Light also took a heavy hit from a consumer controversy, so ULTRA's climb is positioning plus a shock, not positioning alone.

Words to know

Term What it means A quick example
Positioning The place you want your brand to hold in shoppers' minds, compared with the alternatives The light beer with the fewest carbs
Frame of reference The category or set of alternatives you're compared with Light beer, or all beer
Points of parity (POPs) What you must match to be considered at all A light beer still has to taste good
Points of difference (PODs) What you do better that buyers care about 95 calories and 2.5 carbs
Reason to believe The proof behind a point of difference The nutrition label
Positioning statement An internal sentence that pins the choices down For [target], [brand] is the [frame] that [difference] because [proof]
Brand mantra Three to five words that capture what the brand stands for, used inside the company "Fresh food, fast"
Brand equity The extra value a brand name adds, compared with the same product without it Shoppers paying more for the brand than for the store brand
Meaningful, different, salient Kantar's three measures of equity: meets needs, stands apart, comes to mind quickly A brand people like, see as unique and think of first

Three choices: the frame, the parity, the difference

In a 2002 Harvard Business Review article, Kevin Lane Keller, Brian Sternthal and Alice Tybout argued that effective positioning needs points of parityWhat a brand must match to be considered at all in its category. as well as points of differenceWhat a brand does better than the alternatives that buyers care about and believe., and that managers pay too little attention to the frame of referenceThe category or set of alternatives a brand is compared with. It decides what counts as a point of difference. their brands compete in3. OpenStax defines positioning as deciding and communicating how you want the market to think and feel about a product, and its positioning-statement template names the market you compete in, your points of difference and your evidence4.

Make the three choices in order:

  1. Frame of reference: who are you compared with? That decides everything else.
  2. Points of parity: what must you match so shoppers don't rule you out?
  3. Points of difference: what do you do better that this audience cares about, and can you prove it?

The frame changes everything

The classic light beer story shows why. Kellogg's Alice Tybout describes how Miller Lite launched with regular beer as its frame of reference and being less filling as its point of difference, supported by fewer calories. Once Bud Light arrived, shoppers compared light beers with each other, which turned less filling from a point of difference into a point of parity. Bud Light then promoted superior taste, backed by Budweiser's "King of Beers" heritage5.

A point of difference works only inside a frame. When the frame shifts, yesterday's difference becomes the entry ticket. Decades later, Michelob ULTRA found a new difference inside the light beer frame: not lighter in general, but a specific calorie and carb count it could put on the label.

Brand equity: what the positioning is worth

OpenStax puts brand equityThe extra value a brand name adds, compared with the same product without it. It often shows up as a price premium. simply: if a shopper will pay more for one similar product over another because of the brand, that difference is the brand's equity4.

Kantar measures it with three questions: is the brand meaningful, different and salient? Its BrandZ method turns those into Demand Power (demand today), Pricing PowerThe ability to raise your price and lose fewer buyers than competitors would. (the ability to charge a premium) and Future Power (demand to come)6. Kantar's data shows brands with high Pricing Power are bought at double the price of brands with low Pricing Power, and that 94% of Pricing Power is explained by how meaningfully different a brand is seen to be6. These are Kantar's own measures from its own surveys, so read them as a strong pattern, not proof.

Real brands show both sides. e.l.f. says its cosmetics average about $7 in the U.S., against $10 for other leading mass brands and $30 for prestige brands, per Nielsen7. Coty's mass beauty segment went the other way that year: unit volume fell 5%, and price and mix fell 2%, mainly from more discounts and promotions8.

Read that through the three choices. e.l.f.'s frame is prestige makeup, its point of difference is the prestige look at a fraction of the price, and its low price point is the reason to believeThe proof behind a brand's claim, such as an ingredient, a test result or a nutrition label.. A brand without a clear, valued difference often ends up buying volume with promotions, and can still lose it.

The math: how equity shows up in price

An illustration with made-up numbers.

Two brands in the same category each raise their price 10%. Brand A is seen as meaningfully different, with an elasticityHow much the quantity sold changes when price changes. An elasticity of −1.5 means a 10% price rise cuts units about 15%. of −0.8. Brand B looks like everything else on the shelf, with an elasticity of −2.0.

Brand Units after the increase Revenue after the increase
A (elasticity −0.8) 1 − 0.8 × 10% = 92% of before 1.10 × 0.92 = 1.012, up 1.2%
B (elasticity −2.0) 1 − 2.0 × 10% = 80% of before 1.10 × 0.80 = 0.88, down 12%

The formula: revenue after a price change ≈ (1 + price change) × (1 + elasticity × price change).

Why it works: equity shows up twice in the numbers. You can see it in the price premium, and you can see it in how few buyers walk away when the price rises. Here both brands raised prices the same amount, and the meaningfully different brand kept 92% of its volume.

The common mistake: reading any price premium as equity. Compare like with like first, the same pack size and channel on a price-per-unit basis, and check that the premium holds without heavier promotion.

In this Kantar IGNITE talk, Marketing Week columnist Mark Ritson walks through the parts of brand equity, salience versus brand image, distinctive brand assetsThe colors, shapes, logos, characters and sounds that let people recognize a brand without reading its name. and how much differentiation a brand really needs, with KitKat as a positioning example.

In an interview

Positioning questions often hide a frame-of-reference choice. Before you name a point of difference, say who you're competing against, what you must match and what you can prove. If you're asked how you'd know the positioning is working, point to price: is the brand holding its premium without buying volume through deals?

Self-check: A sparkling water brand's positioning is "the most refreshing sparkling water." Sales are flat, and it's discounting more every quarter. What would you check first?

Whether "refreshing" is a point of parity in its frame: every sparkling water claims it, so it can't set the brand apart. Look for a difference that matters to its buyers and that it can prove, such as a flavor, an ingredient or a format. The rising discounts are a warning sign that the brand has little pricing power left.

Check your understanding

4 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. A protein ice cream positions itself against regular ice cream on "more protein." Then a rival protein ice cream launches right next to it, and shoppers start comparing the two protein brands. What happened to its positioning?

    Show answer

    Its point of difference became a point of parity, so it needs a new difference within the protein ice cream frame. Once shoppers compare protein ice creams with each other, "more protein" is the entry ticket. The brand needs a difference that wins inside the new frame.

  2. Brand A raises its price 7% and loses 4% of its units. Brand B raises its price 7% and loses 11%. Which likely has more equity?

    Show answer

    Brand A: its buyers stayed at a higher price, which is what equity looks like in the data. For the same price move, the brand that keeps more of its buyers has the lower elasticity, and a lower elasticity is equity showing up in the numbers.

  3. Your brand sells for 30% more than the store brand. Is that proof of strong equity?

    Show answer

    Not yet: check you're comparing the same pack and channel, and that you're holding the premium without heavier promotion. A premium can come from a bigger pack, a pricier channel or a lot of promotional support. Equity is the premium that holds when those are equal.

  4. A brand manager writes the point of difference as "high quality." What's wrong with it?

    Show answer

    Most rivals claim it too, and it's hard to prove, so it works as a point of parity, not a point of difference. A point of difference has to be something you do better that buyers care about and can believe. "High quality" is what everyone in the aisle says.
