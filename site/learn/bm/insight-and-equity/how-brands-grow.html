How brands grow: penetration, mental availability and distinctive assets

BrandLesson 4.416 min

Goal: Break brand sales into buyers and how often they buy, and see why most growth comes from reaching more buyers.

Skills: Business mathPositioning & strategy

Two small better-for-you brands each reach about 5% of U.S. households. Once Upon a Farm, a kids' food brand, reported household penetrationThe share of households that bought you at least once in a period. of 5.1% for 2025. Its net salesSales after discounts, returns and trade promotions: the revenue a company reports. rose $83.9 million, or 53%, mostly from a 42% rise in volume, driven by new distribution for existing products and new products. It calls its in-store coolers a key entry point into the brand for new households1.

Zevia, a zero-sugar soda, reported U.S. household penetration of 5% for 2024, against 40% to 70% for mainstream category leaders. Its net sales fell from $166.4 million in 2023 to $155.0 million in 2024, largely because it lost distribution in the club channel and at one mass retailer and dropped two product lines2. Zevia describes its drinkers as loyal: they've historically increased their spending on the brand over time and spend more on average than typical soft drink shoppers. In 2025, its net sales recovered to $161.3 million, largely from expanded distribution at one mass retailer2.

One brand grew by putting itself in front of new households. The other had loyal drinkers, but its sales rose and fell with where it was stocked, not with how loyal its drinkers were. They're in different categories, so this isn't a controlled test, but the pattern sits at the core of how brands grow.

The math of brand sales

Circana defines penetration as the number of buying households divided by total households, times 1003. Put it together with how often buyers buy and how much they spend, and you have the whole sales equation.

An illustration with made-up numbers.

A snack brand sells in a country of 130 million households. Its penetration is 10%, its buyers purchase 3 times a year, and they spend $5 each time.

Plan Penetration Purchases per buyer Spend per purchase Sales
Today 10% 3.0 $5 130M × 10% × 3.0 × $5 = $195M
A: more buyers 13% 3.0 $5 $253.5M, up 30%
B: more frequency 10% 3.9 $5 $253.5M, up 30%
A, if frequency also rises a little 13% 3.15 $5 about $266.2M, up 36.5%

The formula: sales = households × penetration × purchase frequencyHow many times a buyer bought you in a period. × spend per purchase.

Why it works: sales are just buyers times how much each one buys. The terms multiply, so a 30% rise in any one of them adds 30% to sales.

Reading it: on paper, plans A and B are equal. In practice, they're not. Double jeopardyThe pattern that smaller brands have fewer buyers, and those buyers are also slightly less loyal. says a brand's loyalty mostly follows its size, so raising frequency 30% without new buyers is rare. Brands that grow share usually see some rise in frequency too, but much less than in penetration, and because new buyers are often light buyersPeople who buy a brand rarely. They make up most of any brand's buyers., frequency can even dip for a while.

The common mistake: building a growth plan on "getting our loyal buyers to buy more," the term that moves least.

What the data says

In Worldpanel by Numerator's 2026 Brand Footprint study, 68% of growing brands added households. Keeping them was harder: of the brands that recruited new households in 2024, 22% kept them and were chosen more often a year later. Doritos, Cheetos and Ruffles all reached more households in 2025 but were bought 6% to 10% less often4.

A 2014 peer-reviewed study of brand share changes found that share growth comes with about three times as much change in penetration as in loyalty5. The Ehrenberg-Bass Institute calls the pattern behind this double jeopardy: smaller brands have fewer buyers, who are also less loyal. Its conclusion is that a brand can't grow sustainably by focusing on loyalty alone6.

Put it together. Recruit buyers, then make sure they can find you and think of you the next time they shop. Penetration is the engine, and how few recruiting brands kept their new households and got them buying more often is the reminder that it has to keep running.

Mental and physical availability

Ehrenberg-Bass defines the two levers. Mental availabilityHow easily buyers think of and notice a brand when they're buying. means buyers easily think of the brand when buying and recognize it quickly. Physical availabilityHow easy a brand is to find and buy, in the right pack, place and price. means they can buy it in a variant, quantity and price that fits what they need. Category entry pointsThe situations and needs that send people into a category, such as a snack for a kid's lunchbox. are the thoughts and motives that send people into a category, and brand assets are the observable features buyers use to recognize a brand6.

Once Upon a Farm uses the same language in its annual report: its marketing aims to drive consumer awareness and mental availability1. Its coolers are physical availability you can see from the end of the aisle. Zevia's sales show the other side: when it lost distribution, it lost sales, however loyal its drinkers were.

The debate: difference or availability?

Not everyone reads the evidence the same way. Kantar says brands that are meaningfully different to more people have five times the market penetration today and an advantage in growing it over the next two years, and its growth rules also stress being present wherever people buy7. Byron Sharp argues that double jeopardy wouldn't exist if brands were highly differentiated and appealed to different segments8.

Both camps agree that penetration drives growth. Kantar leads with meaningful difference, while Ehrenberg-Bass leads with being easy to think of and easy to buy. Ehrenberg-Bass would also point out that bigger brands score higher on almost every attitude, difference included, so the link may run from size to perception as much as the other way. In an interview, knowing both views, and that both point to more buyers, is a strong answer.

In his Brands Lecture for the British Brands Group, Byron Sharp of the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute explains mental and physical availability, why loyalty is real but spread across a repertoireThe handful of brands a shopper rotates among in a category. of brands, and why distinctive brand assetsThe colors, shapes, logos, characters and sounds that let people recognize a brand without reading its name. matter. The start time skips his opening; the earlier part on growth myths is optional.

In an interview

When a case says "grow the brand," split sales into buyers, frequency and spend before you choose a lever. Then check how the brand's loyalty compares with its size. If it's normal for a brand that big, the growth plan is more buyers, through reach and distribution, not a loyalty program.

Self-check: A brand with 8% penetration wants to grow sales 20% next year by "deepening loyalty among our heaviest users." What would you push back with?

Double jeopardy says loyalty mostly follows size, so a 20% jump in buying from the same households would be unusual. The more realistic plan grows penetration from 8% to about 9.6% if frequency and spend hold, through more distribution and broader reach. Then plan the second step, for example tracking how many new buyers come back, since recruiting alone doesn't last.

Check your understanding

4 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. A brand has 12 million buying households who buy 4 times a year at $4 a time. It adds 3 million buying households, and nothing else changes. What are its new annual sales?

    Show answer

    $240 million. 15 million × 4 × $4 = $240 million, up 25% from 12 million × 4 × $4 = $192 million. Penetration growth flows straight through to sales.

  2. Your brand's buyers buy it less often than the category leader's buyers buy the leader. Your CEO calls it a loyalty crisis. Is it?

    Show answer

    Probably not: smaller brands normally have slightly less loyal buyers, so compare with what your size predicts. That's double jeopardy. It's only a crisis if your loyalty is low even for a brand your size.

  3. A brand added a lot of new households this year, but most bought once and didn't come back. What's the best next move?

    Show answer

    Keep recruiting, and make the brand easier to find and remember so light buyers choose it again: distribution, shelf presence, distinctive packaging and broad reach. Recruiting is the engine, but a brand has to stay easy to think of and easy to buy for new buyers to come back. Low repeat after trial can also mean the product or the value disappointed, so check repeat rates and reviews before blaming reach.

  4. Two brands want to grow. One has a clear, meaningful difference but is stocked in a third of stores. The other is in every store but looks like everything else on the shelf. Which plan fits each?

    Show answer

    First, widen distribution so more people can buy it. Second, build distinctive assets and a reason to choose it so it gets noticed and picked. Each is missing a different lever. The first can't be bought; the second isn't noticed or chosen.
