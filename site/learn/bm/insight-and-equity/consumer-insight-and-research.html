Consumer insight and the research toolkit

BrandLesson 4.115 min

Goal: Tell an insight from an observation, and pick the research tool that answers the question you actually have.

Skills: Research & insightWriting & storytelling

Two makeup brands share the same mass-market aisle in U.S. stores. In the quarter to December 2025, e.l.f. Beauty said its namesake e.l.f. Cosmetics brand gained 130 basis pointsOne hundredth of a percentage point. A margin falling from 51.2% to 50.2% is down 100 basis points. of market share1.

Coty, which owns CoverGirl and Rimmel, said a highly competitive U.S. color cosmetics market cut its color cosmetics sales by $47.7 million in its year to June 2026, alongside weaker markets in Europe and the Middle East. It had already put its mass beauty business under strategic review. Its annual report warns that the category has been influenced by new and smaller competitors that are fast to respond to trends and engage customers through digital platforms, data analytics and AI2.

e.l.f. describes the other side of that coin. It calls its approach to innovation community-led, and its best-known products are its "holy grails," which take inspiration from its community and the best prestige products, then sell them at a much lower price. One caution on its headline numbers: e.l.f.'s net salesSales after discounts, returns and trade promotions: the revenue a company reports. rose $323.0 million in fiscal 2026, but $293.5 million of that came from buying the beauty brand rhode3. That's why the share gains for the e.l.f. Cosmetics brand itself are the cleaner signal.

Same aisle, opposite results. Both companies sell more than these brands, so this isn't a controlled test. Still, part of the gap is how fast each one turned what shoppers were saying into products, and that's the job of consumer insight.

Words to know

These are the terms you'll hear whenever a brand team talks about research:

Term What it means A quick example
Insight Why people do what they do, usually a tension between what they want and what they actually do, sharp enough to act on Runners want to refuel right after a run, but most recovery foods need a fridge or a kitchen
Observation What people do, without the why Runners eat granola bars in the parking lot after a race
Data point A single fact or number 42% of buyers are over 45
Truism Something true of every brand in the category People want healthy food
Primary research Research you run yourself, for this question A survey you commission
Syndicated data Data a research firm collects once and sells to many companies Scanner and household panel data
Qualitative research Small samples and open questions, for depth on the why Ten in-home interviews
Quantitative research Large samples and fixed questions, for numbers you can size A survey of 2,000 shoppers
U&A study (usage and attitude) A survey of who uses a category, how, when and why Who buys frozen pizza, and for which nights
Ethnography Watching people in their own homes or stores Watching how a family cooks on a weeknight
Focus group A moderated discussion with a small group Eight runners talking about race day
Concept test Showing people a description of a product before it exists "Would you buy a recovery snack that doesn't need a fridge?"
Social listening Monitoring online conversation about a brand or category Tracking posts asking for a cheaper copy of a prestige product
Household panel Households that record everything they buy, from every store, over time Who bought, how often and what else
Retail scanner data Checkout data from stores: weekly sales, price and promotion Sales of your item at one chain last week

Observation vs. insight

Most of the data a brand team sees tells you what happened, not why. A Harvard Business Review article on "jobs to be done" makes the point: most customer data companies create is built to show correlations, such as which customers look alike or what share prefer one version over another4.

Here's the test to carry with you. An observation says what people do. An insight says why, names the tension underneath, and points to something the brand can do about it. If you can't name the tension, you're still holding an observation.

Try it on e.l.f. The observation is that prestige makeup sells. A sharper read is that shoppers want the prestige result without the prestige price, and they already tell each other which products they wish someone would copy. A brand can build a whole product line on that, which is what a "holy grails" strategy looks like from the outside.

The research toolkit: match the tool to the question

Every research tool answers a different question, and the classic mistake is using a good tool on the wrong one.

OpenStax's marketing textbook lays out the basics. Primary data is gathered for the current project through surveys, observation, experiments or focus groupsA moderated discussion with a small group of people, usually 8 to 12., while syndicated dataStore checkout scans that firms like Circana and NielsenIQ collect from retailers and sell to many brands. is bought by subscription. Qualitative data gives context but can't be reduced to a statistic. Surveys are the usual tool for describing a market, ethnographyResearch that watches people in their own homes, stores or workplaces instead of asking them questions. means watching people in their natural environment, and experiments are the tool for cause and effect. It also warns that a focus group, usually 8 to 12 people, can be taken over by one or two aggressive personalities5. A concept testShowing people a description of a product before it exists to see how much they want it., in the same textbook, shows customers a description of a product or service before it exists5.

The two big purchase datasets look alike but answer different questions. In the NielsenIQ household panelA large sample of households that record what they buy, so you can follow the same buyers over time. data that Chicago Booth's Kilts Center licenses to researchers, panelists scan everything they buy for home use, from any store. The retail scanner data in the same collection is weekly price, volume and store information from the checkout systems of more than 90 retail chains6. The panel tells you who bought and what else they bought. The scanner data tells you what sold where, at what price.

Here's the whole toolkit as a cheat sheet:

Your question The tool What it can't tell you
Who uses the category, how and why? U&A survey Why people really do it: people describe themselves generously
What's really going on in their lives? Ethnography and in-home interviews How common it is
Will people want this idea? Concept test Whether they'll buy again after trying it
Will people like it once they've tried it? Product test, used at home How it sells on a real shelf next to rivals
How does it sell in real stores? Test market in a few cities National results, and rivals can watch and react
What are people saying right now? Social listening Whether the loudest voices are your buyers
Who bought, how often and what else? Household panel Why they bought
What sold where, at what price? Retail scanner data Who bought it
Did this change cause that result? Experiment, such as a store test Anything outside the test's conditions

What people say vs. what they buy

Kantar's Sustainability Sector Index finds that 85% of people worldwide want to make better choices for the planet7. Circana and NYU Stern find U.S. shoppers say they'll pay an average 9% premium for sustainability features, while actual premiums run higher on average. Even so, products marketed on sustainability have reached 25.4% of the U.S. consumer-goods dollar sales the study tracks, up 1.6 points in a year8.

Which number is right? All of them, because they measure different things: what people intend, what they say they'll pay and what they actually bought. Worldpanel by Numerator describes the difference well: its Brand Footprint ranking, built on household purchase data, counts what went into the basket and ignores what people remember, admire or plan to buy9.

The gap between those numbers is where the insight hides. Shoppers clearly care, and many are buying. The question for a brand is how big a premium they'll accept, and that's a question for purchase data and price tests, not an attitude survey.

In this Effie Europe Marketing Factories interview, Federica Pompei, introduced as Procter & Gamble's Europe chief brand officer, explains why her teams still watch consumers in their homes, why social listeningMonitoring online conversation about a brand or category for what people are saying. adds to home visits instead of replacing them, and how a habit spotted in one home became a product.

In an interview

When a case hands you research, sort it out loud: which statements are data, which are observations, and whether any of it is a real insight. Then say which tool you'd use next and why, such as a survey to size a tension you heard in a handful of interviews.

Self-check: Your concept survey said 70% of shoppers would "definitely" or "probably" buy your new protein bar. Six months after launch, sales are a fraction of what that implied. What went wrong in the research plan?

A concept survey measures stated intent, and stated intent runs well ahead of real buying. It also measures interest before anyone has tasted the bar, so it says nothing about repeat. The plan needed a product test after people tried it, and household panel data after launch to see whether triers came back.

Check your understanding

4 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. A brand team wants to know whether a new pack size will lift sales at one retail chain. Which tool gives the most trustworthy answer?

    Show answer

    A store test: put the new pack in some stores and not others, then compare sales. Only an experiment separates cause from coincidence. Everything else tells you what people say or what happened, not what the pack did.

  2. Which of these statements is an insight?

    Show answer

    Commuters want a healthy breakfast, but anything healthy seems to need a plate and a fork, so they grab a pastry on the way to the train instead. It explains why people act, names a tension (healthy vs. portable) and points to something a brand could make.

  3. Your brand's attitude survey says most shoppers feel positive or neutral about the brand, but scanner data shows sales falling sharply. Which do you trust more for what's happening to the business?

    Show answer

    The scanner data, because it shows what people bought; "neutral" can include people who have already switched. Attitudes describe how people feel. Purchase data describes what they did, and for a business question, behavior wins.

  4. You heard a strong tension in 12 in-home interviews. What should happen before the brand plan is built on it?

    Show answer

    Size it with a quantitative survey, then test a concept built on it. Qualitative research is good at finding a tension. It can't tell you how many people share it, and a concept test tells you whether the idea built on it appeals.
