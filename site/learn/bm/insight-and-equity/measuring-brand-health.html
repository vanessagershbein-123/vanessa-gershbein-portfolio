Measuring brand health: tracking, the brand funnel and brand audits

BrandLesson 4.315 min

Goal: Read a brand tracker and a brand funnel, find where a brand is losing people, and know which signals move first.

Skills: Data & chartsResearch & insight

In April 2023, Bud Light landed at the center of a national controversy. YouGov's daily brand trackerThe same survey questions asked over and over, so a brand team can see its awareness, image and consideration change. caught it within weeks: the share of U.S. adults who said they'd talked about Bud Light with family or friends jumped 170% in April, and by April 21 the brand's reputation score had turned negative1.

From the first quarter to the second, the share of people who'd consider Bud Light the next time they bought alcohol fell 2.2 points. Modelo's rose 3 points, from 10.1% to 13.1%, and it jumped from 13th to 6th in YouGov's considerationBeing among the brands someone would think about buying next time they shop the category. ranking, the biggest move of any beer brand1.

Sales followed. In the second quarter of 2023, AB InBev's U.S. sales to retailers fell 14.0%, mainly from Bud Light's volume decline, even as the company said a third-party survey of more than 170,000 consumers found about 80% were favorable or neutral toward the brand2. For the full year, its U.S. sales to retailers fell 11.9%2.

Meanwhile, Constellation Brands said Modelo Especial became the #1 U.S. beer brand by dollar sales in its fiscal year ended February 20243. Modelo was already rising before the controversy, though. Constellation's report for the quarter that ended in May 2023 said it remained the #1 brand share gainer in U.S. beer3. "Remained" means it held that spot before the controversy began.

Same aisle, same quarter: one brand's health collapsed while a rival's rose. Watch how fast each number moved: talk and reputation shifted within days, while consideration and sales both fell over the quarter. Notice, too, that the company's own favorability survey looked far calmer than the tracker or the sales.

The funnel, and why you compare stages

McKinsey's well-known 2009 study of how people shop found the funnel still useful for comparing a brand with its rivals stage by stage and for finding the bottlenecks that stall adoption. It also found that brands in a shopper's initial consideration set can be up to three times more likely to be bought than brands left out of it. The same study argued that real decisions are less linear than a funnel, a more circular journey from consideration to evaluation, purchase and the experience after4.

An illustration with made-up numbers.

Stage Brand A Brand B
Aware 90% 60%
Would consider 45% 36%
Bought in the last year 18% 18%
Buys most often 9% 9%
Conversion Brand A Brand B
Aware to consider 45 ÷ 90 = 50% 36 ÷ 60 = 60%
Consider to bought 18 ÷ 45 = 40% 18 ÷ 36 = 50%
Bought to buys most often 9 ÷ 18 = 50% 9 ÷ 18 = 50%

The formula: conversion rateThe share of visitors or prospects who take an action, such as placing an order. = the share at the next stage ÷ the share at this stage.

Reading it: both brands have the same buyers, but they leak in different places. Brand A is well known but loses people at consideration and again between considering and buying, so its problem is image or relevance: people know it and still don't see it as for them. Brand B converts well once people know it, so its problem is reach: it needs more people to know it exists.

Why it works: raw percentages mix up a brand's size with its health. Conversion removes most of the size effect, though bigger brands still tend to convert a little better, so you can see where each brand loses people.

The common mistake: comparing each stage's raw percentage. The bigger brand looks better at the top stages, which tells you it's bigger, not where it's leaking.

Which signals move first

A sudden shock and a slow slide look different in the data. Beyond Meat's net revenues fell from $343.4 million in 2023 to $326.5 million in 2024 and $275.5 million in 20255. In its 2023 annual report, it listed unfavorable changes in how consumers see the health attributes of plant-based meat among the reasons for weak demand, alongside slower category growth and more competition, and it made improving its health perception a brand priority5.

A shock shows up as a spike in talk and a cliff in reputation within weeks. A slow slide would more likely show up as one image measure, such as whether a product seems healthy, drifting down quarter after quarter while repeat buying fades. A tracker catches the first easily. Catching the second means tracking the right attributes and checking household panelA large sample of households that record what they buy, so you can follow the same buyers over time. data on repeat.

Recovery is uneven too. More than a year after the Bud Light controversy, Molson Coors said Coors Light, Miller Lite and Coors Banquet still held over 80% of their combined share gains in the fourth quarter of 2024, according to Circana6. Getting back into people's consideration is slow, and habits are slower still: once drinkers switch and the new beer works for them, it becomes the default.

Read trackers with care

Two cautions from the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute. First, larger brands have more buyers, and all their loyalty and attitude scores tend to be a bit higher too, from satisfaction to trust7. Second, Byron Sharp argues that brand trackers show attitudes improving mostly after a brand gains share, not before8.

So compare a brand's scores with what its size predicts, and don't read every attitude gain as the cause of a sales gain. Sometimes the buying came first.

Brand audits: the deep checkup

A tracker tells you that something moved. A brand auditA full checkup of where a brand's equity comes from and how well its marketing supports it. asks why, and what the brand is built on. Kevin Lane Keller's "brand report card" in Harvard Business Review set out ten traits the world's strongest brands share and gave managers a way to grade their own brand on each, with constant monitoring as the last of the ten9.

In practice, an audit is the yearly deep dive before planning. It covers where the brand's equity comes from, whether each piece of marketing supports it, how the brand compares with rivals and what the tracker has been signaling.

In this Let's Talk Branding interview, Jenni Romaniuk of the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, author of Better Brand Health, explains what a brand tracker is for and why most trackers ask too much, too often. She also covers which buying situations to track, and why you should check your data follows the double jeopardyThe pattern that smaller brands have fewer buyers, and those buyers are also slightly less loyal. pattern before you trust it.

In an interview

When a case gives you funnel data, turn it into stage-to-stage conversion before you diagnose anything. Then ask which signals moved first, and whether the brand's scores are normal for its size. A drop in consideration with steady sales is a warning to act on now, not after the sales fall.

Self-check: Your brand's awareness is 85% and steady, but consideration fell from 40% to 31% over two quarters while sales are still flat. What's happening, and what would you do?

Aware-to-consider conversion fell from about 47% to about 36%, a leading indicator that sales are likely to follow. Find out why before they do: check which image measures fell in the tracker, look at social listeningMonitoring online conversation about a brand or category for what people are saying. for what people are saying, and run a few interviews. Then fix the reason people stopped considering the brand.

Check your understanding

4 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. Brand X is known by 70% of shoppers and considered by 14%. Brand Y is known by 44% and considered by 22%. Where should each focus?

    Show answer

    X has a consideration problem, since a fifth of the people who know it consider it, while Y has an awareness problem, since half of the people who know it already consider it. X converts 14 ÷ 70 = 20% of aware shoppers. Y converts 22 ÷ 44 = 50%. X needs a reason to be chosen; Y needs to be known by more people.

  2. A controversy hits your brand. Which numbers will move first in a daily tracker?

    Show answer

    Talk about the brand, and its reputation score. Word of mouth and reputation move within days. Consideration and weekly store sales follow, and quarterly sales reports come last.

  3. Your brand's satisfaction score rose 4 points last year, and your share rose too. Your CEO says the satisfaction gain caused the share gain. What's your caution?

    Show answer

    Attitudes often follow buying: as more people buy a brand, its scores rise, so the cause may run the other way. Bigger brands score a bit higher on almost every attitude measure, so a rising score is partly a sign of growth, not proof of what caused it.

  4. A plant-based snack's sales slide a little every year with no single bad event. What should the brand team track most closely?

    Show answer

    Specific image measures, such as whether people see it as healthy, plus panel data on repeat buying. A slow slide comes from one belief eroding and buyers quietly not coming back. The tracker has to ask about that belief, and the panel shows who stopped repeating.
