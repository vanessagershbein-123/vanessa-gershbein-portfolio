Routes to market and channels: why KDP runs its own trucks and Kenvue doesn't

BrandLesson 3.316 min

Goal: Explain why some brands deliver to every store themselves while others ship to retailers' warehouses, and fit the pack and price to the channel where the shopper buys.

Skills: Positioning & strategyPricing & promotion

Keurig Dr Pepper runs a U.S. fleet of about 8,100 vehicles. It sells its drinks by shipping to retailers' warehouses and through its own direct store deliveryDirect store delivery: the brand's own trucks, or distributors working for it, deliver to each store and often stock the shelves, skipping the retailer's warehouse. network, or DSD, which takes drinks straight to stores. KDP says DSD keeps its products available, cuts transport costs and gives it more control over the timing of new launches1. In April 2026, it bought JDE Peet's, the owner of Peet's Coffee, and it plans to put its coffee business and JDE Peet's together in one of two separate public companies2.

Peet's had just gone the other way. In February 2026, it said it was moving its U.S. commercial deliveries off DSD to what it called "an asset-light direct central distribution model," with the rest of the switch due by June3.

Kenvue, the Zyrtec maker that Kimberly-Clark bought in November 20264, describes no delivery fleet at all in its last annual report. It says most of its distribution centers are run by third-party operators, and that it delivers to distributors, customers and consumers5.

One owner, two routes: its own trucks for many of its drinks, central distribution for Peet's coffee. A drug brand with no trucks at all. The reason isn't the size of the company. It's the product, and the stores it sells through.

Words to know

What DSD buys, and what it costs

DSD is more than a truck. Flowers Foods, which delivers fresh bread through independent distributors, lists their jobs: ordering products, selling to stores, stocking shelves, keeping up special displays, looking after customer relationships, keeping enough inventory on hand and removing unsold goods6. Utz, the snack maker, says its DSD system helps it win distribution and shelf space, run in-store merchandising, keep products fresh and test new items quickly in stores7.

That service has a price. Flowers paid its independent distributors fees equal to 11.8% of its total sales in its 2025 fiscal year6. Some of its products go through warehouses instead, so the fees take an even bigger bite out of each DSD sale.

When warehouse delivery wins

Hostess built its business the other way. In its 2022 annual report, it said its direct-to-warehouseThe brand ships to the retailer's warehouses, and the retailer moves the product to its own stores. model, which delivers to customers' warehouses by common carrier, was made possible by technology that extends its products' shelf life. It also said DSD can be inefficient for convenience, drug and dollar stores, where each stop drops off a small amount8.

Big retailers often prefer it too. Utz says some of its national mass, club and grocery customers prefer warehouse delivery because they have the scale to run distribution and restocking for their own stores7.

Put it together and a pattern appears. These are tendencies, not laws:

DSD tends to pay when... Warehouse delivery tends to pay when...
The product goes stale fast and unsold stock has to be pulled The product keeps for weeks or months
It's heavy or bulky for what it's worth, like cases of soda It's small and valuable for its size, like allergy tablets
It sells fast and needs coolers, displays and frequent restocking The shelf stays full between restocks
You need a presence in many small stores Most sales go through big chains with their own warehouses
You launch and test new items often Launches are rarer

Now run the three products through it. Soft drinks are heavy, sell fast and live in coolers and displays, so DSD earns its cost. Coffee and coffee pods keep for months and are worth a lot per case, so a retailer's warehouse can handle them cheaply, and Peet's gave "asset-light" as its reason. Zyrtec tablets are tiny, valuable and long-lasting, and they sell through chains that run their own distribution centers, so a fleet would add little. KDP's filings don't say how its own coffee reaches stores, so treat the coffee half of the answer as a reasoned guess.

Borrowing someone else's trucks

You don't have to own DSD to use it. Celsius moved its Alani Nu energy drinks into PepsiCo's DSD network and named wider distribution through that network as a driver of Alani Nu's growth. The cost showed up too. A bigger share of DSD sales brought higher trade spending and billbacks that cut reported revenue, and Celsius's third-quarter gross marginGross profit as a share of net sales: what's left after the cost of making and shipping the product. fell to 46.8% from 51.3%, which it put down to costlier aluminum and fuel as well as more DSD sales9.

A small, fast-growing brand gains reach it couldn't build alone, and pays for it in margin. Whether that's worth it depends on how much faster the brand grows with the extra stores.

Channels: same product, different shopper jobs

Route to marketHow a product gets from the factory to the shelf, and who sells it to the store. is how the product gets to the store. Channel is which kind of store, or app, the shopper buys from, and each one asks something different of your pack and price.

Club. Costco carries fewer than 4,000 active items per warehouse, far fewer than other broadline retailers. It says its Kirkland Signature products are priced generally lower than national brands and generally earn higher margins10.

On Costco.com in late November 2026, a 365-tablet bottle of Kirkland Signature Aller-Tec, labeled "Compare to Zyrtec® Active Ingredient," listed at $17.99, or $0.05 a tablet, and Costco noted that prices may vary11. A 120-tablet Zyrtec pack, sold as two bottles, listed at $44.99, or $0.38 a tablet11. Here's how the math works: $0.38 ÷ $0.05 means the brand costs more than seven times as much per tablet.

Why carry both? The national brand brings in members who want it by name and sets a reference price. The store brandProducts sold under a retailer's own brand name, like Kirkland Signature at Costco. earns Costco a better margin and gives value seekers a reason to switch. For Zyrtec, club is a channel where its cheapest substitute sits in the same aisle, so its club pack has to give members a reason to pay more. That store brand is a serious rival. A Numerator study summarized by Kantar found that 98% of U.S. households buy private label in health and beauty, and that Costco's Kirkland and H-E-B's Hill Country Fare top the rankings for trust12.

Drugstores and mass. Kantar's ShopperScape survey found the U.S. drugstore shopper base fell 10% in 2024, while Walmart became the top retailer for health and wellness and Amazon climbed into a tie for third. Kantar's advice to health brands is to follow those shoppers with the right pack sizes and channel-specific exclusives for Walmart and Amazon13.

Behind the counter. Some channel choices aren't yours to make. By law, Zyrtec-D and other products containing pseudoephedrine must be sold behind the counter and through online retailers that meet certain requirements14.

Online. Instacart works with more than 2,200 retail banners and reaches over 98% of households in North America15. Shoppers buy from their own stores through the app, so a brand gets onto Instacart by getting into the retailers that use it, then pays for ads to stand out.

Direct to consumerDirect to consumer: a brand selling straight to shoppers through its own website, app, subscription or stores.. Peet's sells coffee, tea, K-Cups and espresso capsules by subscription16, and its coffee is also sold in coffee shops, grocery stores, airports, universities, hospitals and offices16. A coffee brand with repeat buyers and its own cafés can build a direct relationship with shoppers. An allergy brand gated by pharmacy rules and retailer shelves mostly can't, so its levers are retailer partnerships, retail mediaAds a retailer sells on its website, app, stores and partner sites, targeted with its shopper data. and the right pack in each channel.

Channel The shopper's job What it asks of your pack and price
Grocery The weekly shop Core sizes at your anchor price, plus a promotion calendar
Mass One-stop shopping at low prices Sharp everyday prices, often a value pack
Club Stocking up One big pack, a low price per unit and a reason to beat the store brand beside you
Drug Fixing a problem today Smaller packs, and pharmacy-only items
Amazon Searching, reordering, subscribing Packs built for shipping and subscriptions, plus reviews and search ads
Delivery apps Need it within the hour Small packs at a high price per unit
DTC Fans who want more of you Bundles and subscriptions you price yourself

Retailers set their own shelf prices, so a brand steers through the pack, what it charges each retailer and its suggested price. The common mistake is offering the same pack everywhere and watching its shelf price drift far apart from channel to channel. Shoppers and retail buyers compare price per unit across channels, so design different packs on purpose and keep the per-unit gaps steady.

This Wall Street Journal case study explains how Costco built Kirkland Signature and why a Kirkland launch matters so much to a national brand in a store that carries so few items. Watch it with the Zyrtec example in mind.

In an interview

When a case asks whether to enter club, Amazon or a delivery app, answer in four parts: the shopper's job in that channel, the pack and its price per unit, what it does to your prices in other channels, and how the product will physically get there.

Self-check: A new refrigerated cold-brew brand with a three-week shelf life sells mostly in convenience stores and needs space in their coolers. Should it build its own DSD fleet, ship to retailers' warehouses or do something else?

Something else: borrow DSD. Short shelf life, cooler space and many small stores all point to DSD, but a young brand can't afford a fleet. Sign with a beverage distributor that already visits those stores, as Celsius did with PepsiCo, and budget for the trade spending and lower margin that come with it.

Check your understanding

4 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. A bakery makes fresh bread that lasts a few days and shelf-stable snack cakes that last for weeks. Which route fits each?

    Show answer

    DSD for the bread, so drivers can restock often and pull stale loaves, and warehouse delivery for the snack cakes. Freshness and pulling unsold product favor DSD. A long shelf life lets a retailer's warehouse do the job for less.

  2. A club chain wants your allergy tablets in a 150-count pack at $29.99. Your pharmacy 40-count sells for $21.99. What's your first worry?

    Show answer

    At about 20 cents a tablet against about 55 cents in pharmacy, heavy pharmacy buyers may switch, and the club's store brand sits right beside you. Compare price per unit across channels first. A huge per-unit gap invites your best customers to move.

  3. A snack brand's sales jump after it moves into a partner's DSD network, but its gross margin falls. What's the most likely reason?

    Show answer

    DSD brings more trade spending and distributor costs, so each sale earns less even as reach grows. DSD buys reach and shelf service, and you pay for it through billbacks, fees and trade spending.

  4. Your coffee brand sells through grocery, its own cafés and an online subscription. A grocery buyer complains that your subscription price undercuts the shelf. What's the best fix?

    Show answer

    Make the subscription offer different, such as bigger bags or bundles, so shoppers can't compare like for like. Different packs in different channels limit direct price comparisons and keep each partner's offer distinct.
