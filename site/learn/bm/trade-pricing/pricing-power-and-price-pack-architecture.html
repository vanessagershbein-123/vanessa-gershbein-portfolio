Pricing power, elasticity and price-pack architecture

BrandLesson 3.215 min

Goal: Predict what a price increase does to units and profit, and know when a new pack size beats a higher price.

Skills: Pricing & promotionBusiness math

In 2023, Coca-Cola and Kellanova, the maker of Pringles and Cheez-It, both pushed prices up in North America. Coca-Cola's North American price and mix added 8 points of revenue growth, and its volume fell 1%1. Kellanova's North American pricing and mix rose 10.1%, its volume fell 6.1%, and it pointed to rising price elasticityHow much the quantity sold changes when price changes. An elasticity of −1.5 means a 10% price rise cuts units about 15%.2.

Similar price moves, very different reactions. Coca-Cola's shoppers barely changed what they bought. Kellanova's bought noticeably less. The number that describes that difference is price elasticity, and every pricing decision turns on it.

Treat the comparison as a puzzle, not a measurement. Price/mixThe part of a sales change that comes from price changes and from shifts toward pricier or cheaper items. includes selling more of a company's pricier packs and channels, not just higher list prices, and volume moves for other reasons too, such as retailers cutting inventory or a brand losing shelf space.

The elasticity math

OpenStax defines demand elasticity as the percent change in quantity demanded divided by the percent change in price, and notes that when buyers can easily switch to a different product, demand is more elastic3.

An illustration with made-up numbers.

Your coffee sells 100,000 bags a year. Retailers pay you $10 a bag, and you earn $3 a bag after costs. You raise your price 10%, to $11, and your elasticity is about −1.5.

Line Before After
Price per bag $10 $11
Bags sold 100,000 85,000 (10% × −1.5 = −15%)
Revenue $1,000,000 $935,000
Earned per bag $3 $4
Profit $300,000 $340,000

The formula: units after a price change ≈ units before × (1 + elasticity × price change). Here, 100,000 × (1 − 1.5 × 0.10) = 85,000.

Why profit rose while revenue fell: the extra $1 a bag goes straight to profit, so you earn a third more on every bag you still sell. You lost 15% of your bags, less than the third you gained on each one.

The common mistake: judging a price change by revenue or units. Revenue fell 6.5% here, and the move still paid. Elasticity isn't fixed, either. It tends to rise when competitors hold their prices, when shoppers are squeezed and when a cheaper substitute sits right beside you.

Brand elasticity vs. category elasticity

Your brand's elasticity isn't the category's. In a price war Kantar analyzed for a consumer-goods leader in Mexico, category demand was inelastic, at −0.8, while the average brand's demand was elastic, at −1.37. Cutting prices mostly turned into a game of winning and losing share, which did little for the category or for profits4.

The reason is switching. Your brand elasticity is high because shoppers move between brands when one gets cheaper. When a rival matches your cut, those shoppers move back, and you're left with the category's much smaller response and a lower margin on everything you sell.

Where pricing power comes from

Here's the same puzzle inside one company. In the quarter ended October 31, 2025, higher net prices added 27 percentage points to J.M. Smucker's U.S. coffee sales, while lower volume and mix took away 6 points, mainly from Folgers and Dunkin'. Café Bustelo grew anyway5.

Smucker doesn't explain why Café Bustelo held up, so you'd ask the questions that predict pricing powerThe ability to raise your price and lose fewer buyers than competitors would.. Do its buyers have close substitutes? Is it still adding new households? Did its price gap to rivals stay small after the increase?

In general, pricing power comes from a few places:

  • Few close substitutes, in shoppers' eyes, not just on the shelf.
  • Being the brand people want, not just one they know, so a higher price feels worth it.
  • Small dollar stakes, where a 10% increase is a few cents and habit carries the purchase.

Price-pack architecture: change the pack, not just the price

A list-price increase is the most visible move you can make. McKinsey notes that a 10% increase in the suggested retail price hits shoppers harder than raising net price the same amount through smarter promotions, more premium items or better trade terms with retailers, and that pack-size changes are also noticed somewhat less6.

That's where price-pack architectureThe ladder of pack sizes and prices a brand offers, designed so each kind of shopper finds a size and price that fits. comes in: a ladder of sizes and prices designed so each kind of shopper finds a rung that fits. In North America, Coca-Cola's mini-can volume grew high single digits in early 2026 after it launched single-serve mini-cans in convenience stores7.

Retailers build ladders too. Walmart's annual report lists focusing on opening price pointsThe cheapest option in a category at a given store. in some food categories, and on private labelProducts sold under a retailer's own brand name, like Kirkland Signature at Costco. brands and smaller pack sizes, among the ways it keeps prices low8. The store brand often holds the bottom rung, so your packs have to earn their place against it.

An illustration with made-up numbers.

Pack Shelf price Ounces Price per ounce Who it's for
Single-serve bag $1.29 1.5 $0.86 Convenience store, impulse
Family bag $4.99 9 $0.55 Weekly grocery shop
Party size $6.99 15 $0.47 Bigger households, events
Club multipack $15.99 36 $0.44 Stocking up at a warehouse club

The rule: the bigger the pack, the lower the price per ounce, with steady steps between rungs. The small pack hits a low price point, so shoppers can buy without spending much. The big packs reward buying more.

The common mistake: a broken rung, where a bigger pack costs more per ounce than a smaller one, or two packs so close that one just steals sales from the other.

Shrinkflation: a price increase in disguise

Shrinking the pack is the quiet version of a price rise. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics says manufacturers change sizes because consumers are more sensitive to a change in price than a change in size. It counts a smaller pack at the same price as a price increase: in its example, a 64-ounce ice cream at $5.99 that becomes 60 ounces at the same $5.99 is a 6.7% increase per ounce9.

Research backs up the instinct. GAO summarized one study that found a 1% increase in an item's price cut sales by 1.95% on average, while a 1% cut in size slightly raised the number of packages sold. Overall, GAO found downsizing explained less than a tenth of a percentage point of the 34.5% rise in U.S. consumer prices from 2019 to 2024, though far more in some categories, such as paper towels and cereal10.

It can still backfire. Kantar reports that after Toblerone's shrinkflationShrinking a pack while keeping its price, which raises the price per unit. move in November 2016, its sales had fallen more than 7% year over year by December 2017. The change was reversed in July 2018, and by December 2018 sales were growing again11. Kantar doesn't separate out other causes, but the timing is hard to ignore. When costs force your hand, a new pack at a new price point lets shoppers choose to pay less for less, instead of discovering it later.

In this Europanel interview, UNSW Sydney marketing professor Harald van Heerde explains what a price elasticity is, why inflation makes shoppers more price-sensitive, and why launching a new, smaller pack is safer than quietly shrinking the one shoppers know.

In an interview

When a case asks whether to raise the price, answer in three moves. First, how much volume can you lose before the increase hurts profit? Second, what's your likely elasticity, with and without competitors following? Third, is there a pack or price-point move that gets the same money with less risk?

Self-check: Your crackers have an elasticity of about −2 because store brands sit right beside them. Your team wants to cut the price 10% to win volume. What's the catch?

Units might rise about 20%, so revenue rises about 8% (0.9 × 1.2 = 1.08). You now earn less on every box, though, including the ones you'd have sold anyway. If competitors and the store brand match your cut, your response shrinks toward the category's, and you keep the lower price without the volume. Work out profit, not revenue, and plan for the response.

Check your understanding

4 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. Your yogurt's elasticity is about −0.5, and you raise the price 6%. Roughly what happens to units and to revenue?

    Show answer

    Units fall about 3% and revenue rises about 3%. Units change by about −0.5 × 6% = −3%. Revenue ≈ 1.06 × 0.97 ≈ 1.03, so about 3% more. When demand is inelastic, a price rise lifts revenue.

  2. A rival matches every price cut you make within two weeks. Your brand's elasticity is −2.2 when you move alone, and the category's is −0.6. What should you expect from a deep price cut?

    Show answer

    Little extra volume once the rival matches, and a lower margin on everything you sell. Brand elasticity measures switching between brands. When rivals match, the switching cancels out, and you're left with the category's much smaller response.

  3. Costs rise and you need more money per ounce. Shoppers in your category watch shelf prices closely but rarely check price per ounce. Which move carries the most long-term risk to trust?

    Show answer

    Quietly shrinking the pack while keeping the price. Shrinking the pack works at first because shoppers don't notice, but when they do, they feel tricked. A new pack at a new price lets them choose.

  4. Your 12-ounce box costs $3.60 and your 20-ounce box costs $6.40. What's wrong with this ladder?

    Show answer

    The bigger box costs more per ounce (32 cents vs. 30), so value shoppers have no reason to trade up. Price per ounce should fall as packs get bigger. Here the bigger box breaks the ladder.
