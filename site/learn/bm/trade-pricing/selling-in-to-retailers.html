Selling in to retailers: key accounts and the sell story

BrandLesson 3.416 min

Goal: Know what a retail buyer is judged on, and build a sell story that wins shelf space by growing the buyer's category, not just your brand.

Skills: Writing & storytellingPositioning & strategy

Two retailers rearranged a shelf and got opposite results. At Carrefour, Colgate, the chain's oral-care category captainA supplier, often the category leader, that a retailer asks to advise on the whole category., recommended putting toothbrushes above toothpaste, and Carrefour's oral-care sales rose 6% to 16%, according to a 2009 Vanderbilt University study reported by ISB1.

At Kroger, the retailer moved big-pack cereal to eye level to drive big-pack sales. When Kellogg helped Kroger study the results, big packs were growing but the total cereal category was shrinking, partly because the new households it drew spent less than the households it lost2.

One change grew the whole aisle. The other grew the part that moved and shrank the rest. A retail buyer runs the whole aisle, so that's the test every pitch you make has to pass.

Who you're selling to

The buyer is judged on the category, not on your brand. Kantar's 2026 PoweRanking study, based on interviews with hundreds of retailers and manufacturers, found that Delivering Growth & Profitability has been retailers' top measure of their suppliers for several years. The suppliers Kantar calls Total Value Creators are now also judged on growth across the retailer's whole business, from stores and online to retail mediaAds a retailer sells on its website, app, stores and partner sites, targeted with its shopper data. and loyalty. Category sales, in Kantar's words, are "the ante to play"3.

Big retailers also hold the power. Walmart made up about 29% of WK Kellogg Co's net salesSales after discounts, returns and trade promotions: the revenue a company reports. in 2024 and its top five customers about 52%, and the cereal maker warned that large retailers use their size to push for lower prices and more promotions paid for by suppliers4.

That's why brands give big customers their own teams. Reynolds Consumer Products, the maker of Hefty and Reynolds Wrap, gives each of its top customers a dedicated support team covering category managementRunning a product category as its own business, deciding assortment, price, promotion and shelf space together., production planning, demand planning and transportation planning, plus its own customer service representatives5.

Category captains: helpful, and not neutral

Retailers often ask one supplier, called a category captain, to advise on the whole category. ISB describes the captain as generally the retailer's best-performing supplier in that category, one that shares consumer data and insight to grow it, and notes the catch: captains may naturally favor their own brands1. U.S. Federal Trade Commission staff flagged category captains as a competition issue in 2001, recommending that any inquiries into them focus on cases that could shut out rivals or involve collusion6.

Where a supplier stands shapes its advice. Reynolds says it holds category-captain-level advisory roles with retailers, that its retail partners generally measure success by category growth, and that store brandsProducts sold under a retailer's own brand name, like Kirkland Signature at Costco. made up 39% of its 2025 revenue, excluding sales to other businesses5. A supplier that profits whichever label the shopper picks can push for category growth with less conflict than a brand-only supplier, whose rival on the shelf is often the retailer's own brand.

The shelf has to be re-earned

Winning a slot isn't the end. NIQ reports that many major retailers now refresh their assortments on 12-week cycles, while 67% of marketers wait 12 to 15 weeks to judge a launch, missing the retailer's decision window7.

WK Kellogg warned that even products that win shelf space can be pulled if they miss the sales expectations retailers set4. The number that decides it is velocityHow fast you sell where you're stocked: sales per point of distribution, per store or per TDP., sales per store or per point of distribution, which Circana calls a core measure for assortment decisions and resets8.

What makes a buyer say yes

A peer-reviewed study of 392 new-product decisions by buyers at a Dutch food retailer found that buyers were more likely to take on a new item when they expected it to grow the category, and when its relative gross marginGross profit as a share of net sales: what's left after the cost of making and shipping the product. was higher9.

Put that together with what buyers are judged on, and a sell storyA brand's pitch to a retail buyer showing how its product will grow the buyer's category. has one job: show the buyer how your item makes their category bigger and more profitable, in their numbers. A strong one fits on a page and follows this order:

  1. Headline: what the buyer gains, in the buyer's terms.
  2. The gap: where their category trails the market or misses a trend.
  3. Proof: how fast your item sells, the new households it brings and how much of its volume is new to the category.
  4. The math: what your item adds compared with what it replaces.
  5. The ask: which slots, in which reset, on what terms.
  6. Your support: sampling, retail media, a launch promotion.
  7. The risk: the objection you expect, and your answer.

The math every buyer redoes

An illustration with made-up numbers.

You want one slot in a chain's salsa set for a new roasted-tomato salsa. At similar chains, it sells $150 per store per week. The item it would replace sells $60. The chain has 250 stores and earns a 30% margin on the aisle.

Line Math Result
Extra sales per store per week $150 − $60 $90
Extra sales per year $90 × 250 stores × 52 weeks $1,170,000
Extra margin for the chain $1,170,000 × 30% $351,000

The formula: extra sales = (your item's sales − the replaced item's sales) per store per week × stores × 52.

Why it works this way: the shelf is full. The buyer doesn't gain your item's total sales, just the difference between your item and what it pushes out.

The common mistake: pitching your item's total, $150 × 250 × 52 = $1.95 million, as if the shelf space were free. The buyer will redo your math, and you lose credibility when their number is 40% smaller than yours.

One more adjustment: if some of your item's sales would come out of other items in the aisle, not just the one it replaces, subtract those too. The buyer will ask how incremental your item really is.

Retail media: part of the price of the conversation

Retailers increasingly sell advertising, and it's becoming part of what a key accountOne of a brand's biggest retail customers, usually served by its own dedicated team. expects. In the year to January 2026, Walmart's global advertising business grew 46% to nearly $6.4 billion, a figure that includes VIZIO, the TV maker it bought. Walmart Connect in the U.S. grew 41% in the fourth quarter, not counting VIZIO10.

In 2025, Instacart's advertising and other revenue grew 11% to $1,065 million, the same rate as the total value of orders on its platform, so ads held steady at about 2.9% of that total11.

Why the gap? One likely reason shows up in Kroger's annual report, which describes the asset a retailer sells. Kroger serves about 63 million households a year, over 95% of its transactions are tied to a loyalty card, and it can link ad impressions directly to household purchases online and in store12. A retailer that owns the stores, the loyalty data and the shelf can sell ads in its app, on its site and in its aisles, and show suppliers what those ads sold. A marketplace sees its own orders. Keep the caveats in mind: Walmart's figure includes an acquisition, and the two companies' years end a month apart.

For the brand, retail media is often less a choice than a cost of doing business. Kenvue's annual report says big customers have used their bargaining strength to demand more investment in data, retail media, search, trade discounts and logistics services13. Spend it with your eyes open: in a McKinsey survey of advertisers, just 3% said commerce media networks measure incrementality very accurately14.

Joint business plans

With the biggest accounts, the sell story grows into a joint business planJoint business plan: a yearly plan a brand and a retailer build together, with shared goals for sales, promotions, shelf and media.: a yearly plan for growth, promotions, shelf and media, built with the retailer. Deloitte's study of retailers and consumer-goods makers found joint business planning was rated the most impactful area of collaboration, yet one of the least transformational in practice, often turning into an annual negotiation instead of a way to keep growing15.

In this Category Management Association webinar, reposted by Conversations On Retail, Kristine Joji, a former merchandising VP at Walmart, and Capri Brixey, a former sales and customer leader at Coca-Cola, walk through the mistakes suppliers make in joint business planning, from arriving with only their own agenda to building a story on different data from the retailer's. Both now work at an analytics company, Insite AI, which the host promotes briefly mid-session.

In an interview

If you're asked how you'd get a product into a retailer, start with the buyer's scorecard: category growth, margin and shopper trips. Then walk the sell story: the gap, your proof, the math against what you'd replace, the ask and your support. Close with the risk the buyer will raise and how you'd answer it.

Self-check: Your new item would replace one of your own slower items, not a competitor's. How does that change your sell story?

The buyer's math barely changes: they still gain the difference between the new item and the one it replaces. Yours does: you lose the old item's sales, so your own gain is smaller than the buyer's. Say so plainly, and show why the swap still grows the category, for example by bringing in shoppers the old item didn't reach.

Check your understanding

4 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. A buyer offers you one slot. Your item should sell $95 per store per week, the item it replaces sells $35, and the chain has 600 stores. What yearly sales gain does the buyer care about?

    Show answer

    About $1.87 million. ($95 − $35) × 600 × 52 = $1,872,000. The buyer gains the difference, not your item's total of about $2.96 million.

  2. Your shelf change grew your segment by double digits at a chain, but the chain's total category fell. How will the buyer likely see it?

    Show answer

    As a failure, because the buyer is judged on the whole category, not on your segment. A segment can grow by pulling sales from the rest of the aisle or by losing households that bought other sizes. The buyer's scorecard is the category total.

  3. A buyer asks you to spend more on the chain's retail media network as part of next year's plan. What's the strongest response?

    Show answer

    Agree to a test with a clear measure of incremental sales, using the retailer's loyalty data, and scale it if it works. Retail media can work, but advertisers doubt the networks' incrementality numbers. Tie the spend to a measured test.

  4. Two suppliers pitch the same buyer. One makes only branded products. The other makes the branded leader and the retailer's store brand. Why might the buyer weigh the second one's category advice differently?

    Show answer

    It earns money whichever label wins, so its advice is less tilted toward its own brand, though it can still favor what it makes. A supplier's position shapes its advice. Making both labels reduces one conflict but doesn't remove the captain's natural pull toward its own products.
