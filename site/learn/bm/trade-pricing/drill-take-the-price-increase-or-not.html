Take the price increase or not?

BrandDrill 3.215 minIntermediate

Skills: Pricing & promotionBusiness math

The prompt

Hypothetical scenario. The brand and all numbers are made up to show the work.

The facts. You manage Crunchwell crackers, an 8-ounce box:

  • Today: retailers pay you $3.00 a box, and it costs you $1.80 to make and ship, so you earn $1.20 a box. You sell 1,000,000 boxes a year.
  • The problem: wheat and packaging costs are rising $0.20 a box, which cuts your earnings to $1.00 a box.
  • Option A, raise the price 8%, to $3.24 a box. Your past price increases suggest an elasticityHow much the quantity sold changes when price changes. An elasticity of −1.5 means a 10% price rise cuts units about 15%. of about −1.2 if your main competitor raises its price too, and about −2.5 if it doesn't.
  • Option B, hold the price and absorb the cost.
  • Option C, shrink the box to 7 ounces at the same $3.00. After the cost increase, the smaller box costs you $1.75 to make and ship. You expect about 3% fewer boxes sold, and some shoppers will notice.

Your task: Recommend one option, with the numbers behind it, and say what would change your mind.

Your earlier answer

  1. Hint 1 of 3 Start with what holding the price costs you: work out profit per box before and after the cost increase.
  2. Hint 2 of 3 For the price increase, work out how many boxes you could lose before you'd be worse off than holding the price. Then compare that with what elasticity predicts.
  3. Hint 3 of 3 The smaller box earns more per box. Ask what shoppers and retailers will think, and what you'll do the next time costs rise.

The three options

Option Profit per box Boxes Profit
Before the cost increase $1.20 1,000,000 $1.20M
B. Hold the price $1.00 1,000,000 $1.00M
A. Raise 8%, competitor follows (−1.2) $1.24 904,000 about $1.12M
A. Raise 8%, competitor holds (−2.5) $1.24 800,000 about $0.99M
C. Smaller box $1.25 970,000 about $1.21M

The formulas, and why they work

  • Units after a price change ≈ units before × (1 + elasticity × price change). With −1.2 and +8%: 1,000,000 × (1 − 0.096) = about 904,000 boxes.
  • Volume you can lose before a price increase hurts = the price increase ÷ (old profit per box + the price increase). Here: $0.24 ÷ ($1.00 + $0.24) ≈ 19%.
  • Why: you break even when (1 − lost share) × new profit per box = old profit per box. Solve for the lost share and you get the increase divided by the new profit per box.
  • Reading it: with the competitor following, you lose about 10% of boxes, well inside the 19% cushion, so the increase pays. If the competitor holds and you lose 20%, you're slightly worse off than holding the price.

A strong recommendation

"Take the 8% increase, but only with a plan for the competitor. If they follow, we earn about $120,000 more than holding the price. If they don't, we land about where holding leaves us, so I'd watch weekly share for six to eight weeks and be ready to fund a promotion if our price gap widens too far. I wouldn't shrink the box: it looks best on paper, but it uses up shoppers' trust, retailers may push back, and it doesn't solve the next cost increase."

Why the smaller box is a trap, even when it wins on paper

  • It's a bigger price increase than it looks. Going from 8 ounces to 7 at the same price raises the price per ounce by about 14.3% (8 ÷ 7 ≈ 1.143), almost twice Option A's 8%. It wins on paper only because you assumed just 3% fewer boxes sold.
  • Shoppers notice, and the story of a smaller box at the same price can do more damage than an honest price increase.
  • It only works once. Next year you face the same problem with a box that's already smaller.
  • There's a better use of pack size: add a new size at a new price point, such as a family box, so shoppers choose what they pay instead of discovering it later.

Practice twist

The competitor raises its price 5%, not 8%, so your price gap widens a little. You estimate your elasticity at −1.8 in that case. What happens?

Answer: units fall about 14.4% (1.8 × 8%), to about 856,000 boxes, for profit of about $1.06M. That's still above holding the price at $1.00M, because 14% is inside the 19% cushion, but the margin of safety is thin.
