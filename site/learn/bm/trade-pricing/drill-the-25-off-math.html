The 25%-off math

BrandDrill 3.112 minIntermediate

Skills: Business mathPricing & promotion

The prompt

Hypothetical scenario. The brand and all numbers are made up to show the work.

The facts. You manage Sunny Grove orange juice. A grocery chain's buyer offers a promotion:

  • Normal economics: the 52-ounce carton sells for $4.00 on shelf. The chain pays you $3.00, and it costs you $1.80 to make and ship, so you earn $1.20 a carton.
  • The deal: 25% off for two weeks, so the shelf price drops to $3.00. You fund $0.75 of every carton sold on deal through a scan-downA trade deal the brand pays only on units that scan at the register during the promotion. payment, and the chain gives up the other $0.25 from its own margin.
  • A display: the chain also asks for a $15,000 fee to put Sunny Grove on an end-of-aisle display during the deal.
  • Normal sales at this chain are 10,000 cartons a week.
  • Past events like this one tripled sales during the deal weeks, to 30,000 a week, then sales dipped 15% for the two weeks after.

Your task: Does this event make money? What lift would it need to break even? What would you negotiate before saying yes?

Your earlier answer

  1. Hint 1 of 3 Compare two worlds over the same weeks: one with the promotion and one without it.
  2. Hint 2 of 3 Work out what you earn per carton on deal. Every carton sold on deal earns less, including the 10,000 a week you'd have sold anyway.
  3. Hint 3 of 3 To find breakeven, add up everything the event has to cover (what you'd have earned anyway, the display fee and the dip), then divide by what you earn per carton on deal.

The event, line by line

Line Math Result
Earned per carton on deal $1.20 − $0.75 $0.45
With the promotion, two weeks 60,000 × $0.45 $27,000
Without it, the same two weeks 20,000 × $1.20 −$24,000 (what you give up)
Display fee −$15,000
The dip after, two weeks 2 × 1,500 fewer cartons × $1.20 −$3,600
Net effect of the event −$15,600

Tripling sales sounds like a hit. It still loses $15,600.

The general formula, and why it works

  • Breakeven units on deal = (what you'd have earned without the deal + fixed costs + the dip) ÷ what you earn per unit on deal.
  • Here: ($24,000 + $15,000 + $3,600) ÷ $0.45 ≈ 94,700 cartons over two weeks, or about 47,300 a week. That's about 4.7 times normal sales, against the 3 times you expect.
  • Without the fee and the dip, the bare breakeven liftHow much sales must rise during a promotion just to earn the same profit as not running it. is just the ratio of the two margins: $1.20 ÷ $0.45 ≈ 2.7 times normal sales.
  • Why: on deal, every carton earns less, including the 10,000 a week shoppers would have bought at full price. Those cartons are subsidized. The extra cartons have to earn back that lost margin before they earn anything else, and then pay the fees too.
  • The common mistake: judging the event on the lift (+200%) or on total sales during the deal, instead of on what you earn compared with doing nothing.

What you'd negotiate

  1. Drop or cut the display fee, or ask for it to be tied to a second event. On its own, the fee is most of the loss.
  2. Ask for a shallower discount. At 15% off, you might give up less per carton than the extra cartons earn.
  3. Pick a better week, such as one when the category is busy anyway, or when a competitor isn't promoting.
  4. Ask for the chain's data on how many deal buyers are new to the brand. A promotion that brings in new households can be worth more than its two weeks of numbers.

What interviewers push on

  • "But the buyer says the display will drive the lift." Then ask for the lift with and without a display from past events, and pay only for the difference it makes.
  • "Isn't a loss fine if it builds the brand?" Sometimes, if it recruits new buyers who come back. Say what you'd measure to prove it, such as the repeat rateThe share of buyers who come back to buy again. of new buyers from the event.

Practice twist

The chain waives the display fee in exchange for a second event later in the year. Does this event make money now?

Answer: −$15,600 + $15,000 = −$600, so it's about breakeven. You'd need about 61,300 cartons over two weeks to break even (($24,000 + $3,600) ÷ $0.45), against the 60,000 you expect. The dip now decides it, so check whether past events really showed one.
