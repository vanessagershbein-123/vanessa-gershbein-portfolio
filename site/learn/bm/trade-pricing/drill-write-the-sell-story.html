Write the sell story

BrandDrill 3.415 minIntermediate

Skills: Writing & storytellingData & chartsPositioning & strategy

The prompt

Hypothetical scenario. The brand and all numbers are made up to show the work.

The facts. You're pitching a grocery chain's buyer at the yearly review of the nutrition bar aisle. You want two slots for Summit Oats Protein Bites, a new item. Your data:

  • The category grew 6% at this chain over the past year, against 9% nationally.
  • Bite-size bars, a newer segment, grew 22% nationally, but this chain carries only two bite items.
  • Your existing Summit Oats bars sell 15% faster per store than the average bar at this chain.
  • At three other chains that launched the Bites, about 40% of buyers were new to the brand, and about 25% hadn't bought any nutrition bar at that chain in the year before.
  • The two items you'd replace are in the bottom 10% of the aisle for sales per store. Together they sell about $90 per store per week. Based on the other chains, your two Bites should sell about $210 per store per week.
  • The chain has 400 stores and earns a 35% margin on the aisle.
  • Your support: $40,000 for in-store sampling and $25,000 of ads on the chain's own website and app.

Your task: Write a one-page sell storyA brand's pitch to a retail buyer showing how its product will grow the buyer's category.: a headline, three proof points, the math for the buyer, your ask, and the risk the buyer will raise, with your answer to it.

Your earlier answer

  1. Hint 1 of 3 Write for the buyer, not for your brand. What is the buyer judged on? Category sales, margin and shopper trips.
  2. Hint 2 of 3 Find the gap in the data: where is this chain behind the national trend, and how does your item help close it?
  3. Hint 3 of 3 End with a specific ask and the support behind it: which slots, when, and what you'll spend to make it work.

A strong one-pager

Headline: "Two bite-size slots can help close a 3-point growth gap in your bar aisle and add up to about $2.5 million in yearly sales across your 400 stores."

Proof points:

  1. The gap: your bar aisle grew 6% last year against 9% nationally, and the fastest-growing segment nationally, bites at +22%, has just two items on your shelf.
  2. Speed on shelf: Summit Oats bars already sell 15% faster per store than your average bar, so shoppers here know and buy the brand.
  3. New shoppers for the aisle: at three chains that launched the Bites, about a quarter of buyers hadn't bought a bar there in the year before, so part of the gain is new to the aisle, not just switching between brands. About 40% were new to Summit Oats, so the Bites won't just move sales from one of our items to another.

The math for the buyer:

  • The formula: extra sales = (new items' sales − replaced items' sales) per store per week × stores × 52 weeks.
  • Here: ($210 − $90) × 400 × 52 = about $2.5 million a year in sales, or about $874,000 of margin at your 35%.
  • Why it works this way: the buyer's shelf is fixed, so they judge a new item by what it earns compared with what it replaces, not by its own total.
  • The catch: this assumes the Bites' extra sales don't come out of other bars that stay on the shelf. Some will, so treat $2.5 million as the top of the range. The quarter of buyers who are new to the aisle is the part the buyer can count on most.

The ask: two slots in the spring reset, replacing the two bottom-10% items.

Our support: $40,000 of in-store sampling in the first eight weeks, and $25,000 of ads on your website and app aimed at bar buyers who don't buy bites yet.

The risk you'll hear, and your answer: "What if it just takes sales from your own bars?" Answer: "At the other chains, about 40% of Bites buyers were new to our brand and about a quarter were new to the bar aisle. We'll review your loyalty-card data with you at 12 weeks, and if the Bites aren't adding to the aisle, we'll take the slots back ourselves."

Why this works

  • It leads with the buyer's problem, the growth gap, not your product.
  • Every proof point is a number the buyer can check.
  • The ask is specific, and the support and the exit plan lower the buyer's risk.

What interviewers push on

  • "Your $210 comes from other chains. Why would it hold here?" Show that those chains look like this one in size, shoppers and price, and offer a test in a subset of stores if the buyer wants proof first.
  • "What if the buyer wants a promotion to launch it?" Say what you'd fund and why: a launch price to drive trial makes sense; a deep, permanent discount doesn't.

Practice twist

The buyer offers one slot instead of two, replacing the weaker of the two items ($40 per store per week). Your stronger Bite should sell $120 per store per week. What's the yearly gain?

Answer: ($120 − $40) × 400 × 52 = $1,664,000 in sales, about $582,000 of margin at 35%. One slot still delivers two-thirds of the total gain, so it's worth taking as a first step.
