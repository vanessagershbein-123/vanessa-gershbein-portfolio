Trade and promotion economics: where the money goes and breakeven lift

BrandLesson 3.115 min

Goal: Follow a trade dollar from the brand to the shelf, and work out whether a promotion paid for itself.

Skills: Pricing & promotionBusiness math

In the second quarter of 2026 (April to June), two Hefty businesses inside Reynolds Consumer Products went opposite ways on promotion. Hefty Home & Tableware ran fewer deals. Its retail volume fell 14% and its revenue fell $25 million, mostly because foam products were declining, yet its adjusted EBITDAEarnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization: a common measure of operating profit. (a common measure of operating profit) rose $8 million. Reynolds credited manufacturing efficiencies for the gain and said less promotion offset the profit lost on lower volume1.

Hefty Storage & Organization went the other way. Its retail volume rose 8% on strong food bag sales, but its adjusted EBITDA fell $3 million, mainly from the cost of ramping up new business and promoting behind new distribution1.

Fewer deals, less volume, more profit. More deals, more volume, less profit. Promotion wasn't the only thing moving either result, but it showed up in both. To see how both can make sense, follow the money a brand spends through retailers, then learn the one calculation that tells you whether a promotion paid for itself.

Words to know

Trade spendMoney a brand spends through retailers on price cuts, displays and features. Companies record it as a reduction of sales, not a marketing cost. is the money a brand pays retailers to cut prices, feature and display its products, and stock new items. These are the terms you'll hear in any trade conversation:

Term What it means A quick example
Trade promotion A short-term discount or in-store event the brand pays for at a retailer Two weeks at a lower price, plus an end-of-aisle display
TPR (temporary price reduction) A short-term cut in the shelf price $4.49 down to $3.49 for two weeks
Feature The product in the retailer's weekly ad, app or email, usually with its price "This week: $3.49" in the circular
Display Extra space outside the normal shelf An end-of-aisle stack or a pallet up front
Slotting fee A one-time payment to get a new item onto a retailer's shelf or into its warehouse A fee to launch a new flavor
Co-op advertising Brand money that helps pay for the retailer's ads featuring the brand The brand pays for part of a grocery chain's ad
Off-invoice deal A discount on every unit the retailer buys during the deal period 40 cents off each unit ordered in March
Scan-down (scan-based deal) A discount the brand pays on each unit that scans at the register during the deal 40 cents for each unit sold March 1 to 14
Baseline What you'd have sold with no promotion 1,000 units a week
Incremental units The extra units a promotion adds above baseline 600 more units during the deal
Subsidized units Units sold on deal that shoppers would have bought anyway at full price Your usual 1,000 a week, now discounted
Post-promotion dip A drop after the deal while shoppers use up what they stocked Sales below baseline for two weeks
Forward buying A retailer buying extra at the deal price to sell later at full price Ordering ten weeks of stock during a two-week deal
Breakeven lift How much sales must rise during a deal just to match what you'd have earned without it "We need sales to double to break even"

Where the money goes

Trade spend doesn't show up where you might expect. Kimberly-Clark's annual report lists its trade promotion programs as slotting feesA one-time payment a brand makes to get a new product onto a retailer's shelf or into its warehouse., cooperative marketing, temporary price reductionsTemporary price reduction: a short-term cut in the shelf price, usually paid for by the brand. and other activities its retail customers run, and it books their cost as a reduction in sales revenue, not as a marketing expense2.

That accounting choice matters when you read results. Trade spend comes out before net salesSales after discounts, returns and trade promotions: the revenue a company reports., so when a brand cuts promotion, the saving shows up as better price/mixThe part of a sales change that comes from price changes and from shifts toward pricier or cheaper items., and when it promotes harder, its net price falls even if its list price never moved.

It's a lot of money. In 2019, McKinsey reported that consumer-goods companies worldwide put about 20% of their revenue into trade promotion each year and, citing 2016 Nielsen research, that 59% of promotions lost money, or 72% in the United States3. Those figures are several years old, so treat them as a warning sign rather than a current benchmark.

What a promotion bump is made of

When a deal starts, sales jump. The question that matters is where the extra units came from.

A classic study of store scanner dataStore checkout scans that firms like Circana and NielsenIQ collect from retailers and sell to many brands. split the bump into three parts: shoppers switching from other brands, purchases borrowed from other weeks, and genuinely new buying in the category. Across four datasets, each part averaged about a third4.

Which third is which depends on whose money it is. The switching third is real growth for your brand, but not for the retailer, whose category didn't grow. The borrowed third is nobody's growth: those shoppers would have bought later at full price. The last third grows the category, which is what the retailer's buyer is judged on.

Borrowing is easy to miss. Circana warns that a promotion showing a 12% bump during the deal can add nothing to the year once the slump afterward is counted. It depends on the category: promote a drink and people tend to drink more, but promote laundry detergent and shoppers stock up at home and wait longer to buy again5.

Retailers see the same leak from their side. A study of CVS's 2003 promotions found that about 45% of the gross salesSales at list price, before trade discounts and returns. lift was incremental for CVS. A field test then indicated that dropping promotions chainwide in 15 of the worst-performing categories would cut sales by about $7.8 million but raise profit by about $52.6 million6.

The breakeven math

An illustration with made-up numbers.

You sell a jar of pasta sauce to a grocery chain for $3.00. After making and shipping it, you earn $1.00 a jar. The chain normally sells 2,000 jars a week. It offers a one-week TPR, and you agree to fund $0.40 off every jar sold during the deal through a scan-downA trade deal the brand pays only on units that scan at the register during the promotion..

Line Math Result
What you earn per jar on deal $1.00 − $0.40 $0.60
What you'd earn without the deal 2,000 × $1.00 $2,000
Jars you must sell on deal to match it $2,000 ÷ $0.60 about 3,333
Breakeven lift 3,333 ÷ 2,000 about 1.67 times normal sales, or +67%

The formula: breakeven units on deal = (normal units × normal earnings per unit + any fixed costs + profit lost in the dip afterward) ÷ earnings per unit on deal. With no fees and no dip, the breakeven liftHow much sales must rise during a promotion just to earn the same profit as not running it., as a multiple of normal sales, is simply normal earnings per unit ÷ earnings per unit on deal: here $1.00 ÷ $0.60 ≈ 1.67.

Why it works: the discount lands on every jar sold during the deal, including the 2,000 shoppers would have bought anyway. Those jars are subsidized: you've given away $0.40 on each, $800 in all. The extra jars have to earn that back before the deal makes a cent.

Watch how fast it climbs. At $0.50 off, you earn $0.50 a jar on deal, so you need double your normal sales. At $0.75 off, you need four times. Add a $1,000 display fee at $0.40 off, and the target jumps from about 3,333 jars to 5,000: ($2,000 + $1,000) ÷ $0.60.

The common mistake: judging a deal by its lift. Say sales rise 50%, to 3,000 jars. That sounds strong, but 3,000 × $0.60 = $1,800, against $2,000 without the deal, so you lose $200 for the week before counting any dip. The real comparison is profit with the deal against profit without it, over the same weeks, including the weeks after.

Who pockets the discount

How you pay for a deal changes who benefits. A Knowledge at Wharton article explains the trouble with off-invoice dealsA trade deal taken off the invoice for every unit a retailer buys during the deal period, whether or not it passes the discount on., which pay retailers on every unit they buy during the deal: retailers can load up, skip passing the discount on, and sell the extra later at full price (forward buyingA retailer buying extra during a deal to sell after the deal ends.) or resell it to other retailers (diverting). Pay-for-performance deals, often called scan-backs, pay just for units sold to shoppers during the promotion7.

The problem hasn't gone away. UNFI, a major U.S. grocery wholesaler, warned in its 2026 annual report that fewer chances to forward buy on promotions could significantly hurt its profitability8.

Why everyone keeps promoting

If so many deals lose money, why do brands keep running them? Part of the answer is competition. No single brand can stop on its own, and as Wharton's David Bell put it, "they all end up in the trade promotion trap"7.

Part of it is measurement. In a 2026 survey of senior UK marketers, spending on price promotions had overtaken spending on paid media. Yet 75% couldn't estimate what share of the previous year's promotions were profitable, 82% believed their most recent one was, and 76% repeated promotions they hadn't checked for profitability9.

Design matters too. In one company's case described by McKinsey, buy-one-get-one deals mostly subsidized shoppers who were already loyal, while promotions aimed at infrequent shoppers who weren't loyal to any brand raised household penetrationThe share of households that bought you at least once in a period. enough to pay for themselves3. A good promotion is designed around who it reaches, not just how deep it cuts.

In this Europanel interview, Tilburg University marketing professor Marnik Dekimpe explains why a sales spike isn't profit, how promotions subsidize loyal buyers who would have bought anyway, and how repeated deals train shoppers to wait for a lower price. The promotion part runs to about 8:25.

In an interview

When a case puts a promotion on the table, don't start with the lift. Start with what you earn per unit on deal, what you'd sell without it, and whether a dip follows. Work out breakeven, compare it with the lift you can realistically expect, and then say what you'd change: the depth, the fee, the timing or the shoppers it targets.

Self-check: You earn $2.20 a unit on a frozen meal and normally sell 1,500 a week at one chain. The chain wants a TPR that costs you $0.80 a unit, and you expect sales of 2,100 that week. Does it pay?

On deal you earn $1.40 a unit. Without the deal you'd earn 1,500 × $2.20 = $3,300. With it, you'd earn 2,100 × $1.40 = $2,940, so you lose about $360 before any dip. Breakeven is $3,300 ÷ $1.40, about 2,357 units, a lift of about 57%, and you expect 40%. Either negotiate a shallower discount or aim the deal at shoppers who don't buy you yet.

Check your understanding

4 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. You earn $2.70 a unit at regular price, and a promotion will cost you $0.90 a unit. Ignoring fees and any dip, how big a lift do you need to break even?

    Show answer

    50%. On deal you earn $1.80 a unit, so you need $2.70 ÷ $1.80 = 1.5 times your normal sales just to match what you'd have earned without the deal.

  2. A promotion lifted your sales 140% during the deal week, and the buyer calls it a big success. What do you need to know before you agree?

    Show answer

    How many of those units shoppers would have bought anyway, what you earned per unit on deal, and whether sales dipped afterward. A big lift can still lose money if most units were subsidized, the margin on deal was thin, or the deal borrowed sales from the weeks after it.

  3. Two deals cost you the same. One pays the retailer on every case it orders during the deal period. The other pays on each unit that scans at the register during the promotion. Which is likely to waste less of your money?

    Show answer

    The scan-based deal, because the retailer can't stock up at the deal price and sell the extra later at full price. Paying on units bought invites forward buying and diverting. Paying on units sold during the deal ties your money to what shoppers actually got.

  4. Paper towels and potato chips run the same discount and get the same lift. Which is more likely to see a deep dip afterward?

    Show answer

    Paper towels, because shoppers stock up and use them at the same rate, so the deal mostly borrows from later weeks. When extra product at home doesn't change how much people use, a deal pulls purchases forward. When it does, as with many snacks and drinks, part of the lift is new consumption.
