How a CPG brand makes money: retailers, sell-in and sell-through

BrandLesson 1.213 min

Goal: See why retailers are a brand's biggest customers, tell shipments apart from what shoppers buy, and explain it when the two numbers drift apart.

Skills: Business mathPricing & promotion

In June 2025, Clorox was about to move its U.S. business onto a new ERPEnterprise resource planning: the software that runs a company's orders, inventory, shipping and finance. system, the software that takes orders and runs shipping. To be safe, it was adding 1.5 weeks of inventory at retailers, which typically hold four weeks' worth of goods1.

Retailers stocked up. In the April to June quarter, Clorox's net salesSales after discounts, returns and trade promotions: the revenue a company reports. rose 4% to $2.0 billion, and the extra ERP shipments added about 13 to 14 percentage pointsThe plain difference between two percentages. Going from 17.3% to 17.7% is a rise of 0.4 points. to that growth rate2. In other words, without the extra stock, sales would have fallen.

Then the stock ran down. By the end of June, Clorox said, it had shipped about two weeks of inventory "ahead of consumption," a bit more than the original cushion. As retailers worked through it in the July to September quarter, net sales fell 19% to $1.43 billion, mainly from lower shipments tied to the switch. Some of the damage was real, though: order-fulfillment problems earlier in the year had cost Clorox consumption and market share2.

Two numbers moved very differently there: what Clorox shipped, and what shoppers bought. Brand managers live between those two numbers.

Your biggest customers are retailers

When you buy Tide, you almost always buy it from a store, not from P&G. That makes a handful of retailers enormous customers:

Company Walmart's share of its sales The wider picture
P&G (year to June 2026) About 16% Its top ten customers are about 43% of net sales3
General Mills (year to May 2026) 22% Walmart is 31% of its North America Retail segment4
Kraft Heinz (2025) About 21% Both of its reportable segments sell to Walmart5

When one customer buys a fifth of what you make, its buyer's view of your plan carries real weight. That's why brand managers spend so much time with their Sales partners on plans for each big retailer: which items it carries, at what price, with which promotions and displays.

Retailers also charge brands to be on the shelf. This explainer walks through the fees and discounts, from slotting feesA one-time payment a brand makes to get a new product onto a retailer's shelf or into its warehouse. for new items to penalties for late or incorrect deliveries.

Sell-in and sell-through

  • Sell-inWhat a brand ships to retailers and distributors. It's what the brand books as sales. is what you ship to retailers and distributors. It's what your company books as sales, so it's the number in quarterly results.
  • Sell-throughWhat shoppers buy from the retailer. (some companies say sell-out) is what shoppers buy at the register. Brands track it with scanner dataStore checkout scans that firms like Circana and NielsenIQ collect from retailers and sell to many brands. from firms like Circana and NielsenIQ, and call it consumption or retail sales.
  • Retailer inventory sits between the two. When sell-in runs ahead of sell-through, stock builds up in stores and warehouses. When it falls behind, stock drains.

Big companies explain the gap to investors. In results released in March 2026, General Mills said organic net salesSales growth after removing currency moves and businesses bought or sold, so you see what the existing brands did. in its North America Retail business were down 4%, while Nielsen-measured retail sales were down 3%, with the 1-point gap reflecting changes in retailer inventory4.

Here's what usually pulls the two numbers apart:

What happened Sell-in vs. sell-through What comes next
A new item ships to every store Sell-in jumps far ahead Reorders depend on whether shoppers come back
Retailers stock up before a price increase or a big promotion Sell-in runs ahead Orders dip once the extra stock sells
Retailers cut inventory to free up cash Sell-in falls behind Orders recover once stock settles at its new level
A supply problem, like a plant outage or a system switch Sell-in falls behind, and shelves can go empty Shoppers who switch brands may not come back

Over a longer stretch, growth in shipments and growth in consumption tend to line up, because stores can't keep piling up stock. In any single quarter they can be far apart, so when someone tells you shipments jumped, your first question is what shoppers did.

Where a shelf dollar goes

An illustration with made-up numbers.

Say a jar of salsa sells for $5.00 on the shelf. The retailer keeps $1.50, a 30% margin on the shelf price, so it pays the brand $3.50. Across the year, the brand spends an average of $0.40 a jar on promotions and displays, so it really nets $3.10. Making and shipping the jar costs $1.90, leaving $1.20 of gross profit. Advertising takes another $0.30.

That leaves the brand $0.90 out of a $5.00 sale to cover salaries, R&D and offices, and to earn a profit. Every dime of extra discount comes out of that $0.90.

The online shelf

P&G's e-commerce sales grew 6% in its 2026 fiscal year and now make up 20% of its total sales3. Much of that online shelf belongs to retailers like Amazon and Walmart, so the same logic applies: you sell in to a retailer, and the retailer sells through to shoppers.

Self-check: A new salsa shipped 1.2 million jars in its first quarter. Scanner data shows shoppers bought 400,000. Is it a hit?

Too early to say. Most of the 1.2 million is pipeline fill: stock sitting on shelves and in warehouses. Watch sell-through per store per week and how many first-time buyers come back for a second jar. Reorders will follow what shoppers do, not what shipped.

Check your understanding

4 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. This quarter your shipments rose 12%, but scanner data shows shoppers bought only 2% more. What's the most likely story?

    Show answer

    Retailers built up inventory, so expect slower orders ahead. When sell-in runs ahead of sell-through, stock piles up at retailers. Unless shoppers speed up, orders slow later as that stock sells down.

  2. Next year you'll lap a quarter with a big new-item launch. Shipments fall 15% while scanner sales are flat. What do you tell leadership?

    Show answer

    Shoppers are buying the same amount; last year's number included a one-time pipeline fill. Compare against what made last year's number. A one-time fill inflates the base, so the next year looks like a decline even when shoppers haven't changed.

  3. You want to know whether a TV campaign worked. Which number do you check first?

    Show answer

    Sell-through and market share during and after the campaign. Advertising aims at shoppers, so judge it on what shoppers bought. Shipments mix in each retailer's ordering decisions.

  4. One retailer is a fifth of your sales and asks for a deeper discount to keep a display. What's the right first step?

    Show answer

    Work out with Sales and Finance whether the display's extra sales pay for the discount, then negotiate. A big customer deserves a serious answer, and the answer still has to pay back. Run the math, then negotiate with real numbers.
