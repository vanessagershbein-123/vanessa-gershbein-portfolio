What a brand manager does, and who you work with

BrandLesson 1.112 min

Goal: Know what a CPG brand manager owns, who you'll work with every week, and how to read titles that don't match across companies.

Skills: Interviews & career

In 1931, a young ad man at Procter & Gamble got fed up. Neil McElroy was working on the advertising for Camay soap, and Camay wasn't only up against soaps from Lever and Palmolive. It was also up against Ivory, P&G's own flagship. On May 13, 1931, he wrote a memo arguing that each brand should have one person in charge, backed by a team that worked on that brand and nothing else: a brand assistant plus a few specialists. The memo ran three pages, which broke President Deupree's well-known one-page-memo rule. Deupree endorsed it with enthusiasm anyway1.

The idea was that each brand would be marketed "as if it were a separate business." It became the modern system of brand management, and it became P&G's route to the top. By 2000, every P&G chief executive after Deupree had brand-management experience. McElroy later ran the company himself, then became President Eisenhower's Secretary of Defense in 19571.

P&G still tells it the same way. It says it invented brand management in 1931 to give each brand one point of accountability, and it describes the job today as being "a total business owner." It draws the line clearly: brand management creates the brands and oversees every part of their success, while marketing focuses on individual campaigns2.

What you actually own

"Business owner" sounds grand. Day to day, it means a set of numbers you answer for and a set of people you need. Job postings are the clearest description of the work, because companies have to spell it out:

  • The numbers and the rhythm. Kraft Heinz's associate brand managers own key parts of what it calls the "brand operating rhythm": performance tracking, forecasting inputs, customer programs, commercialization work and agency coordination, all while partnering closely with Sales, Finance, Insights and Operations. They track share, volume and revenue, find what's driving them and help act on it3.
  • The data and the budget. Clorox's associate marketing managers analyze the health of the business with syndicated dataStore checkout scans that firms like Circana and NielsenIQ collect from retailers and sell to many brands. from Circana plus custom research, and they manage the A&SPAdvertising and sales promotion (or advertising and promotion): the brand's budget for reaching consumers directly. and packaging budgets for their business unit's marketing team4. A&SP means advertising and sales promotion. Syndicated data is store sales data that a research firm collects from retailers and sells to the brands that subscribe, so you can see your share and your competitors' wherever the data covers.

Notice who's missing from those lists: anyone who reports to you. You own the result. Sales, Finance, R&D and Operations own most of the levers that move it.

Who you work with

Keurig Dr Pepper tells its MBA brand interns to work with colleagues across Marketing, Consumer Insights, Finance, Innovation, Sales and Operations5. Here's what each of those partners typically owns at a big CPGConsumer packaged goods: everyday products like food, drinks, cleaning supplies and personal care items. company, and what they need from you. This is a general guide, not one company's org chart:

Partner What they own What they need from you
Sales (customer teams) The relationship with each retailer: which items it carries, shelf space, promotions A story the retailer's buyer cares about, and promotions that pay back
Revenue growth management (at many big companies) Pricing, pack sizes and promotion rules across the portfolio A clear view of what your shoppers will pay and which promotions actually work
Finance Forecasts, budgets and the official numbers Numbers you can defend, and early warning when they move
Consumer insights Research and syndicated data The decision a study will inform, before it's fielded
R&D and innovation Formulas, products and packaging A clear consumer need and a target cost
Operations and supply chain Making and shipping the product A forecast they can plan to, and time to react to changes
Agencies Creative and media A tight brief and fast, clear feedback

The skill underneath all of it is influence. Most weeks you're asking people who don't report to you to put your brand near the top of their list, so you win them over with clear goals, good data and follow-through.

Titles don't line up

At P&G, the ladder runs Brand Manager, Senior Brand Manager, Brand Director and Senior Brand Director, and Senior Brand Manager is the level for people with an advanced degree and some work experience2. Other companies use the same words differently:

Company Title for the role Experience asked Posted pay
P&G Senior Brand Manager (MBA hires) An advanced degree and some work experience Not on that page
Clorox Associate Marketing Manager (MBA recent graduate) At least 3 years of work; an MBA or master's is a plus, not a must $117,000 to $130,000 a year4
Kraft Heinz Associate Brand Manager (experienced hire) A bachelor's degree and 3 to 5 years $104,200 to $130,2003
Keurig Dr Pepper Associate Brand Manager (MBA summer intern) 3 to 6 years after college5 Not compared here

So "senior" at one company can be the entry point for MBAs, while "associate" at another is a hire with years of experience. Ignore the title and read three things: the experience asked for, the pay band and what you'd own.

Two jobs called "brand manager"

Search job boards for "brand manager" and you'll find two different jobs under one title.

At a consumer goods company, the brand manager runs a business: share, volume, forecasts and budgets, worked through Sales, Finance and Operations, like the Kraft Heinz and Clorox roles above. At many other companies, the brand manager looks after how the brand shows up. Drone maker Unusual Machines, for example, wants its brand manager to serve as the steward of its brand identity: positioning and messaging, integrated campaigns, consistency across launches and packaging, and its social media strategy and brand voice. The same posting calls the role both "Brand Manager" and "Brand Marketing Manager"6.

Here's how the two usually differ. Plenty of smaller companies blend them:

CPG brand manager Brand identity and marketing manager
You own The brand's results and its P&L How the brand looks, sounds and tells its story
You're judged on Share, volume, net sales and profit Awareness, consistency and engagement, plus campaign results
You work most with Sales, Finance, R&D, Operations, agencies Creative, design, social, product, agencies
The data you live in Syndicated store data and shipments Campaign, social and brand-tracking data
The interview tests A marketing case with numbers Your portfolio, creative judgment and campaign thinking

Neither is the "real" brand manager. They reward different strengths, so read the duties before you prep: a P&LProfit and loss statement: sales at the top, costs below, and profit at the bottom., share data and forecasts point to the first, while brand guidelines, voice and social point to the second.

In an interview

When someone asks, "What does a brand manager do?", skip the textbook. Say you'd own the brand's results, name the two or three partners you'd lean on hardest for the problem in front of you, and say how you'd earn their help.

Self-check: Your brand's share fell a point at one big retailer last month. Who do you talk to first, and what do you ask?

Start with the data and the Sales lead for that account. Pull the syndicated data (or ask Insights) to see whether the drop is distribution, price, promotion or shoppers buying less per store. Ask Sales what changed on shelf: a lost display, a competitor's promotion, an out-of-stock. Bring Finance in once you know the cause and need money to fix it.

Check your understanding

4 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. R&D has a cheaper formula that saves money but scored slightly lower with consumers. Sales wants it for the margin. Who should own the recommendation?

    Show answer

    You, as the brand's owner, after weighing the consumer risk against the savings with R&D and Finance. The brand manager is the single point of accountability. Partners own the levers and the expertise, but you own the trade-off and the recommendation. A call this size usually still goes to your director for sign-off.

  2. You're weighing two offers: "Associate Marketing Manager" at one company and "Senior Brand Manager" at another. How do you tell which is more senior?

    Show answer

    Compare the experience each asks for, the pay band and what you'd own. Titles don't travel between companies. One company's senior title is where MBAs start, and another's associate title asks for years of experience.

  3. An agency presents a campaign your director loves, but it doesn't match the brief. What do you do?

    Show answer

    Give the agency specific feedback tied to the brief and the consumer insight, and raise your concern with your director before approving. Agencies need a tight brief and clear, fast feedback. You own the brand, so you speak up, with reasons, before it ships.

  4. Your updated numbers say next quarter's forecast is too high. Operations has already planned production to your old number. What's your first move?

    Show answer

    Tell Operations and Finance right away, agree a revised plan and explain what changed. Operations needs a forecast it can plan to and time to react. Early warning costs far less than a warehouse full of product nobody ordered.
