Marketing math sprint

BrandDrill 1.212 minIntermediate

Skills: Business math

The prompt

Hypothetical numbers, made up for practice.

The facts. Eight questions, all from a made-up snack brand. Set a 10-minute timer and write one line of math for each.

  1. A box of crackers sells to the retailer for $5.00 and costs $3.50 to make. What's the margin %? What's the markupProfit as a share of cost rather than of price. A $1.50 profit on a $3.50 cost is a 43% markup. %?
  2. A bag of chips sells on shelf for $3.99. The retailer keeps 25% of the shelf price, and the brand earns 35% of what the retailer pays. How much does the brand earn per bag?
  3. A retailer offers an end-cap display for $600,000. Each extra unit sold earns $1.20. How many extra units do you need to break even? If the display adds 40,000 units a week, how many weeks does that take?
  4. A product earns $1.50 a unit. Packaging costs rise $0.25 a unit, and you don't raise the price. How much more volume do you need to earn the same total margin?
  5. A new flavor earns $0.72 a unit. The original flavor it may steal from earns $1.20. What's the breakeven cannibalization rate?
  6. Brand sales grew from $50 million to $62 million over three years. What's the compound annual growth rateCompound annual growth rate: the steady yearly growth rate that gets you from a starting value to an ending one.?
  7. The brand sells 40,000 small bags at $2.49 and 10,000 large bags at $4.99. What's the average price per bag?
  8. Price elasticityHow much the quantity sold changes when price changes. An elasticity of −1.5 means a 10% price rise cuts units about 15%. is −1.5, and you raise the price 6%. What happens to units? What happens to revenue?

Your task: Answer all eight, then check which tool each question needed.

Your earlier answer

  1. Hint 1 of 3 Before you calculate anything, name the tool each question needs: margin or markup, the channel chain, breakeven, CAGR, a weighted average or elasticity.
  2. Hint 2 of 3 Watch what you divide by. Margin divides by price and markup divides by cost, and each partner's margin comes off that partner's own selling price.
  3. Hint 3 of 3 For #4 and #5, write total margin = units × margin per unit and set the old total equal to the new one. For #6, growth compounds, so you need a root, not a division.

How each one works

1. Margin vs. markup

  • The formulas: margin % = (price − cost) ÷ price. Markup % = (price − cost) ÷ cost.
  • The math: $5.00 − $3.50 = $1.50 of profit. $1.50 ÷ $5.00 = 30% margin, and $1.50 ÷ $3.50 = 43% markup.
  • Why: it's the same $1.50, measured against two different bases. Margin asks what share of the selling price is profit, which is how a P&LProfit and loss statement: sales at the top, costs below, and profit at the bottom. works, since every line is a share of net salesSales after discounts, returns and trade promotions: the revenue a company reports.. Markup asks how much was added on top of cost, which is how many retailers think about pricing.
  • To convert: margin = markup ÷ (1 + markup), so 43% ÷ 1.43 = 30%. Markup = margin ÷ (1 − margin), so 30% ÷ 0.70 = 43%.
  • The trap: a buyer quotes one while you're thinking in the other. Ask which they mean.

2. The channel chain

  • The formula: what each partner pays = the next partner's selling price × (1 − that partner's margin). Your profit = your selling price × your margin.
  • The math: the retailer keeps 25% of $3.99, so it pays the brand $3.99 × 0.75 = $2.99. The brand keeps 35% of its own $2.99: $2.99 × 0.35 = about $1.05.
  • Why: each partner's margin is a share of that partner's own selling price, so you work backward from the shelf one step at a time.
  • The trap: taking the brand's 35% off the shelf price ($3.99 × 0.35 = $1.40) overstates what you earn by 35 cents a bag.

3. Breakeven volume

  • The formula: breakeven units = fixed cost ÷ contribution per unit. Weeks to pay back = breakeven units ÷ extra units per week.
  • The math: $600,000 ÷ $1.20 = 500,000 extra units. 500,000 ÷ 40,000 a week = 12.5 weeks.
  • Why: a fixed cost doesn't change with volume. Each extra unit chips away at it by its contribution, the price minus what it costs to make and ship one more.
  • The trap: counting every unit sold during the display. If the store sells 100,000 units a week while the display is up, but only 40,000 of them are extra, using 100,000 says you pay back in 5 weeks instead of 12.5. Then check the answer against how long the display runs: if it's up for four weeks, it can't earn back a cost that needs 12.5 weeks of extra sales.

4. Volume needed after a margin cut

  • The formula: extra volume needed = old unit margin ÷ new unit margin − 1.
  • The math: the new margin is $1.50 − $0.25 = $1.25. $1.50 ÷ $1.25 = 1.20, so you need 20% more volume.
  • Why: total margin = units × margin per unit. To keep the total the same, old units × $1.50 must equal new units × $1.25, so new units ÷ old units = $1.50 ÷ $1.25.
  • Where else it works: any time you give up margin per unit, including a price cut. For example, cutting price by $0.30 drops a $1.50 margin to $1.20, so you'd need $1.50 ÷ $1.20 − 1 = 25% more volume just to stand still.

5. Breakeven cannibalization

  • The formula: breakeven cannibalization rate = new item's unit margin ÷ old item's unit margin.
  • The math: $0.72 ÷ $1.20 = 60%.
  • Why: each new unit earns $0.72. If a share c of those units would otherwise have been the original flavor, each one also costs c × $1.20 in lost original sales. You break even when $0.72 = c × $1.20, so c = 60%. Check with 100 new units: you earn $72, and losing 60 original units costs 60 × $1.20 = $72.
  • The trap: it looks like #4, but there's no "− 1" and the margins are the other way up. Say which formula you're using before you divide.

6. Compound annual growth rate (CAGR)

  • The formula: CAGR = (ending value ÷ starting value)^(1 ÷ n) − 1, where n is the number of years of growth.
  • The math: $62M ÷ $50M = 1.24. The cube root of 1.24 is about 1.074, so about 7.4% a year. Check it: $50M × 1.074 × 1.074 × 1.074 ≈ $62M.
  • Why: growth compounds. Each year grows from the year before, not from the starting point, so the yearly rate is the root, not the total divided by the years.
  • The trap: 24% ÷ 3 = 8% a year overstates it, because it ignores compounding. Also, n counts years of growth, not data points: 2023 to 2026 is three years of growth, even though it spans four years of data.

7. Weighted average price

  • The formula: average price = total revenue ÷ total units, never the average of the prices.
  • The math: 40,000 × $2.49 = $99,600, and 10,000 × $4.99 = $49,900. That's $149,500 ÷ 50,000 bags = $2.99.
  • Why: each price should count as many times as the units sold at it. The simple average of the two prices, $3.74, pretends both sizes sold equally.
  • Where it shows up: this is what "mix" means in a sales bridge. For example, if next year the brand sells 30,000 small bags and 20,000 large at the same prices, the average price rises to $3.49, about 17% higher, without a single price change.

8. Price elasticity

  • The formulas: % change in units ≈ elasticity × % change in price. % change in revenue = (1 + % price change) × (1 + % unit change) − 1.
  • The math: −1.5 × 6% = −9% units. Revenue: 1.06 × 0.91 = 0.965, so revenue falls about 3.5%.
  • Why: elasticity measures how sensitive shoppers are to price. Below −1, units fall faster than price rises, so revenue drops. Between 0 and −1, units fall more slowly than price rises, so revenue grows. The formula is an estimate that works best for small price changes.
  • Don't stop at revenue: profit can still rise. For example, if the bag sells for $4.00 with a $1.00 margin and costs don't change, a 6% price increase adds $0.24 to the margin. Profit becomes 0.91 × $1.24 ≈ 1.13 times what it was, up about 13%, even though revenue fell.

Which tool when

If the question says... Use
"How many units to pay back" a fixed cost Breakeven volume: fixed cost ÷ unit contribution
Cost goes up or price goes down: "how much more must we sell?" Breakeven rate: old margin ÷ new margin − 1
A new item steals from an existing one Breakeven cannibalization: new margin ÷ old margin
"Average price" across items Total revenue ÷ total units
"Margin" or "markup" Margin: profit ÷ price. Markup: profit ÷ cost
What each partner in the chain earns Work back from the shelf: price × (1 − partner's margin)
"Growth per year" over several years CAGR: (end ÷ start)^(1 ÷ years) − 1
"What happens to units if we raise price?" Elasticity × % price change, then check revenue and profit

Practice twist

Redo #8 with an elasticity of −0.5. Does revenue rise or fall?

Answer: units fall about 3%, and revenue rises about 2.8% (1.06 × 0.97 − 1). Demand is inelastic, so the price increase wins.
