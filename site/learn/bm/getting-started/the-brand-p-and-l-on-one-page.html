The brand P&L on one page

BrandLesson 1.313 min

Goal: Read a brand P&L from gross sales down to profit, see where trade spend and advertising sit, and know which lines you control.

Skills: Business mathPricing & promotion

P&G spent $10.2 billion on advertising in its 2026 fiscal year, on $87.0 billion of net salesSales after discounts, returns and trade promotions: the revenue a company reports.. That number is easy to find. What it spends on retailer price cuts, displays and coupons is harder to spot, because it doesn't show up as a marketing cost. P&G records its sales net of trade promotionMoney a brand spends through retailers on price cuts, displays and features. Companies record it as a reduction of sales, not a marketing cost. spending, so that money comes off the top before sales are reported1.

That's the first surprise in a brand P&LProfit and loss statement: sales at the top, costs below, and profit at the bottom.. One of its biggest numbers isn't booked as a marketing expense at all, even though brand and sales teams plan it together. Some CPGConsumer packaged goods: everyday products like food, drinks, cleaning supplies and personal care items. companies spend up to 20% of their gross revenue on promotions, which makes it one of the largest items on the P&L2.

The P&L, top to bottom

− Cost of goods sold −$51M Ingredients, packaging, making and shipping

− Overheads −$11M Salaries, R&D, offices, usually shared across brands
Operating profit $13M About 15% of net sales

Four habits will keep you out of trouble in a P&L review:

  • Margins are a share of net sales, not gross salesSales at list price, before trade discounts and returns.. Gross marginGross profit as a share of net sales: what's left after the cost of making and shipping the product. is gross profit divided by net sales.
  • Trade spend sits above net sales, and advertising sits below gross profit. Move a dollar from promotions into advertising, and if units don't change, your reported sales go up by a dollar while profit stays the same.
  • Price-based promotions count as trade spend. Coupons and other money off the price usually come out of sales, so they sit above net sales even when the consumer gets the discount.
  • Names vary. "Brand contribution" might be called direct margin or brand profit somewhere else. Ask what's included before you compare two brands.

Trade spend comes off the top

P&G isn't unusual. Clorox, General Mills and Kraft Heinz also book trade promotions as a cut to sales, not as a marketing cost:

  • Clorox nets the cost of shelf price reductions, in-store merchandising and consumer coupons against sales. Its estimate of what it owes retailers is big enough that a 10% change would move net sales by about $21 million3.
  • General Mills reports revenue net of trade promotion and consumer coupon redemption4.
  • Kraft Heinz records trade promotion as a reduction to revenues, while its advertising sits in selling, general and administrative expenses5.

McKinsey calls trade incentives one of the largest and least disciplined investments most organizations make: many put 8 to 11% of their revenue into them, and some up to 20%2.

Here's why it matters to you. A promotion lowers the price on every unit sold on deal, including the ones loyal shoppers would have bought at full price anyway. Shoppers also stock up on deal and then buy less for a few weeks afterward. So a promotion can double your volume for two weeks and still lose money.

P&G keeps about half of each sales dollar after making the product, and Kraft Heinz keeps about a third. Relative to its sales, P&G also spends far more on advertising. Their categories and business models differ, so compare a brand with its own history and its closest competitors, not with a company in a different aisle.

Margin vs. markup

Retail buyers and brand teams can use different math for what sounds like the same idea.

  • MarkupProfit as a share of cost rather than of price. A $1.50 profit on a $3.50 cost is a 43% markup. divides the profit by what the seller paid. OpenStax's example: buy shoes for $50, sell them for $75, and the markup is 50%6.
  • Margin divides the same profit by the selling price. On those shoes, for example, the margin is $25 ÷ $75, or about 33%.

Same $25, two very different percentages. When a buyer quotes a number, ask which one they mean.

Unit contribution: the number behind most brand math

Contribution marginPrice minus the variable cost of one more unit: what each unit adds toward fixed costs and profit. is how much a product's selling price exceeds its variable cost per unit. It's called a contribution because it's what each unit adds toward covering fixed costs6.

Brand decisions turn on it. Whether a display fee, a TV flight or a new flavor that steals sales from the old one pays off comes down to how many extra units you sell and what each one contributes.

What you control

You don't control every line, but you influence most of them, usually through a partner:

P&L line Your lever Who you need
Gross sales Price, pack sizes, which retailers carry you Sales, Finance
Trade spend How deep, how often and with which retailers you promote Sales
Cost of goods Formula, packaging, pack size R&D, Operations
Advertising Budget, media mix, creative Agencies, Insights
Overheads Mostly not yours Leadership

When a case hands you a promotion or a price change, walk down the P&L out loud: what happens to units, net price, gross profit and contribution. Don't stop at volume.

Self-check: Your director wants more profit next year with no extra budget. Name three places on the P&L to look.

Trade spend first: cut promotions that don't pay back, since that money comes straight off net sales. Then cost of goods: a packaging or formula saving that consumers won't notice. Then price and mix: a price or pack-size change shoppers will accept, and more of the sizes and items with higher margins. Cutting advertising works on paper this year, but check what it does to sales next year.

Check your understanding

4 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. A two-week promotion cuts the shelf price 25% and doubles units. Finance says the event lost money. How can both be true?

    Show answer

    The discount applies to every unit sold on deal, including ones shoppers would have bought anyway, so the extra units didn't cover it. Trade spend comes off net sales on all units sold on deal. Only the truly extra units pay for it, and they often aren't enough.

  2. You move $5 million from trade promotions into advertising, and units don't change. What happens to reported net sales?

    Show answer

    They rise by $5 million, while profit stays the same. Trade spend is subtracted from sales, while advertising is an expense below gross profit. Moving money between them changes reported sales and margins, not profit.

  3. A buyer says the retailer needs a 30% margin on your item, which will sell for $4.00. What will the retailer pay you?

    Show answer

    $2.80. Retail margin is a share of the selling price: 30% of $4.00 is $1.20 for the retailer, so it pays you $2.80. Treating the 30% as a markup on cost ($4.00 ÷ 1.3 = $3.08) is the classic mix-up.

  4. Your gross margin fell 2 points while net sales grew. What do you check first?

    Show answer

    Whether costs rose faster than your net price, including deeper promotions, or sales shifted to lower-margin items. Gross margin only uses net sales and cost of goods. Advertising and overheads sit below it, so they can't move it.
