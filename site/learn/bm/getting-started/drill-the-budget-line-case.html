The budget-line case

BrandDrill 1.115 minIntermediate

Skills: Business mathInterviews & careerPositioning & strategy

The prompt

Hypothetical scenario. The brand and all numbers are made up to show the work.

The facts.

Your interviewer slides a page across the table:

  • Casa Verde is a premium pasta sauce. Its 24-ounce jar sells for $4.49 on shelf, and the brand earns $1.40 on each jar it ships.
  • Over the past year it lost two share points. Most of the lost buyers switched to store brandsProducts sold under a retailer's own brand name, like Kirkland Signature at Costco. at two big grocery chains, where the store-brand jar sells for $2.29.
  • The team wants to launch Casa Verde Everyday: a simpler recipe in the same size jar, priced at $2.99. The brand would earn $0.63 on each one.
  • A concept test suggests that about half of the Everyday jars sold would replace a regular Casa Verde jar the shopper would otherwise have bought.

Your task: "Should we launch Everyday?" You have five minutes. Walk through your clarifying questions, your structure, the one number that decides the case and your recommendation.

Your earlier answer

  1. Hint 1 of 3 Start with the goal, not the math. What is the team trying to win back, from whom, and at which retailers? Write one clarifying question first.
  2. Hint 2 of 3 Every Everyday jar earns less than a regular jar. What happens to profit when someone who would have bought a regular jar buys Everyday instead?
  3. Hint 3 of 3 Work out how many Everyday jars can come from regular-jar buyers before the launch loses money: compare Everyday's margin with the regular jar's. Then hold that number up against the concept test.

A strong five-minute answer

1. Clarify (30 seconds). "What's the goal here: winning back share, protecting profit, or protecting shelf space at those two chains? Over what time frame? Is the share loss everywhere, or mostly at those two retailers?"

2. Start with the shopper (1 minute). Who's switching, and why? If they're leaving because money is tight, a cheaper Casa Verde could win them back. If they're leaving because they can't taste a difference, a cheaper version of the same promise won't fix it, and the real problem is that the premium jar isn't earning its price.

3. Run the number that decides it (1 minute). The general formula: breakeven cannibalization = the new item's margin ÷ the old item's margin. Here, $0.63 ÷ $1.40 = 45%. Why it works: each Everyday jar earns $0.63, but every one that replaces a regular jar also costs you that jar's $1.40. You break even when $0.63 = (share of jars that replace a regular one) × $1.40. If more than 45% of Everyday jars sold replace a regular jar, the launch loses money.

The concept test says about 50%. For every 100 Everyday jars sold, the brand earns $63, but gives up 50 regular jars worth $70. That's $7 lost per 100 jars, before paying for the launch itself.

4. Check the competition and the retailer (1 minute). The retailer makes money on its own store brand, so a buyer may not want a third pasta sauce sitting between the store brand and the regular Casa Verde. Expect the buyer to ask what Everyday does for the category, not just for you.

5. Sketch the 4Ps if you'd go ahead (30 seconds). A recipe and label clearly different from the regular jar, a price near the store brand, shelf placement next to the store brand rather than next to the regular jar, and light support aimed at shoppers in store.

6. Recommend, with a number (1 minute). "As designed, I wouldn't launch it nationally. At 50% cannibalization against a 45% breakeven, it loses money on every jar. I'd look at two fixes: raise Everyday's margin, or make it different enough from the regular jar that fewer regular buyers trade down. Then I'd test it at the two chains where we're losing the most, and launch more widely only if fewer than 45% of its sales come out of the regular jar."

Why this answer works

  • It starts with the shopper, not a framework. The "why are they switching?" question changes everything after it.
  • It brings one number that decides the case. Breakeven cannibalization turns "it might cannibalize" into a clear yes-or-no test.
  • It ends with a recommendation and a test, not "it depends."

What interviewers push on

  • "What if those shoppers were leaving for the store brand anyway?" Then those sales aren't really cannibalized. Cannibalization means sales the regular jar would have kept without Everyday. If 25 of those 50 regular jars would have gone to the store brand regardless, only 25 are truly lost, and Everyday makes money. Always compare against what happens if you do nothing, not against today.
  • "Why not just cut the price of the regular jar?" Run the math. A price cut shrinks the margin on every jar you already sell, including to loyal buyers who'd pay full price. Everyday only gives up margin on the buyers who switch.
  • "How much would you trust the concept test?" Not fully. People often say they'll buy things they don't. Treat the 50% as a starting point, then measure the real number in a market test.

Practice twist

The operations team finds a cheaper jar, and Everyday's margin rises to $0.84. What's the new breakeven, and does your recommendation change?

Answer: $0.84 ÷ $1.40 = 60%. With the concept test at 50%, Everyday now earns $84 per 100 jars and gives up $70, so it makes money. You'd still test first, because the 50% is only an estimate.
