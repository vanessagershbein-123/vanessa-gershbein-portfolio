Jobs to be done and win/loss interviews

PMMLesson 2.311 min

Goal: Find the job customers hire your product to do, and learn the real reasons deals are won and lost.

Skills: Research & insight

Ask a customer why they bought something and you'll get a neat, sensible answer. It's often not the real one. People aren't lying. They just tidy up the story after the fact and leave out the months of low-grade frustration, or the worry that almost stopped them.

Segments and personas tell you who buys. They can't tell you why someone switched from what they used before, or why a deal you expected to win went to a rival or to nobody at all. Two kinds of interview can: jobs-to-be-done interviews with people who recently switched, and win/loss interviews with buyers who just made a decision. Both work only if you ask about what actually happened, not what people think they'd do.

What are they hiring it to do?

Ask people why they buy a milkshake at a drive-through and they'll talk about flavor. The Clayton Christensen Institute uses that example to show what the flavor answer misses: morning buyers were really hiring the milkshake to stay full, make a dull drive more interesting and eat with one hand, so its competition was bananas and bagels, not other milkshakes. Its software example is a tax company that built a powerful platform for maximizing refunds, but the extra complexity meant customers spent more time on their taxes. For many of them the real job was spending less time on taxes, and simplifying the product increased sales1.

That's the idea behind jobs to be done. The institute describes it as a lens on the circumstances that push people toward or away from a decision. A job is the progress someone is trying to make in a particular situation, and people "hire" a product when that job comes up. Every job has functional, social and emotional sides, which is why it explains choices that demographics and feature lists miss1.

Why should you care as a PMM? Because the job does two things for you:

  • It names the real competitor. If the job is "get my taxes off my plate," the rival may be an accountant or a deadline extension, not another app. It's the competition, seen from the customer's side.
  • It hands you the message. "Done in under an hour" speaks to that job. "Maximize your refund" speaks to a different one.

Rewind to the moment they started looking

A man buys a new mattress at Costco and calls it an impulse buy. Bob Moesta's firm, the Re-Wired Group, uses his interview to show why you shouldn't take that at face value. Probing showed he'd been thinking about replacing the mattress for more than a year, and that Costco's return policy and the absence of high-pressure salespeople mattered more to the decision than any feature2.

That's what a switch interview is for. It walks someone through a recent purchase, back to the first moment they noticed a problem.

The same approach sorts what drives or blocks a switch into four forces. Progress happens when the push of the current situation and the pull of the new solution outweigh anxiety about the new and the habit of the present2. Say a team runs its projects in spreadsheets and is weighing a project-management tool. Here's how the four forces might look:

Force Direction Example What the PMM can do
Push Toward switching Missed deadlines because nobody sees the whole plan Name the pain plainly in the message
Pull Toward switching One view of every project Show the result in the first session
Anxiety Against switching "Will the team actually use it? What about our data?" Import help, a free trial, a customer like them
Habit Against switching Everyone already knows the spreadsheet Make the first week feel familiar, such as importing the sheet as is

Look at the bottom two rows. Most marketing pushes harder on pull, with more features and bigger promises. Many deals are won by lowering anxiety instead.

Ask about the past, not the future

Ask someone "would you use...?" and they'll happily answer. The answer just isn't worth much. Nielsen Norman Group warns that people are poor at predicting their own future behavior, so hypothetical questions give unreliable answers. Ask about specific past experiences instead, and avoid wording that hints at the answer you expect3.

How many interviews should you run? Keep going until new conversations stop teaching you anything. A narrow group needs fewer than a broad one.

A few switch-interview questions that follow those rules:

  • "When did you first think about changing how you did this?"
  • "What were you using before, and what was going wrong?"
  • "What almost stopped you from switching?"
  • "Walk me through the day you decided."

Win/loss: the reason behind the reason

Every lost deal ends up with a reason in the CRM, and it's usually a word or two long. Win/loss interviews go back to the buyers and ask why they chose you, or didn't.

Pragmatic Institute's advice is specific. Interview both won and lost buyers two to four weeks after the decision. Have someone outside sales run the interviews, because buyers speak more freely to a non-seller. Review at least ten deals before calling something a pattern4. Its best-practice guide adds that reps often blame price, product or timing, assumptions that only conversations with the buyers themselves can test4. Forrester makes the same point about CRM close reasons like "budget" or "pricing": they're shallow, and sellers' reading of a loss often differs from the buyer's real reason5.

Here's what that short reason often hides:

What the CRM says What the interview often finds
"Price was too high" The buyer didn't see enough value for the price, or the champion couldn't make the case internally
"Went with the incumbent" Anxiety about switching outweighed the pull of something new
"Bad timing" Nobody owned the problem, so the group never decided

Common traps

  • Asking "why did you buy?" People give tidy reasons after the fact. Ask what happened, in order.
  • Letting sales run win/loss. Buyers soften the truth for the person who sold to them.
  • Interviewing only wins. Wins tell you what worked. Losses tell you what to fix.
  • Treating one vivid story as a pattern. Wait until the same theme shows up across several interviews.

In an interview

If you're asked how you'd find out why a company is losing deals, describe a win/loss program: who you'd interview (won and lost buyers), when (within weeks of the decision), who asks (not the rep), and how many deals you'd review before trusting a pattern.

Self-check: Your CRM says 60% of lost deals were lost on price. What do you do before recommending a discount?

Interview a set of lost buyers within a few weeks of their decision, with someone outside sales asking the questions. Have them walk through the decision step by step instead of asking why they said no. "Price" often stands in for value the buyer couldn't see or a risk they didn't want to take, and some of those deals may have been lost to doing nothing rather than to a rival. A discount fixes none of that.

Check your understanding

3 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. A project-management tool sees a spike in sign-ups from small agencies in the week they land their biggest client yet. In jobs-to-be-done terms, what's the job?

    Show answer

    Stay organized and look credible while taking on a bigger client than before. A job is the progress someone is trying to make in a particular situation, with functional, social and emotional sides. Tasks and Gantt charts are features, and "10-person agency" is a demographic.

  2. Which question is best for a switch interview?

    Show answer

    "Walk me through the day you decided to look for something new.". Questions about specific past events give reliable answers. Hypotheticals and "why" questions invite guesses and tidy explanations.

  3. Your VP wants each rep to call the buyers from their own lost deals and ask why they chose a rival. What's the main risk?

    Show answer

    Buyers are less candid with the person who sold to them, so you'll mostly hear polite reasons like price. Buyers speak more freely to someone who didn't sell to them, and the reasons reps record are often too shallow to act on. Someone outside sales gets closer to the real reason.
