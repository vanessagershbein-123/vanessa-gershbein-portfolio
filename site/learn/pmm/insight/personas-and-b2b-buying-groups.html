Personas and B2B buying groups

PMMLesson 2.210 min

Goal: Build personas from research instead of guesses, and map who in a real B2B deal signs, uses, influences and moves it forward.

Skills: Research & insight

Every B2BBusiness-to-business: selling to companies rather than to consumers. deal has a cast. Someone signs. Several people will use the product. An IT lead can stop the whole thing, and someone in finance is usually asking what it costs. Most teams sketch that cast from memory in an afternoon and call it a persona. The teams that win go find out who's really involved and what each of those people needs to hear.

How far can you trust a persona?

Write for "everyone" and nobody recognizes themselves in what you wrote. A persona fixes that by turning a group into one concrete person: a short, realistic profile of a typical customer, so the team designs and writes for someone specific. The catch is that a persona is only as good as the research behind it. Nielsen Norman Group sorts personas into three kinds on exactly that basis1:

Type Built from Good for Watch out for
Proto-persona The team's assumptions and data already on hand, usually in a two- to four-hour workshop Getting a team aligned fast It can be wrong, and a weak one can sour people on personas
Qualitative persona About 5 to 30 interviews or field studies, coded into themes Most PMM work It can't tell you what share of buyers each persona represents
Statistical persona Qualitative work, then a survey of at least 100 people (ideally 500 or more), then clustering Sizing segments It's costly and needs statistical help

Whichever kind you build, watch what goes into it. That same NN/g article advises against personas built only from demographics or analytics. Those details leave out what people are trying to do and why. "38, lives in Denver, likes hiking" might feel vivid, but it tells sales nothing about how to win the deal.

The person who pays isn't the person who uses

Take a VP of sales who signs for a new CRM, and the reps who'll have to log every call in it. The VP wants forecasts she can trust. The reps want fewer clicks between calls. One persona can't speak to both, because they're judging the product on different things.

In B2B, the person who pays and the person who uses are often different people, so each gets a persona of their own. Pragmatic Institute describes buyer personas as profiles of the people who make or influence the purchase, such as sponsors, approvers and influencers, capturing their goals, concerns, authority and success criteria2. User personas profile the people who use the product, capturing their workflows, needs, context and behavior. Pragmatic treats the two as separate kinds of persona2. That matters because a buyer's approval doesn't guarantee that users adopt the product.

Buyer persona User persona
Who Sponsors, approvers, influencers The people who use the product every day
Captures Goals, concerns, authority, success criteria Workflows, needs, context, behavior
What the PMM uses it for The business case, sales messaging, pricing Onboarding, product messaging, adoption campaigns

The last row is one reading of how a PMM puts each persona to work.

Map the buying group from your own deals

A generic list of roles gets you started. The buying group that matters, though, is the one that actually shows up in your deals, and you can map it from records you already have:

  1. Pull 10 to 20 recent deals, won and lost. List everyone who appeared: who joined calls, who signed, who asked for a security review, who went quiet.
  2. Interview a few of them, especially from lost deals, because they can tell you where things stalled.
  3. Group people by role, not title: sponsor, approver, influencer, user and possible blocker. The same title can play different roles at different companies.
  4. For each role, write down three things: the worry, the question they'll ask, and the proof that answers it.
  5. Find the person who can move the group.

That last step matters more than it looks. You might assume a big deal stalls because one stakeholder says no. In The Challenger Customer, built on CEB research with thousands of B2B marketers, sellers and buyers, Brent Adamson, Matthew Dixon, Pat Spenner and Nick Toman argue that's rarely the reason. Deals stall because the stakeholders can't agree on what the problem is. The people who get deals done are what the authors call "Mobilizers," who have the credibility and the will to push colleagues past the status quo. Deals reach the finish line far more often with them than with friendlier "Talkers" who seem so receptive at first3.

That changes who you build for. The contact who always takes your call isn't necessarily your champion. Your champion is whoever can make the case inside the company when you're not in the room, so your job is to arm them with a business case, answers for the skeptics and a short pitch they can forward.

Worked example: a persona card for the security reviewer

Say you sell a security product, and your deal reviews keep turning up the same person: the security lead who has to sign off before procurement will move. Here's what a research-based buyer persona for that person might hold. Every field ties back to the decision:

Field Example
Role in the decision Influencer who can block: signs off on security before procurement moves
Goals No new risk to company data, and fewer tools to monitor
Concerns Where data is stored, who can access it, how the vendor handles incidents
Success criteria Passes the security review without exceptions, and fits the current tools
Proof they need A security overview, certifications, a reference from a similar company
Where they learn Peers, analyst reports, the vendor's security documentation

Notice what's missing: no age, no hobbies, no stock photo. A good rule: keep only the details that change what you design or say.

Common traps

  • Building personas from demographics alone. They describe people without explaining what they're trying to do.
  • One persona for the buyer and the user. The CFO who approves and the analyst who logs in every day want different things.
  • Treating the friendliest contact as the champion. Ask whether they can actually move the rest of the group.
  • Making personas nobody uses. Build them with sales and product, and put them where those teams already work.

In an interview

If you're asked "who's the customer?", don't give one answer. Name the buyer and the user separately, say how you'd confirm each one (deal reviews and interviews, not guesses), and point out who in the group could block the deal.

Self-check: Your team's only persona is "Marketing Mary, 35, loves yoga." A sales VP says it isn't helping close deals. What's wrong, and what do you build instead?

It's a proto-persona built on demographics, and nothing on it changes how a deal gets won. Build a buyer persona from interviews in recent won and lost deals: the person's role in the decision, their goals, concerns and success criteria, and the proof they need. If the people who use the product are different, give them their own user persona.

Check your understanding

3 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. Your team has one afternoon to agree on who it's selling to, and no time for new research. What kind of persona can you build, and what's the catch?

    Show answer

    A proto-persona from the team's assumptions, which may be wrong until interviews test it. A proto-persona comes from a short workshop using what the team already believes and knows. It aligns people fast, but treat it as a guess until interviews back it up. Qualitative personas come from interviews, and statistical ones from large surveys.

  2. A CFO approves your product, but finance analysts use it every day. What's the minimum set of personas you need?

    Show answer

    A buyer persona for the CFO and a user persona for the analysts. Approval doesn't guarantee adoption, so the person who buys and the people who use need separate personas.

  3. An enterprise deal has stalled. Which contact is most worth investing in?

    Show answer

    The stakeholder with the credibility to get colleagues to agree on the problem, even if they're skeptical of you. Big deals stall when stakeholders can't agree on the problem. The person who can move the group matters more than the person who's easy to talk to.
