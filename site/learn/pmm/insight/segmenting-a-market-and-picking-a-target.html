Segmenting a market and picking a target

PMMLesson 2.111 min

Goal: Divide a market in a way that predicts what people buy, then pick one segment to win first.

Skills: Research & insightPositioning & strategy

A sales team gets handed the target "mid-size companies." Where do they start? Which industry, which city, which problem do they open with? Nobody knows, so each rep picks their own, and the message ends up saying a little to everyone and a lot to no one.

Every later choice you make as a PMM, from positioning to pricing, depends on who the product is for. You can't build, position and sell a product to everyone at once, so the first job is to split the market into groups that behave alike and pick the one you'll win first. Get this wrong and even sharp positioning ends up aimed at the wrong people.

Where to draw the lines

Consumer markets usually get split by where people live, who they are, how they buy and what they value. Business markets have their own set of cuts, which OpenStax's Principles of Marketing lays out1. Each row is a different way of looking at the same pool of companies:

Basis What it divides by A software example
Firmographics Industry, location, size, structure, performance Hospitals with more than 500 beds
Technographics The hardware and software a company already uses Companies already running a particular CRM
Needs-based The features or benefits a customer wants Teams that need audit trails for compliance
Value-based How much a customer could be worth, often called tiering Accounts likely to spend $100,000 a year or more
Behavioral How a customer engages with the company Free users who invited three or more teammates

Who they are vs. why they'd buy

Look closely at those five and you'll see two kinds. Firmographics and technographics describe who a customer is. A needs-based cut gets much closer to why they'd buy. The argument for looking past the who isn't new. Daniel Yankelovich made the case in Harvard Business Review back in 1964 for segmenting by more than demographics, and in 2006 he and David Meer argued that segmentation had narrowed into a tool for serving advertising2.

You need both halves. For a tech PMM, the segment worth chasing usually pairs them:

  • A descriptive cut you can find and reach, such as industry, company size or the tools a team already uses. It's what sales builds a list from.
  • A need that explains why they'd buy, such as a compliance deadline or hours lost to manual work. It's what your message speaks to.

Compare the two. "Mid-size accounting firms" is a list. "Mid-size accounting firms losing billable hours to manual data entry" is a segment you can sell to.

Choosing where to start

Once you've sliced the market, you still have to decide how many slices to go after. OpenStax describes four ways to answer that3:

  • Undifferentiated: one offer for everyone.
  • Differentiated: a separate plan for each of several segments.
  • Concentrated, or niche: one segment, with the aim of dominating it. It suits companies with limited resources.
  • Micromarketing: tailoring to individuals or very small groups.

If you're a startup launching something new, you'll usually land on the third one. A startup can't afford to fight on several fronts at once. Geoffrey Moore's Crossing the Chasm explains why focus pays off in tech: mainstream buyers want proof that a product works for companies like theirs, so a company should pick one segment and one demanding use case, win it decisively, and become the leader in that niche4. Marketers often call that first segment the beachhead.

To choose between candidates, score each one on the same six questions, so you're comparing segments instead of defending a favorite:

Question What a good answer looks like
Is it big enough? Enough customers to build a business on, with room to grow
Is the pain urgent? They're losing money or time now, not someday
Can you reach them? A list, a channel or a community where they gather
Can you win? Your difference matters more here than anywhere else
Do they talk to each other? Word of mouth makes the next customer cheaper
Where does it lead? Nearby segments you can win next

Worked example: Toast bets on one industry

Toast built its point-of-sale and restaurant software for exactly one kind of customer. Its 2021 IPO filing described the platform as purpose-built for restaurants and the company as "exclusively focused on the restaurant industry"5.

You might expect a focus that tight to cap growth. It didn't. Toast went from 19,891 restaurant locations in June 2019 to 47,942 in June 2021, across about 29,000 customers, and still had only about 6% of roughly 860,000 US restaurant locations. Its plan for growing further was to sell more products to the same restaurants, add financial services, expand internationally and win larger restaurant groups. Both acquisitions it named, for payroll and for invoicing, were built for restaurants too5.

Three things to take from it:

  • A narrow segment can still be huge. Restaurants were specific enough to speak to directly and large enough that 6% share left plenty of room.
  • Go deeper before going wider. Toast grew by selling more to restaurants before reaching for other industries.
  • Specific needs make the message easy. Kitchen tickets, tips and menus are restaurant problems a general-purpose system handles poorly, which gave Toast a clear difference to talk about.

Common traps

  • A segment you can't reach. If there's no list, channel or community, you can't sell to it, however good the fit.
  • Demographic segments with no buying difference. If two groups would buy for the same reason, they're really one segment.
  • Picking the biggest segment instead of the most winnable one. A huge market where you have no edge loses to a smaller one where you do.
  • Never choosing. Going after several segments at once spreads a small team thin before it wins any of them.

In an interview

When you're asked who you'd target first, don't hedge. Name one segment, give two or three reasons from the scoring questions, and say what you'd turn down for now. The no matters as much as the yes, because it shows you understand the trade-off.

Self-check: A startup selling expense software says its target is "small and medium businesses." What's wrong, and how would you narrow it?

That's a size band, not a segment. It covers millions of companies with different needs and no shared channel. Narrow it by pairing something you can reach with a need: for example, agencies that bill client expenses back to their clients, which you can reach through agency networks and the accounting tools they already use. Then run it through the scoring questions before you commit.

Check your understanding

3 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. You sell an add-on that only works inside Salesforce. Which segmentation basis finds your best prospects fastest?

    Show answer

    Technographics, starting with companies already running Salesforce. When your product depends on a tool, the software a company already runs is the first filter. Industry, size and spend can narrow the list after that.

  2. A startup with a small team is launching its first product. Which targeting strategy usually fits best?

    Show answer

    Concentrated, winning one segment first. A concentrated strategy suits limited resources. Win one segment decisively, then expand to the next.

  3. Which of these is the most useful segment definition?

    Show answer

    Mid-size accounting firms losing billable hours to manual data entry. It pairs a group sales can find with a need the message can speak to. The others describe people without explaining why they'd buy.
