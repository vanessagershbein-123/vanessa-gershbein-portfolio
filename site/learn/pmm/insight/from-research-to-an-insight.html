From research to an insight

PMMLesson 2.410 min

Goal: Turn interview notes and data into a few clear insights that change what a team builds or says.

Skills: Research & insight

You've done the interviews. Now you're staring at pages of notes, a few win/loss summaries, maybe a survey export and a handful of quotes you love. None of it helps until it becomes a few statements a team can act on. This is where research most often stalls: a deck of quotes and charts that everyone finds interesting and nobody uses. The skill is getting from "we talked to customers" to "so here's what we'll change."

From one quote to a decision

Take a single line from an interview: "I spent the first week just figuring out which columns to import." On its own, it's an anecdote. Your job is to work out whether it's part of something bigger, and what to do if it is.

Nielsen Norman Group's guide to thematic analysis separates three layers. Observations are the raw data, such as a quote or something you watched a person do. Codes are labels you attach to pieces of that data. Themes are patterns that recur across several participants or sources. The guide's six steps: gather the data, read all of it, code it (plain descriptive labels first), add interpretive codes, step away for a while, then evaluate the themes1.

An insight goes one step past a theme, adding why the pattern happens and what it means for your product or message. Follow that one quote all the way through:

Layer Example
Observation "I spent the first week just figuring out which columns to import."
Theme New admins get stuck importing their existing data
Insight New admins stall at import because they don't know how their old fields map to ours, and many trials end there
Action Lead onboarding with an import template, and say "bring your spreadsheet as is" in the trial message

The whole table is an illustration, not real research.

Sort first, name later

An affinity diagram is the hands-on version. NN/g's method runs like this. Each person writes notes on their own for 5 to 10 minutes. The group sorts the notes by content first and names each cluster only afterward. Small clusters stay, because they can reflect a distinct view. A note that fits two clusters gets duplicated instead of forced into one. Then the team ranks the clusters, using dot voting if it can't agree2.

Naming last can feel backward, which is exactly why it works. Name the clusters first and you'll sort every note into the categories you already believed in.

Write it in one sentence

A fill-in-the-blanks sentence keeps insights honest, because it won't let you skip the why or the action:

[Who] does or believes [what] because [why], so we should [action].

Before sharing one, check it against three questions:

  • Is it backed by more than one source? A pattern across interviews, or interviews plus data, beats one memorable quote.
  • Does it change what the team would do? If it changes no decision, it's a reminder, not an insight.
  • Does it point to an action? If nothing would change, keep digging.

Watch the gap between saying and doing

In a 2025 study by the research group METR, experienced developers using early-2025 AI tools believed AI tools had made them about 20% faster, while their tasks actually took 19% longer3. What they felt and what happened had simply come apart.

That's why you want more than one kind of evidence. Interviews and surveys capture what people say. Usage data and experiments capture what they do. The two often disagree, and that gap is usually where the insight is.

Share it so it gets used

Research readouts often run in the order the work happened: method first, findings next, recommendations at the very end. Flip that. Lead with the insight, then the evidence, then the action, and keep it to the three or four insights that matter most. A team will act on three clear insights. Thirty findings get filed away.

Common traps

  • Summarizing instead of interpreting. "Users mentioned onboarding" is a topic, not an insight.
  • Treating one vivid quote as a pattern. Check that the theme shows up across several people.
  • Naming the clusters first. You'll find what you expected to find.
  • Skipping the "so what." An insight without an action is trivia.

In an interview

When a case hands you research or data, the temptation is to recite it. Don't. Give one insight, the two pieces of evidence behind it, and what you'd do about it.

Self-check: Your research summary says "Users find setup confusing." Is that an insight? How would you make it one?

It's a theme. To turn it into an insight, add who exactly, why it happens and what should follow. For example: "First-time admins at small companies abandon setup at the permissions step because they don't know which roles they need, so we should offer three preset role templates and mention them in the trial email." Then check that more than one source backs it.

Check your understanding

3 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. Which of these is a theme rather than an observation?

    Show answer

    Several admins describe getting stuck at the import step. Observations are raw data like one quote or one session. A theme is a pattern that recurs across several people or sources.

  2. Before anyone sorts their interview notes, a PM writes five labels on the wall: Pricing, Onboarding, Integrations, Support and Performance. What's the risk?

    Show answer

    Notes get forced into categories the team already believed in, and new patterns get missed. NN/g's method sorts notes by content first and names clusters afterward. Name them first and the exercise turns into confirming what you already thought.

  3. What turns a theme into an insight?

    Show answer

    Explaining why it happens and what the team should do about it. An insight adds the why and the so-what. Without an action, it's a finding.
