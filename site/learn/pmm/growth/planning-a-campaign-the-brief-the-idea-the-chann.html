Planning a campaign: the brief, the idea, the channels, the measure

PMMLesson 7.312 min

Goal: Write a brief people can act on, balance long-term and short-term spending, and measure what a campaign actually caused.

Skills: Writing & storytellingPositioning & strategy

eBay used to buy search ads on its own name, so people searching for eBay saw a paid eBay ad as well as the normal result. Then economists Thomas Blake, Chris Nosko and Steven Tadelis ran large field experiments at the company to see what those ads actually did. The brand-keyword ads had no measurable short-term benefit. For other keywords, ads helped mainly new and infrequent shoppers, but most of the spending went to frequent shoppers who'd have bought anyway, so average returns were negative1.

The ads had looked great in the dashboards, because people who click on search ads are often already planning to buy. A campaign has four parts, the brief, the idea, the channels and the measure, and it's the last one that tells you whether the other three worked.

The brief: one page someone can act on

Briefs are where most campaigns go wrong first. In a 2021 IPA survey of more than 1,700 marketers and agency staff in over 70 countries, 80% of marketers said they write good briefs, but only 10% of agencies agreed. Respondents estimated that a third of marketing budgets is wasted on poor briefs and misdirected work2.

A good brief fits on a page and answers six questions:

Question Weak answer Strong answer
What's the business problem? "Raise awareness" "Trial sign-ups from small agencies fell 15% this year"
Who's it for? "Everyone" "Owners of 5- to 20-person agencies"
What do they think now? Left blank "They think we're built for big companies"
What's the one thing to say? Five messages One line from the positioning
What must it include? Every logo and feature Only real requirements
How will we know it worked? "Engagement" One number, with a date and a baseline

The idea: clever isn't the same as right

An idea can be brilliant to the people who made it and still land badly with the people it's about. In May 2024, Apple released an iPad Pro ad showing a piano, a camera, paint cans and other creative tools being flattened by an industrial crusher. Creatives hated it3. Apple's vice president of marketing, Tor Myhren, apologized: "We missed the mark with this video, and we're sorry"4.

The point of the ad, that one thin device can do the work of a studio, was a fine product message. The image chosen to express it told artists their tools, and maybe their craft, didn't matter. Test the idea with the audience it depicts, not just the team that loves it.

The channels: long-term and short-term

Les Binet and Peter Field, in research published by the IPA, found that the best results come from balancing two kinds of marketing: brand building, which shapes what people think of you over time, and sales activation, which prompts people to act now. In their landmark study, the best split on average was 60:40 in favor of brand building, and a later update found 62:385.

B2BBusiness-to-business: selling to companies rather than to consumers. is different, but not as different as many B2B marketers assume. Their B2B research recommends roughly a 50/50 split, and finds that strategies for winning new customers tend to be much more effective than loyalty strategies6. That's a finding about where marketing budget pays off. It doesn't change the product rule: a product still has to keep the users it wins. The reason acquisition matters so much in B2B is timing. John Dawes of the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute points out that companies switch providers like their bank or law firm about once every five years, so only around 5% of business buyers are in the market in any quarter7. Brand building reaches the other 95% before they start shopping.

The measure: what did the campaign cause?

Start with the standard scorecard. OpenStax's Principles of Marketing lists the usual ways to measure an ad campaign: brand recognition and awareness, impressions, conversion rateThe share of visitors or prospects who take an action, such as placing an order. (the share of an audience that takes the action you wanted) and return on ad spend, which divides the revenue from a period by the ad dollars spent in it8. Those numbers tell you what happened while the campaign ran. They don't tell you what the campaign caused, and that's the harder question.

The pandemic handed Airbnb a natural experiment. When it filed to go public in 2020, it said about 23% of its traffic in 2019 had come from paid performance marketing, including search ads on the word "Airbnb." During the pandemic it suspended nearly all performance marketing, and in the first nine months of 2020 about 91% of its traffic came through direct or unpaid channels. Its rivals were also bidding on its brand name in search. Its stated strategy afterward: more brand marketing, less performance marketing9. The paid share of traffic was bound to fall once the ads stopped, so that number alone doesn't prove the ads added nothing, but it gave Airbnb reason to rethink the mix. A 2026 Kellogg case puts you in its marketing VP's seat in December 2020, deciding whether $5 million should go to trackable performance ads or to brand building on YouTube10.

The fix is to measure what a campaign added, not just what happened after it. The cleanest way is a holdout: show the campaign to a random group, hold it back from a similar group, and compare. Shortcuts don't hold up well. In a study of 15 advertising experiments at Facebook, covering 1.6 billion ad impressions, Brett Gordon and colleagues found that the usual observational methods often failed to match what the randomized tests showed11.

Common traps

  • A brief with five messages. The work will say none of them clearly.
  • Testing the idea only on fans. Ask the people the ad is about.
  • All activation, no brand. It looks efficient until the pool of people who already know you runs dry.
  • Reading credit as cause. Last-click attribution rewards ads shown to people who'd have bought anyway.

In an interview

For "Plan a campaign for X," move through the four parts in order: the business problem and the one number you'll move, the audience and the single message, the idea and why it fits them, the channels and how you'd split long-term and short-term spend, and the holdout test that will prove it worked.

Self-check: Your paid search dashboard shows a 9x return on ads for your own brand name. Your boss wants to triple the budget. What would you propose first?

A holdout test. Turn the brand ads off for a random set of regions or weeks and compare sales with the rest. eBay found its brand-keyword ads had no measurable short-term benefit, because many people searching for eBay by name were going to buy anyway. Results can differ when rivals bid on your name, which Airbnb said its competitors did. If sales barely move without the ads, that budget can go somewhere it actually adds customers.

Check your understanding

4 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. You want to know whether ads on your own brand name are worth it. What's the strongest test?

    Show answer

    Turn them off for a random set of regions or users and compare with the rest. eBay's experiments found brand-keyword ads had no measurable short-term benefit. Clicks happen anyway when people already plan to buy, so only a holdout shows what the ads added.

  2. A brief says "make the product look exciting to everyone." What's the most important fix?

    Show answer

    Name one audience, one thing to say and one number the campaign should move. Marketers and agencies disagree sharply on whether briefs are clear. A specific audience, message and goal leave much less to guess.

  3. A B2B company puts its whole budget into retargeting visitors to its pricing page. What's the risk?

    Show answer

    It ignores the roughly 95% of future buyers who aren't shopping yet. Only about 5% of business buyers are in the market in a given quarter. Brand building reaches the rest before they start shopping.

  4. A launch ad for a creative tool shows traditional instruments and paints being destroyed. What should you check before it runs?

    Show answer

    How the people the ad depicts will feel about seeing their tools destroyed. Apple's iPad Pro "Crush" ad had a sound product message, but creatives read it as dismissing their craft, and Apple apologized.
