Growth loops and channels that compound

PMMLesson 7.212 min

Goal: Tell a growth loop from a one-way funnel, and pick the loop and channels that fit your product.

Skills: Positioning & strategy

Wise, the money-transfer company, counted 19 million active customers in its 2026 fiscal year, up more than 21%. Its shareholder letter credits word-of-mouth recommendations for about 70% of new customers. Wise still raised its marketing spending 62% that year, adding brand campaigns on top of its existing channels1.

When seven in ten new customers arrive because somebody told them to, growth works differently. Customers aren't just the output of marketing. They're the engine.

Funnels end, loops feed themselves

A funnel runs one way: you spend money, people flow through, and when you stop spending, the flow stops. A loop feeds its own output back in, so each round sets up the next.

Andrew Chen, author of The Cold Start Problem, argues there are only a few ways to scale user growth: paid acquisition, virality, search traffic from user-created content, and sales. They all work for the same reason. They're feedback loops: revenue from customers buys more customers, or a group of new users invites even more users2.

The common loops look like this:

Loop How it feeds itself Fits when
Viral or referral Users invite users The product is better with other people, or worth recommending
Content User activity creates pages people find through search Users make public content others search for
Paid Revenue funds ads that bring more paying customers A customer is worth more than it costs to acquire one
Sales Revenue funds reps who close more revenue Deals are big enough to pay for a salesperson

What the research says about viral loops

Sinan Aral and Dylan Walker ran a randomized experiment on Facebook with the 1.4 million friends of 9,687 users. Automatic "passive broadcast" notifications raised peer influence by 246%, while adding personal invites raised it only another 98%. Personal invites worked better per message and came with more engagement, but broadcasts went out far more often, so they drove more total adoption3.

That's a design choice, not just a marketing one. Small product decisions about what gets shared, and how, decide how strong a viral loop can be.

Referrals bring better customers

Paying for referrals can sound like bribery. A study by Philipp Schmitt, Bernd Skiera and Christophe Van den Bulte suggests it's often a good deal. Looking at a German bank that paid customers 25 euros per referral, they found referred customers were about 18% more likely to stay, and worth 16% to 25% more over the long run. That worked out to roughly a 60% return over six years on the 25 euros4.

A community loop: LEGO Ideas

LEGO runs one of the clearest community loops in consumer products. Fans submit their own set designs, and any idea that wins 10,000 supporters is considered for production as an official set, with its creator earning 1% of net sales. One designer's promo video went viral and helped push a cat-themed set past 10,000 votes5. Every hopeful designer becomes a recruiter, bringing new fans to LEGO Ideas to vote.

A paid loop, and its limits

Booking Holdings, the company behind Booking.com, spent $8.2 billion on marketing in 2025, up 12.5%. Most of it was performance marketing, led by search ads on Google. Booking judges each channel partly by the incremental bookings it brings and how often those customers come back, and it notes that its marketing gets more efficient as more customers book directly. In 2025, people coming straight to its platforms accounted for a mid-fifties percentage of room nights6.

A paid loop rents attention. It keeps working only while each customer is worth more than they cost, and it gets cheaper as more of those customers come back on their own.

Common traps

  • Forcing a viral loop onto a solo product. If nobody benefits from inviting a friend, an invite button won't create a loop.
  • Counting the first round. A loop only matters if each round brings in enough people for the next.
  • Renting forever. Paid growth that never turns into direct, repeat visits often gets more expensive over time.
  • Copying another company's loop. LEGO's works because fans love designing sets. Yours has to fit what your users already want to do.

In an interview

For "How would you grow this product?", ask what users do that could bring in the next user, and sketch that loop step by step. Then name the step that's weakest and how you'd measure it, such as invites per new user or the share of content that ranks in search.

Self-check: A project-management tool is mostly used by solo freelancers. The team wants a referral program like a social app's. What would you check first?

Whether users have a reason to bring anyone in. Solo freelancers rarely need collaborators inside the tool, so invites may go nowhere. Look for a natural sharing moment instead, such as sending a client a project link, and build the loop around that: every client who sees a polished project page is a potential new user.

Check your understanding

4 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. Which product is best suited to a content loop?

    Show answer

    A recipe site where users' posts become pages people find through search. Content loops work when user activity creates public, searchable content that brings in the next visitor.

  2. Your app lets users send a personal invite to one friend at a time. A designer proposes an automatic "your friend just joined" notice. Based on Aral and Walker's experiment, what's the likely trade-off?

    Show answer

    Automatic notices spread further in total, while personal invites convert better per message. Passive broadcasts raised peer influence 246%, and adding personal invites raised it another 98%. Personal messages worked better one at a time, but broadcasts went out far more often.

  3. A bank's CFO asks whether paying 25 euros per referral is worth it. What does the research suggest?

    Show answer

    Yes, often: referred customers tended to stay longer and be worth more. In the German bank study, referred customers were about 18% more likely to stay and worth 16% to 25% more over the long run.

  4. Booking spends billions on paid search, yet more than half its room nights come from people booking directly. Why does that direct share matter?

    Show answer

    Customers who come back on their own don't have to be paid for again, so marketing gets more efficient. A paid loop rents attention. Booking notes its marketing efficiency depends on how many customers book directly.
