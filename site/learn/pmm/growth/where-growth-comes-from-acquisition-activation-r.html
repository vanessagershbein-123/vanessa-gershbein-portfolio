Where growth comes from: acquisition, activation, retention, referral

PMMLesson 7.112 min

Goal: Break growth into stages, find the stage that's actually holding a product back, and see why retention usually comes first.

Skills: Data & chartsBusiness math

When Snowflake filed to go public in 2020, one number stood out. Its net revenue retentionNet revenue retention: what a group of existing customers pays now compared with a year ago, after upgrades, downgrades and cancellations. Above 100% means growth from existing customers outweighs losses. rate was 158%: customers who'd been around for a year were spending 58% more than they had the year before. The number of customers paying Snowflake more than $1 million a year had jumped from 22 to 56 in twelve months1. As Snowflake grew, the rate came down, to 126% by mid-2026, but existing customers were still spending more each year1.

Most people picture growth as new customers. Snowflake's filing shows another source: customers already in the door, using more. Growth comes from several places, and the first job is knowing which one is stuck.

Five stages, one funnel

In 2007, investor Dave McClure gave growth teams a vocabulary they still use. His "Startup Metrics for Pirates" broke a customer's journey into five stages, abbreviated AARRR (say it like a pirate)2:

Stage McClure's definition The question it asks A number to watch
Acquisition Users come to the site from various channels Are the right people finding us? Visitors or sign-ups by channel
Activation Users enjoy a happy first visit Do new users get value fast? Share who reach a first real result
Retention Users come back and visit many times Do they keep coming back? Share still active after 30 or 90 days
Referral Users like it enough to refer others Do they bring anyone? Invites sent, and referred sign-ups
Revenue Users do something that makes money Do they pay, and pay more over time? Paid conversion, revenue per customer

Find the stage that's actually stuck

The stages chain together, so a weak one limits everything after it. Here's an illustrative product. Each month 2,000 people sign up, 30% of them activate and a quarter of those are still active after 30 days. That's 150 lasting users a month. Now compare three fixes:

  • Get 20% more sign-ups: 2,400 × 30% × 25% = 180 lasting users, and you pay for every extra sign-up.
  • Lift activation from 30% to 45%, a 50% jump that's realistic when the first session is broken: 2,000 × 45% × 25% = 225, with no extra ad spend.
  • Lift 30-day retention from 25% to 30%: 2,000 × 30% × 30% = 180.

Notice that a 20% lift at any single stage gets you to the same 180, because the stages multiply. So the stage to fix is the one where you can realistically win the biggest lift, and that's often a neglected one, like a confusing first session, rather than the one getting the most attention in meetings.

Why retention usually comes first

The economics lean hard toward keeping customers. Depending on the study and the industry, winning a new customer costs five to 25 times more than keeping one, and research by Bain's Frederick Reichheld found that raising retention by 5% lifts profits by 25% to 95%3. Treat that range as a direction rather than a promise. It isn't an argument against acquisition, either: the point is that a product has to hold on to the users it wins before you pay for more of them.

Retention also tells you whether growth is even possible. Plot the share of each month's sign-ups still active over time, and you get a retention curve. Peter Fader and Bruce Hardie, who build statistical models of these curves, note that cohort retention rates typically rise over time. Much of that comes from sorting: the customers most likely to leave do it early, so the ones left are the loyal ones. Their newer model also lets each customer's odds of leaving change over time4.

A healthy curve drops early and then flattens, which means some group finds lasting value. A curve that keeps sliding toward zero means no group does, and more acquisition just pours water into a leaky bucket.

Growth beyond the funnel

The funnel describes one product and one market. Companies also grow by selling to a new segment, entering a new country or adding a product their current customers will buy. Those bets are bigger and slower, and they work best once the core funnel holds water.

Common traps

  • Buying growth before fixing retention. New users leak out as fast as you pay for them.
  • Celebrating sign-ups. Acquisition numbers look great right up until you check who stayed.
  • Optimizing every stage at once. Pick the one that limits the rest.
  • Averaging away the story. A total user count can grow while every new cohort retains worse than the last.

In an interview

For "Our growth has stalled. What would you do?", don't jump to a campaign. Walk the funnel stage by stage, ask for the conversion at each step and how recent cohorts retain, and name the one stage you'd fix first and why.

Self-check: A meditation app gets plenty of downloads, but only 8% of new users open it a second time. The team wants an influencer campaign. What would you say?

Hold the campaign. The first session isn't delivering enough value: 92% of new users never come back, so more downloads mostly buy more one-time visits. Find out what the 8% who return did in their first session, design the first session to get everyone there, and revisit acquisition once more new users stick.

Check your understanding

4 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. Every monthly cohort of a product slides steadily toward zero active users. Leadership wants to double ad spend. What do you say?

    Show answer

    Fix retention first, since new users would leak out the same way. When no cohort levels off, new users leave just like the old ones. Acquisition pours water into a leaky bucket.

  2. Each month 5,000 people sign up, 20% activate and 40% of those are still active after 30 days. Which change keeps the most users?

    Show answer

    Raising activation from 20% to 30%. Work it through: today that's 400 lasting users. More sign-ups gives 500, better retention gives 480, and better activation gives 600. The stages multiply, so the biggest relative lift wins, here 50% against 25% and 20%.

  3. A software company reports net revenue retention of 130%. What does that number tell you?

    Show answer

    Customers from a year earlier are spending 30% more, so their expansion more than makes up for any churn. Net revenue retention compares what the same customers spend now with a year earlier. Above 100% means expansion outweighs churn. It doesn't tell you how much growth came from new customers.

  4. A cohort's retention rate rises between month 3 and month 12. What's the most likely explanation?

    Show answer

    The customers most likely to leave did so early, leaving the loyal ones. Fader and Hardie's work shows much of the rise comes from sorting. Individual loyalty can change too, so test it rather than assume it.
