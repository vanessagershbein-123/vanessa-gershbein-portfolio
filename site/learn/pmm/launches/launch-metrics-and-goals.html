Launch metrics and goals

PMMLesson 4.310 min

Goal: Set launch goals that predict success instead of just looking good, and know what you'd do if you miss them.

Skills: Data & chartsSizing & forecasting

It's the Monday after launch. Page views are up, sign-ups are up, and someone is drafting the celebration email. Can anyone say whether the launch worked?

Only if you decided before launch which numbers count and what level means success. Otherwise the launch gets judged by whichever number looks best afterward.

Numbers that flatter and numbers that teach

Eric Ries, author of The Lean Startup, has a name for numbers like these: vanity metrics. Page views, message counts and "billions served" all qualify. They look good, but they don't tell you what to do next. Worse, when they rise, every team credits its own work, so nobody learns anything. Ries argues that metrics should be actionable, accessible and auditable. Vanity numbers may be fine for press, he says, but they're dangerous for running a company1. An actionable metric shows cause and effect. If it moves, you can tell why1.

In a launch, that usually means total sign-ups and page views. A better number is the share of new users who reach a first real result. If it drops, you can go find where people got stuck.

Early warnings and final scores

Revenue is the number everyone wants from a launch, and it's one of the last to arrive. By then, it's too late to fix anything.

McKinsey separates leading indicators, which look forward and let a team act early, from lagging indicators, which measure results after the fact. It recommends tracking three to eight key metrics at any level, and warns that relying mostly on lagging ones makes a team reactive2. It wrote that about product development, but the same split works for launches:

Leading (first weeks) Lagging (months later)
Self-serve launch Share of new users who reach a first real result; trial starts Conversion to paid; retention after 90 days
Sales-led launch New pipeline that mentions the launch; sales calls using the new pitch Win rate; deal size; revenue
Feature for existing customers Share of target accounts that try it Ongoing use; renewals; fewer support tickets on the old problem

How many goals each tier gets

A bug fix doesn't need a dashboard. The tier sets how ambitious the goals are and how many you track:

  • Tier 1: a small set, about three, with at least one leading and one lagging goal, each with a target, reviewed weekly for the first month.
  • Tier 2: one adoption goal among the customers it's for.
  • Tier 3: no launch goal. Quality and support tickets are enough.

The five parts of a real goal

"Drive awareness" sounds like a goal. It isn't. It has no metric, no target, no date, no baselineWhere a chart's value axis starts. For bars it should be zero. and no owner. Compare: "Raise the share of new trials that invite a teammate within seven days from 25% to 40% by March 31, owned by the growth PM." That one has all five parts, so on April 1 everyone will know whether it happened.

Then decide in advance what you'll do if you miss. A leading indicator well below target in week two is your cue to change the message, the onboarding or the audience. Don't wait for the quarterly numbers.

Where launch goals go wrong

  • Reporting only sign-ups. They prove interest, not value.
  • Tracking everything. Twenty metrics on a dashboard means nobody acts on any of them.
  • No baseline. "10,000 users" means nothing until you know what normal looks like.
  • Setting goals after launch. Every result then looks like a win.

In an interview

When someone asks how you'd measure success, give one leading and one lagging metric, each with a target. Then say what you'd change if the leading one comes in low. That shows you'd act on the numbers.

Self-check: The week-one dashboard shows 50,000 page views and 8,000 sign-ups, and leadership is calling the launch a success. What do you ask before agreeing?

What share of the 8,000 reached a first real result, and how that compares with the goal and with past launches. Then which segments signed up: the target customers or curious visitors? Sign-ups and page views show interest. Activation and early retention show whether the launch is working.

Check your understanding

3 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. Which of these is a vanity metric for a launch?

    Show answer

    Total sign-ups in launch week. Total sign-ups look good but don't tell you whether people got value or what to change.

  2. Which is a leading indicator for a self-serve launch?

    Show answer

    The share of new trial users who reach a first real result in week one. Leading indicators show up early and let you act. Revenue and renewals are lagging results.

  3. Which goal is written well?

    Show answer

    "Raise the share of new trials that invite a teammate within seven days from 25% to 40% by March 31, owned by the growth PM". It has a metric, a target, a date, a baseline and an owner.
