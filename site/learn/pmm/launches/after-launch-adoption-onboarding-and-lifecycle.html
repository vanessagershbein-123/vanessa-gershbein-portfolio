After launch: adoption, onboarding and lifecycle

PMMLesson 4.411 min

Goal: Get customers to actually use what shipped, spot when adoption stalls, and know what to fix.

Skills: Data & charts

Ninety days after launch, nobody will care how many people opened the announcement email. What matters is how many of them changed the way they work because of it.

That's adoption. A launch announces a feature. Adoption is whether anyone actually uses it. For B2BBusiness-to-business: selling to companies rather than to consumers. products in particular, it's what renewals and upsells are built on, so your job doesn't end on launch day. It shifts from getting attention to getting use.

Why adoption is the real finish line

Start with what's at stake. For a software company with $500 million in annual recurring revenueAnnual recurring revenue: the yearly value of subscription contracts., Bain estimates that each point of net revenue retentionNet revenue retention: what a group of existing customers pays now compared with a year ago, after upgrades, downgrades and cancellations. Above 100% means growth from existing customers outweighs losses. is worth about $25 million over five years. Net revenue retention is the revenue a company keeps and grows from its existing customers over a year. Bain reported in November 2026 that the median for public software companies fell from about 115% in 2023 to about 107%. Customer success hiring rose by more than half over six years as companies tried to protect that revenue1.

Customers renew and expand when the product keeps proving its value. A feature nobody uses can't prove anything. That's why adoption belongs in the launch plan, with its own goal and a date to hit it.

Who adopts first, and who waits

People don't all show up at once, and the ones who arrive first aren't much like the ones who come later.

Everett Rogers introduced the diffusion of innovation theory in 1962. It sorts the people who adopt something new into five groups: innovators (2.5% of adopters), early adopters (13.5%), the early majority (34%), the late majority (34%) and laggards (16%). OpenStax notes that early adopters, often opinion leaders whose advice others follow, are usually the best first target for something new2.

The handoff from one group to the next is where adoption often stalls. Geoffrey Moore made the same point about tech markets: mainstream buyers want proof that a product works for companies like theirs3. Each group needs something different from you:

Group What gets them to adopt What the PMM provides
Innovators and early adopters The new capability itself Early access, a direct line to the product team
Early majority Proof it works for companies like theirs Customer stories, benchmarks, simple setup
Late majority Low risk and low effort Defaults that work, migration help, support on hand
Laggards Little choice Sunset notices for the old way, plenty of support

Teach in the moment, not up front

You've probably opened a new app, been walked through a tour of every button before you'd clicked anything, and hit "Skip" halfway through.

Nielsen Norman Group warns that up-front tutorials, the walkthroughs that appear before someone has done anything, tend to interrupt people, get skipped and often don't improve how well they do the task. What works better is help that appears when someone needs it, such as a tip on an unfamiliar tool. It should be easy to dismiss and easy to find again, and it should give more detail only on request. NN/g suggests saving help for the features that really need it4. Empty screens are another chance to teach: a new user's blank dashboard should explain what will appear there and offer a direct path to the first key task4.

That changes where your launch messaging should live. The announcement email reaches people when they're not using the product. An in-product prompt reaches them at the exact moment the feature would help.

The right message for where each person is

Someone who's never touched the feature and someone who uses it daily shouldn't get the same email. After launch, what people need depends on what they've done so far:

Who What they need Example
Customers who haven't tried it A reason that fits their work An in-product prompt when they do the task it improves
Tried it once Help getting to a real result A short guide or template for the first real use
Using it regularly The next step The related feature, or the paid tier that extends it
Admins and buyers Proof it's paying off A usage summary for their next business review
Sales and customer success What to say now Updated talk tracks and the first customer results

Check adoption at 30, 60 and 90 days against the goal you set. Wherever people drop off, that's the message to change.

When adoption stalls: know, can or want?

A stall usually looks like one of two things. Early users signed up, but the next wave isn't following. Or people try the feature once and never come back. Before you change anything, work out which of three gaps is to blame:

Gap The question Typical fix
Know Do the right users know it exists? In-product prompts, targeted email, a sales and success briefing
Can Can they get it working? Templates, better defaults, help in context, setup support
Want Is it worth it to them? Better positioning for the next group, proof from peers, or a product change

Getting the diagnosis right matters because the fixes cost very different amounts. You can send all the emails you like, and they still won't solve a "want" problem.

Where adoption plans go wrong

  • Treating launch day as the finish line. Most of the value comes from adoption in the months after.
  • One onboarding tour for everyone. It interrupts people at the wrong time, so they skip it.
  • Reading early-adopter usage as proof. The early majority needs different evidence.
  • Fixing the wrong gap. More awareness won't help users who can't get the feature to work.

In an interview

If you're asked what you'd do after launch, walk through how you'd measure adoption, where you'd look for drop-off, and how you'd tell an awareness problem from a value problem. That last distinction is where you show you'd fix the right thing.

Self-check: Three months after launch, 20% of target accounts have tried a new reporting feature, but only 5% use it weekly. What do you look at, and what might you change?

There are two gaps here. The 80% of target accounts that never tried it point to "know." The drop from trying to keeping points to "can" or "want." Find where people stop: while setting up a first report, or after seeing one. If setup is the problem, add templates and help in context. If people build one report and leave, it may not answer a question they care about. Talk to a few who stopped, then reposition or improve it for that need.

Check your understanding

3 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. Your app opens with a seven-screen tutorial. Most people tap Skip, and the ones who finish don't do any better. What would you try instead?

    Show answer

    Tips that appear when someone first reaches a feature, easy to dismiss and easy to find again. Nielsen Norman Group warns that up-front tutorials interrupt people before they need help. Help that shows up at the moment it's useful works better.

  2. Your new tier is a hit with early adopters, but deals with mainstream companies keep stalling. What are those buyers most likely missing?

    Show answer

    Proof it has worked for companies like theirs. Early adopters will try something on its promise. Geoffrey Moore's point is that mainstream buyers want to see it proven out first, so they look for proof from companies like theirs.

  3. Many users tried a feature once but few came back. Which gap is least likely to be the problem?

    Show answer

    Know: they aren't aware it exists. People who tried it already know about it. The problem is getting it to work or making it worth coming back to.
