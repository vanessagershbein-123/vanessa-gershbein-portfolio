Launch tiers

PMMLesson 4.29 min

Goal: Decide how much launch a release deserves, and match the effort to the impact, not to how hard it was to build.

Skills: Positioning & strategy

Most releases shouldn't be launches. A team that turns every update into an event wears out its sales reps, the press and its own customers. Then, when something truly big ships, it sounds like one more announcement in the pile.

Launch tiers are the fix. They give everyone a shared way to decide, before anything ships, how much attention each release gets.

The one rule: impact, not effort

A team that just spent six months on a project will want it launched loudly. That's a natural instinct, and it's usually the wrong guide.

Pragmatic Institute's guide to launch tiers puts one rule at the center: size the launch by its expected impact on the business and the market, not by how much engineering went into it. Tier 1 is for the biggest changes, such as a new category or a major shift, and it gets the most resources. Tier 2 covers smaller or incremental releases that still deserve enough support to get noticed. Tier 3 is for minimal-impact releases like maintenance updates, which need only routine communication. Pragmatic also notes that circumstances can override a tier1.

In practice, each tier comes with a default package, so nobody rebuilds the plan for every release:

Tier What usually qualifies What it gets
1 A new product, a new paid plan or price, or a change that opens a new segment A full go-to-market plan, press and analyst briefings, sales training, a campaign, an in-product announcement
2 A meaningful new feature for existing customers, or one that closes a gap competitors use against you A blog post, email to the customers it affects, a sales update, an in-product message
3 Improvements and fixes Release notes or a changelog entry

Let the tier set the process, too

Tiers decide how loud a launch is. They can also decide how much process a release has to go through before it ships.

GitLab's public handbook draws that line clearly. A feature that fits its existing plans simply ships in a monthly release. Mainly new products, services or SKUsStock-keeping unit: one specific product, such as one flavor in one size., or changes just as complex to run, go through its new product introduction process, with a steering committee, one directly responsible person and a launch checklist2. GitLab's marketing runs on a matching rhythm: smaller launches for the monthly releases, major launches twice a year and campaigns each quarter2.

That rhythm hides a useful trick. Small releases don't each need their own moment. Bundle several into one bigger launch, and customers hear one clear story instead of a trickle of minor announcements.

Five questions that settle the tier

Tier debates get heated when someone's favorite project is on the line. Ask these first, and the answer is usually clear:

  • Does it change what we sell or what we charge? If it adds a paid plan or raises prices, it's probably Tier 1. A price cut on an existing feature can be Tier 2.
  • Does it open a new segment or help win deals against a rival? Tier 1 or 2.
  • Who needs to know? Every customer, some of them, or almost nobody.
  • Does sales need to say something new? Then it needs enablement, which means at least Tier 2.
  • What happens if nobody hears about it? If the honest answer is "not much," it's Tier 3.

Where tiering goes wrong

  • Tiering by engineering effort. A six-month rebuild that customers barely notice is still Tier 3.
  • Making everything Tier 1. If every launch is big, none of them are.
  • Setting a tier and forgetting it. A Tier 2 feature that starts winning deals may deserve a second, bigger push.
  • Skipping Tier 3 notes. Small changes still need a changelog entry, because customers and support need to know what changed.

In an interview

Asked how you'd launch a small feature? Start with how you'd tier it. Recommending a smaller launch can feel risky, but explaining why a release deserves less noise shows judgment, not a lack of ambition.

Self-check: Engineering spent six months rebuilding the search backend. Users will notice that results come back a little faster. What tier is it, and why?

Tier 3 by default. The impact on customers and the business is small, however much work went in. It could move up to Tier 2 if slow search was a top complaint in support tickets or lost deals, because then it answers a real objection and sales should know about it.

Check your understanding

3 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. What should decide a release's launch tier?

    Show answer

    Its expected impact on the business and the market. Tiers allocate launch effort by impact, not by development effort.

  2. Which release most clearly deserves Tier 1?

    Show answer

    A new paid plan that opens the product to a new segment. Changing what you sell or charge, or who you sell to, is the kind of change Tier 1 exists for.

  3. Engineering redesigned a popular feature that's already included in every plan, and wants a full launch. How would GitLab's rule handle it?

    Show answer

    Ship it in the monthly release with the smaller launch that goes with it, since it doesn't change what customers buy. At GitLab, features that fit existing plans ship in the monthly release. The full process is saved for new products, services or SKUs, which change what a customer can buy.
