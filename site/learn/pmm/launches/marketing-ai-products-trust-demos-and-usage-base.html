Marketing AI products: trust, demos and usage-based value

PMMLesson 4.511 min

Goal: Launch an AI product buyers can believe: claims you can prove, demos that hold up, controls that reduce risk, and pricing tied to value.

Skills: Positioning & strategyWriting & storytelling

Your AI demo probably isn't the first one your buyer has sat through. They've watched demos that worked perfectly on stage and fell apart on their own data. They've read the headlines about AI that got rolled back. So they show up skeptical, and they have good reason to.

Positioning and launch planning still apply. An AI launch just adds four questions you have to answer: what can you honestly claim, how will you demo it, what controls does the buyer get, and how will you charge for it?

What buyers mean by trust

"Trustworthy AI" can sound vague until someone breaks it into parts. The US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) does that in its AI Risk Management Framework, which lists seven characteristics of trustworthy AI: valid and reliable; safe; secure and resilient; accountable and transparent; explainable and interpretable; privacy-enhanced; and fair, with harmful bias managed. Valid and reliable is the base the others rest on1. Frameworks like this get revised, so check for the newest version.

Each one turns into a question a buyer will ask, and something you can hand them in reply:

Trait What the buyer asks What the PMM provides
Valid and reliable "Will it work on our data?" A pilot or test on the buyer's own data, with results
Accountable and transparent "Who's responsible when it's wrong?" Clear human handoff and approval steps
Explainable "Why did it answer that?" Sources or reasoning shown with each answer
Privacy-enhanced "What happens to our data?" A plain data-use policy, including whether data trains models
Secure "Will it pass our security review?" Certifications and a security overview

Claims you could defend to a regulator

DoNotPay marketed itself as the world's first "robot lawyer." According to the FTC's complaint, it did so without testing whether its output matched what a human lawyer would produce. In September 2024, the US Federal Trade Commission announced Operation AI Comply, five enforcement actions against deceptive AI claims and schemes, and DoNotPay was one of the targets2.

Regulators are watching AI claims, which gives you a simple test for every one you write: could you show the evidence to a skeptical buyer, or a regulator, today? "Answers 60% of support questions in our pilot with three customers" passes. "Replaces your support team" doesn't.

Demos that survive the buyer's data

A scripted demo on clean data proves one thing: the product can work once. Your buyer wants to know whether it'll work for them:

  • Use realistic data, including the messy questions and edge cases their customers actually send.
  • Show how it handles low confidence. It feels backward, but the buyer already expects the AI to be wrong sometimes. Let them see what happens when it isn't sure: does it hand off, ask for approval, or make something up?
  • Show how success is measured. Name the metric and how it's calculated, so the number in the demo can be checked later.
  • Offer a pilot. The strongest demo is the buyer's own results.

Charging for use, or for results

AI changes how software gets priced, because every use has a real cost. Bain groups AI pricing into three kinds of meter: effort (such as compute used), output (such as drafts written or records updated) and outcome (a business result). In its review of about 200 B2BBusiness-to-business: selling to companies rather than to consumers. software companies' public pricing in June 2026, among companies adding an AI meter, about 55% charged by output, 35% by effort and 10% by outcome. About four in five offered capacity models, where customers commit to a set amount up front. Bain sees outcome pricing fitting customer support best, because a resolved conversation is easy to observe and credit, and it expects outcome pricing to stay narrow for several years3.

Support is where outcome pricing caught on early. In August 2024, for example, Zendesk said customers of its AI agents would pay only for issues the AI resolved on its own4.

Notice what that does for trust. "You pay only when it works" is a pricing model and a promise at the same time. The promise only holds if the definition of "works" is clear and fair, so put that definition in the launch materials, not the fine print.

Worked example: Klarna's big claim, and what came next

In February 2024, Klarna announced that its AI assistant had handled 2.3 million conversations in its first month, two-thirds of its customer service chats. Klarna said that was the work of 700 full-time agents, that resolution time fell from about 11 minutes to under 2, and that customer satisfaction matched human agents5.

Then the story changed. In May 2025, Klarna's CEO told Bloomberg that cutting costs in customer service had gone too far and led to lower quality. Klarna started recruiting so customers would always be able to reach a real person6.

What you can take from it:

  • Replacement claims age badly. "The work of 700 agents" made headlines, then became the benchmark the company was judged against.
  • Lead with control, not replacement. Many buyers trust AI that handles routine work and hands the rest to people more than AI pitched as a substitute for people.
  • Quality is part of the claim. Speed and cost numbers need a matching quality measure, or the launch story can turn against you.

Where AI launches go wrong

  • Demoing only on clean data. It proves nothing about the buyer's world.
  • Claims you can't back up. If you couldn't show the evidence to a regulator, don't publish it.
  • A metric definition hidden in the fine print. If the number changes when the definition changes, say so.
  • Pitching replacement without the quality evidence to back it. It invites the hardest version of every trust question.

In an interview

If an interviewer hands you an AI feature to launch, cover four things in order: the claim you can prove, the demo, the controls the buyer gets, and the pricing. Interviewers want to see that you plan for skepticism instead of hoping enthusiasm wins.

Self-check: Your AI feature resolved 80% of tickets in a pilot with one customer. Marketing wants "80% resolution rate" on the homepage. What do you check first?

Start with how the 80% was calculated: which conversations counted and which were left out. Then ask whether one customer's results represent the market, and whether you could show the evidence to a skeptical buyer or a regulator. Until you have results across many customers, a safer claim names the context, such as "80% of routine questions resolved in a pilot".

Check your understanding

3 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. Your AI feature's launch plan leads with explainability and a bias audit, but in testing it gives a wrong answer one time in five. What should come first?

    Show answer

    Fixing or narrowing what it does until it works reliably. In NIST's framework, valid and reliable is the base the other trustworthy-AI characteristics rest on. Explaining a wrong answer doesn't make it right.

  2. Your team wants to market its AI contract reviewer as "as good as a lawyer." What should you check before that line goes anywhere?

    Show answer

    Whether you've tested its output against what lawyers actually produce. The FTC acted against DoNotPay for marketing a "robot lawyer" without testing whether its output matched a human lawyer's. A capability claim needs testing behind it.

  3. Which AI product is the best fit for charging per outcome?

    Show answer

    A support agent that resolves customer conversations on its own. Outcome pricing needs a result both sides can see and agree on. A resolved support conversation fits, which is why Bain sees support as the best fit today.
