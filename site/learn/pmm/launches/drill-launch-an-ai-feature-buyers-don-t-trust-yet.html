Launch an AI feature buyers don't trust yet

PMMDrill 4.515 minAdvanced

Skills: Writing & storytellingPositioning & strategy

The prompt

The facts.

  • In October 2026, Intercom launched Fin 4, the latest version of its AI agent for customer service. It added proactive conversations with customers, an agent for back-office tasks, and "Human in the Loop," which uses a confidence score to decide when Fin should ask a teammate to approve, edit or take over1.
  • Fin's site says its resolution rate averages 76% across more than 12,000 customers1.
  • Fin is priced at $0.99 per "outcome." An outcome counts when the customer confirms the issue is solved, stops asking after Fin answers, or Fin completes a defined workflow. There's no charge when Fin can't answer or the customer asks for a human, and a reopened conversation is refunded2.
  • In July 2026, Intercom changed how it reports Fin's performance. Automation rate equals involvement rate times resolution rate. Conversations where Fin was active but never had a chance to answer, for example because escalation rules sent them to a person, stopped counting as "involved." In Intercom's own example of 1,000 conversations, the resolution rate rises from 33% to 50% while the automation rate stays at 25%. Intercom says the number of resolutions and billing don't change2.

Your task: You're launching Fin 4 to heads of support at mid-size companies. Many tried an AI chatbot before and got burned by wrong answers. Write:

  1. The headline claim, and the evidence behind it.
  2. The demo plan.
  3. The two controls you'd lead with.
  4. How you'd present the 76% figure, given the July 2026 change.
  5. The offer that gets a skeptical buyer to try it.

Your earlier answer

Sample answer

1. Headline claim: "Fin handles the routine questions and hands the rest to your team, and you only pay when it resolves one." The evidence is the outcome-based pricing itself plus a pilot on the buyer's own conversations. It leads with control and fair pricing, not with a percentage.

2. Demo plan: run it on a sample of the buyer's real past tickets, including the messy ones. Show one case where Fin isn't confident and Human in the Loop sends it to a person. Then show the reporting, with the metric definitions on screen.

3. Controls to lead with: Human in the Loop, because the burned buyer's fear is a wrong answer going out unchecked, and the pricing rules, because no charge when Fin can't answer or the customer asks for a human puts much of the risk on the vendor.

4. The 76% figure: use it carefully, with its definition. First confirm which definition the 76% uses, then say so, and pair it with the metric a head of support budgets around: the automation rate across all conversations. Hiding the definition change would hand a competitor an easy attack, and a buyer who later spots the jump in their own dashboard would stop trusting every number.

5. The offer: a time-limited pilot on one queue of routine questions, with a spending cap and a pre-agreed success measure, such as the automation rate on that queue after 30 days.

Why it works

  • It answers the burned buyer's real fear, wrong answers going out unchecked, before talking about scale.
  • It uses the pricing model as a trust signal, not just a revenue model.
  • It treats metric definitions as part of the message, which is exactly where AI claims tend to break.

What interviewers push on

  • "Isn't 76% your strongest selling point? Why downplay it?" It's strong, but it's easy to misread. Pairing it with the automation rate keeps it credible after the buyer's own data comes in.
  • "Won't 'only pay when it works' cut revenue?" It can lower revenue per conversation early, but it removes the main reason not to start, and usage grows as trust does.

Practice twist

A competitor's homepage claims a 90% resolution rate with no definition. Write the two-sentence answer your sales team should give when a prospect brings it up.
