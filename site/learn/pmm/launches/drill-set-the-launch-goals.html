Set the launch goals

PMMDrill 4.312 minIntermediate

Skills: Sizing & forecastingData & charts

The prompt

The facts.

  • On October 29, 2025, Canva relaunched Affinity, its professional app for photo editing, vector design and page layout, as completely free. New users need a free Canva account to download it. Canva premium subscribers get Canva's AI tools inside Affinity, and projects can be exported to Canva to collaborate and publish1.
  • On November 3, Canva said more than one million people had signed up for Affinity in four days. It explained that its model is to offer powerful tools free and charge for advanced features such as premium content, collaboration and AI, and that it has more than 28 million paying customers and $3.5 billion in annualized revenue1.

Your task: It's October 2025, before launch, and you're the PMM for Affinity.

  1. Set one leading goal for the first four weeks and one lagging goal for the first year. Include the metric, target, date, baseline and owner, and estimate the numbers you don't have.
  2. Write the failure condition: what result in week four would make you change the plan?
  3. After launch, Canva announced one million sign-ups in four days. Is that a good measure of success? What would you want to know next?

Your earlier answer

Sample answer

Leading goal: the share of new Affinity users who save a real project and come back in their second week, with a target of 30% by the end of November and the growth lead as owner. Sign-ups are tracked, but they aren't the goal.

Lagging goal: the number of Affinity users who become paying Canva customers, through Canva AI inside Affinity or by moving work into Canva to collaborate, measured over the first 12 months against Canva's normal conversion rate for new free users.

Failure condition: sign-ups beat expectations but fewer than one in five new users returns in week two. That would suggest people tried it out of curiosity, and that the onboarding or the fit with professional work needs fixing before more promotion.

On the million sign-ups: it's a strong signal of demand and a good press number, but it's exactly the kind of metric Ries warns about. It doesn't show whether professional designers switched their work to Affinity, or whether any of those users will pay Canva. Next you'd want week-two return rates, the share who are professional designers, and early conversion to Canva premium.

Why it works

  • It ties the goals to how Canva actually makes money from a free product: accounts, then paid AI and collaboration in Canva.
  • It separates a press metric (sign-ups) from a decision metric (return and conversion).
  • It sets a failure condition in advance, so the team knows when to change course.

What interviewers push on

  • "Why not just aim for more sign-ups?" Sign-ups were never the business goal. A free product that nobody uses for real work costs money and builds no revenue.
  • "You estimated the targets. Is that a problem?" No, as long as you say so and set the baseline from Canva's own data on new free users before launch.

Practice twist

Canva's leadership decides the real goal is winning professional designers from paid tools. Rewrite both goals for that audience.
