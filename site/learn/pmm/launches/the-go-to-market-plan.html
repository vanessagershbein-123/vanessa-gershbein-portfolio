The go-to-market plan

PMMLesson 4.112 min

Goal: Write a launch plan that says who it's for, how they'll buy, who has to be ready and how you'll know it worked, and pick the motion that fits.

Skills: Positioning & strategy

Most launches don't fail on launch day. Launch day usually looks great. They fail over the next few months, when sales can't explain the product, support can't answer the questions coming in, and the people who signed up never come back.

A go-to-marketGo-to-market: the plan for how a product reaches and wins customers, covering the target, message, pricing, channels and launch. plan is how you head that off. It's less a document than a handful of decisions you make before anyone announces anything: who you're going after, what you'll tell them, how they'll buy, who has to be ready, and what counts as winning.

The three things to settle first

Pragmatic Institute boils launch planning down to three questions, and each one catches a different kind of failure1:

  • Goals: what does winning look like, and by when? Pragmatic's example is a revenue target for the first nine months. Set it before launch, or every result will look like a success afterward.
  • Readiness: can the whole company sell and support this on day one? That means support, accounting, delivery and legal, plus a sales team that understands the economic, user and technical buyers. Pragmatic warns that tossing a product to sales "over the wall" can delay pipeline by at least three months.
  • Constraints: does the plan fit the people you actually have? In Pragmatic's example, seven salespeople can work about 42 opportunities a month. If your goal needs far more than that, the plan is broken before launch day.

Its pre-launch checklist adds the questions teams skip when they're excited about a launch: how urgent the problem really is for buyers, exactly which segment you're aiming at, and whether your positioning and messaging actually say the same thing1.

Seven questions every plan answers

Put it all together and a launch plan comes down to seven questions:

The question
Target Who exactly are we going after first?
Message Why should they care, compared with what they use today?
Motion How will they buy: on their own, through sales or through a partner?
Channels Where will they hear about it?
Readiness Can sales, support and billing handle it on day one?
Size How much effort does this launch deserve?
Goals What counts as success, and by when?

When a team can't answer these, it shows fast. Joan Schneider and Julie Hall, who wrote about launch failures for Harvard Business Review, say they can often predict a flop from a single conversation with a team that calls its product revolutionary without the research to back it up2.

How will they buy?

Two products launch the same week. One is a $12-a-month tool a designer can buy with a credit card before lunch. The other is a $150,000-a-year platform that needs a security review, a procurement process and six weeks of setup. Same launch date, completely different launches.

That difference is the motion, and a few signals usually make it obvious:

Signal Points to self-serve Points to sales-led
Price Low, monthly, on a card High, annual contract
Who decides One person or a small team A buying group
Reviews before buying None, or a quick trial Security, legal, procurement
Time to value Minutes to days Weeks of setup
Existing customers Mostly self-serve accounts Mostly accounts with an account manager

There's a third option that's easy to forget: let someone else sell it. On AWS Marketplace, for example, a software company can make private offers to customers and authorize channel partners to resell its product. AWS bills the customer and pays the seller, and sellers can work deals alongside AWS's own account teams3. A partner-led motion makes sense when a partner already owns the relationship or the budget, such as budget a customer has already set aside with a cloud provider.

Plenty of launches end up using more than one motion. Just decide which one leads. A plan that tries to do everything at once usually does nothing particularly well.

Worked example: Asana's spring 2025 release

Here's what a real launch looks like through this lens. On May 28, 2025, Asana released ready-made AI workflows for request tracking, creative requests, ticketing and work intake, plus new AI Studio rules for summarizing and sorting tasks. It aimed the release at the teams that live on requests and process (operations, IT, support, marketing, design, HR, finance and legal) and pitched it as AI for everyone, helping teams move faster and share what works. It also said AI would be included in every paid plan, with AI Studio's basic tier rolling out to all of them from June 20254.

The pricing page shows how people actually buy. The free Personal plan and the paid Starter ($10.99 per user a month, billed annually) and Advanced ($24.99) plans are self-serve. Enterprise and Enterprise+ go through sales. Paid plans include monthly AI Studio credits, and teams can buy more4.

Read it as a plan and the choices jump out:

  • Target: teams that already run the same requests over and over, where a template saves time on day one.
  • Message: AI for everyone, not just power users. Including AI in every paid plan backs that up instead of just saying it.
  • Motion: product-led for the self-serve plans, where the templates do the onboarding, and sales-led for Enterprise, where buyers want governance and higher limits.
  • Goals: Asana didn't publish them. A PMM there would probably watch template use and upgrades to paid plans.

Prices and AI credits change, so check Asana's pricing page before quoting these numbers.

Common traps

  • Launching before anyone's ready. A great announcement that sales can't explain and support can't handle costs you deals for months.
  • Trying every motion at once. Pick the one that leads, and add the others once it works.
  • Setting goals after launch. Then every number looks like a win, and nobody learns anything.
  • A plan the team can't staff. Check the goal against the sales and marketing capacity you really have, not the capacity you wish you had.

In an interview

"How would you launch X?" is a common PMM case question, and it rewards structure more than creativity. Walk through the seven questions in order, then slow down on the one or two that matter most for this particular product. That's where you show judgment.

Self-check: A team plans to launch a $150,000-a-year security product with only a self-serve sign-up page and no sales involvement. What do you push back on?

The motion. At that price, a buying group decides, security and legal will review it, and setup takes time, so it needs a sales-led motion. A free trial or sandbox can still help evaluators, but someone has to run the deal. Then check readiness: does sales know the buyers, and can support handle onboarding?

Check your understanding

3 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. A launch plan has a revenue target and a big campaign ready, but support hasn't been trained and sales can't demo the feature yet. Which part of the plan is weak?

    Show answer

    Readiness. Readiness asks whether the whole company can sell and support the product on day one. Pragmatic warns that tossing a product to sales "over the wall" can delay pipeline by at least three months.

  2. Which launch most clearly needs a sales team to run the deal?

    Show answer

    A data-security platform sold per company, which needs a legal review, a pilot and integration with existing systems. A large contract, several approvers, formal reviews and setup work all point to a motion where a sales team runs the deal.

  3. A big prospect already spends heavily with AWS and has budget set aside there. Why might you sell through AWS Marketplace instead of a direct contract?

    Show answer

    The prospect's budget is already set aside with AWS, and AWS bills the customer and pays you. A partner-led motion makes sense when a partner already owns the relationship or the budget. On AWS Marketplace, AWS handles billing, and sellers can work deals alongside AWS's own account teams.
