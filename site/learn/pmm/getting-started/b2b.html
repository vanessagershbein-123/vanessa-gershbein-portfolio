B2B product marketing

PMMLesson 1.212 min

Goal: Explain who buys in B2B, how they buy, and what a PMM builds for each step.

Skills: Interviews & careerPositioning & strategy

A software deal has gone well for three months. The team on the free plan loves the product, and their manager wants to buy. Then the security team sends questions nobody prepared for, finance asks for the return math, and the deal sits still for weeks. Nothing was wrong with the product. It just had more people to convince than anyone planned for.

Most tech PMM roles are B2BBusiness-to-business: selling to companies rather than to consumers.: the customer is a company, and the person who signs is rarely the only one deciding. A single deal can involve a group of people with different worries, take months, and pass through a website, a free plan and a salesperson before anyone pays. Your job is to make sure each of those people, at each of those steps, finds a reason to say yes. Three questions organize the work: who buys, how they buy, and what each of them needs to see.

Who's actually buying?

Start with company size, because a 20-person firm and a 20,000-person firm buy software in very different ways. There's no single cutoff. The US Small Business Administration sets "small" by industry, using either employee counts or revenue. Most manufacturers count as small at 500 employees or fewer, for example1. The EU uses different numbers: small means under 50 staff, and medium-sized means under 2502. Tech companies set their own tiers, usually some version of small business, mid-market and enterprise. So before you answer a case, ask what the company means by each.

The buyer is a group. Gartner puts B2B buying groups at five to 16 people from as many as four functions3. Forrester's 2026 survey counts 13 internal stakeholders in a typical decision, plus outside influencers4. Don't picture a room full of spreadsheets, either. Research from Bain finds that personal and emotional factors weigh more and more in B2B buying5.

Here's who tends to sit in a software buying group, and what each one needs from you:

Member What they worry about What the PMM gives them
The executive who signs Return, risk, priorities A business case and proof from similar companies
Daily users Whether it makes their work easier A demo, a trial, clear onboarding
IT and security Integration, data protection, compliance Security and integration documentation
Finance and procurement Cost and contract terms Clear pricing and packaging, return math
The champion Whether they can sell it internally A short internal pitch they can forward

How do they buy?

B2B companies sell in one of three ways, and the way they sell decides where your words end up.

Self-serve, or product-led. The product does the selling: it finds customers and turns them into paying ones, with a free or low-cost way in and little human sales contact6. More and more buyers want exactly that. In Gartner's survey of 646 B2B buyers in 2025, 67% said they prefer a buying experience without a sales rep, and 45% had used AI during a recent purchase3. With no seller in the room, the website, the pricing page, the free plan and the help docs carry your message.

Sales-led. A sales team runs the deal, usually for larger contracts. Your work reaches buyers secondhand, through what the sellers say and send.

Both. McKinsey found that most public B2B companies that started out purely product-led have since added their own sales teams. Sales turns trial and free users into larger deals, while marketing and product nurture those users together7. That's why most B2B PMMs end up writing for both motions at once.

What you build for each step

Follow a deal from the first website visit to renewal. Every stop has its own audience, its own thing for you to build and its own sign that it's working:

Touchpoint Who it's for What the PMM builds Signal it's working
Website and pricing page Buyers researching on their own Positioning, plan comparison, proof Sign-ups, trial starts, demo requests
Free plan or trial Daily users Onboarding emails, in-product tips Activation, trial-to-paid conversion
Sales conversations The buying group Pitch deck, demo script, battlecards, objection handling Win rate, deal cycle length
Security and procurement review IT, security, finance Security overview, pricing FAQ, return calculator Fewer deals stalling at review
After the sale Admins and users Adoption campaigns, release notes, customer stories Expansion and renewal

The sales row alone covers a lot. Pragmatic Institute lists case studies, presentation decks and one-pagers among the enablement materials PMMs create, along with information about target buyers and users and key messaging points for sales8. A Salesforce posting for a sales enablement PMM goes further, adding battlecardsA short internal guide that helps sales handle a specific competitor: where you win, where you lose and how to answer objections., objection handling, quarterly sales plays, and onboarding and training programs9.

Late reviews are a common place for deals to slow down. Forrester predicts that in 2027, risk reviews will delay or block a third of enterprise purchases of AI software, as legal, security and compliance teams ask vendors to prove their governance4. It's a forecast, not a measured number. Still, it explains why security documentation has landed on a PMM's list.

Worked example: Atlassian starts low-touch and adds sales later

Atlassian, the maker of Jira and Confluence, shows both motions inside one company. It grew on what its annual report calls a "high-velocity, low-friction distribution model." Its website is the main sales channel, customers can buy online with a credit card, prices are public, there's a free tier, and it says it doesn't rely on a traditional sales force until customers reach a certain size. As customers grew, a second motion joined the first. A direct sales team now focuses on large existing customers, adding teams, products and higher editions, and channel partners drove more than half of its fiscal 2025 revenue. The filing also warns that enterprise sales cycles are longer and cost more10.

By June 30, 2026, 57,334 Atlassian customers paid more than $10,000 a year for its cloud products, up from 51,978 a year earlier, and channel partners still drove more than half of its revenue10.

If you were the PMM there, your job would change as the customers grew:

  • Early on, the work looks consumer-like. The website, the pricing page, the free tier and onboarding do the selling, so clarity and self-serve proof matter most.
  • Large customers add a second job. Enablement for sellers and partners, a business case for the executive who signs, and answers for IT and security.
  • Same product, two kinds of messaging. One positioning, framed one way for a team that signs up on its own and another way for a buying group negotiating a contract.

Common traps

  • Writing only for the person who signs. The buyer signs once, but daily users decide whether the product sticks. If they hate it, renewals suffer.
  • Calling a company product-led because it has a free plan. Ask who actually closes the revenue.
  • Treating enablement as a deck. Sellers need training, updates and answers to new objections, or the deck goes unused.

In an interview

Interviewers often ask which markets you've worked in and how they differ from theirs. Before you answer any B2B case, say three things out loud: what size of customer you're assuming, how they buy, and who's in the buying group. Each of those choices changes the rest of your answer.

Self-check: Sales says three large deals stalled after the customer's security review. What do you build?

First find out what the security teams asked and which answers were missing or slow. Then build what would have helped: a security overview sellers can send before the review starts, a short FAQ for the common questions, and a proof point from a similar customer that passed review. The goal is to answer IT's questions before they turn into a delay.

Check your understanding

3 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. In a software deal, who most needs a business case that shows return and risk?

    Show answer

    The executive who signs. The executive answers for the spend, so return and risk carry the most weight. Users need to see their work get easier, and IT needs security and integration answers.

  2. A product-led company has no sales team. Where does most of the PMM's messaging live?

    Show answer

    The website, the pricing page, onboarding emails and in-product messages. With no sellers, the self-serve touchpoints have to carry the message.

  3. A company has a free plan, but 80% of revenue comes from contracts its sales team closes. How should a PMM treat it?

    Show answer

    As a hybrid, weighted toward sales-led. Ask who actually closes the revenue. McKinsey found most public, once purely product-led B2B companies have added their own sales teams.
