PMM interviews and how to prepare

PMMLesson 1.58 min

Goal: Know the question types in a PMM loop, and how to prepare for each.

Skills: Interviews & career

A PMM interview loop is the job in miniature. In one loop you may position a product, plan a launch, explain a metric and answer behavioral questions about your past work. That sounds like a lot to prepare for. The good news is that the question types are predictable, so you can walk in with a story or an approach ready for each one.

The questions you'll hear

PMA's interview guide splits questions into behavioral and role-specific ones, and its examples cover the types below. It also suggests preparing by studying the job description, using the product, reading its reviews and putting numbers on your results1:

  • Behavioral questions about past work
  • Walking through a launch you ran
  • Launch and metrics cases (for example: how would you launch X, and how would you know it worked?)
  • Funnel diagnosis (for example: why are paying users dropping off?)
  • Adapting the marketing mix for B2BBusiness-to-business: selling to companies rather than to consumers. vs. consumer
  • Cross-functional questions

The same guide makes a point to carry into every answer: "the launch itself is not the end goal."

Other questions worth preparing: walking through your last launch, how you'd measure product marketing, what you think of the company's messaging, and what you'd do if a new feature had low adoption. Francisco Bram, who led product marketing for several Uber businesses, comes at it from the skills side. He lists five hard skills these interviews test: market sizing, segmentation, narrative design, go-to-marketGo-to-market: the plan for how a product reaches and wins customers, covering the target, message, pricing, channels and launch. strategy and measurement2.

What a real loop looks like: Google

Loops vary by company and level, so treat this as one example. Google says the same basics apply whether you're applying for an engineering job or a marketing one. Its process typically takes about 6 to 8 weeks. You may take an online assessment, then have one or two short recruiter conversations. Depending on the role, you may be asked to do a small project, such as preparing a case study, before a panel of structured interviews with open-ended, role-related questions and no brainteasers3. Guides go out of date, so check any company's current process with your recruiter.

When they ask for a 90-day plan

Many PMM loops end with a presentation, and a 90-day plan is a common one. It's an odd assignment: you're planning a job you haven't started, at a company you've only seen from the outside. Two pieces of guidance help:

  • Start with learning. Michael Watkins, author of The First 90 Days, says to be systematic about deciding what you need to learn, and to set goals for the end of the first month, the second month and day 90, with a few early wins4.
  • Build it around their definition of success. Early in the process, ask the hiring manager how they define success in the role, then keep the plan high level. A typical one runs 3 to 4 pages5.

What interviewers listen for

Google's interview advice is to expect two kinds of questions: behavioral ones about past situations, and hypothetical ones about how you would approach a problem. Back up your impact with data, and structure answers with STAR3. Chicago Booth's guide to PM and PMM cases says interviewers judge your thought process, not just your answer, and one student found that pinning down a clear target customer made the rest of a case easier6.

A prep plan for each question type

The fastest prep is one concrete rep for each question type, built on products you already use:

Question type How to prepare
"What is product marketing?" A two-sentence definition, plus one real launch you can explain from start to finish
Customer research, segments and personas Walk through how you'd find the target customer for a product you use
Positioning and messaging Write a positioning for a product you like, starting from its real alternatives
Launch and go-to-market cases Practice structuring a launch: target, message, motion, readiness, goals
Pricing and packaging Explain how a product you pay for decides what's free and what costs more
Enablement and competitive questions Write a one-page battlecard against a real rival
Growth and campaign ideas Pick a product and say which stage of its funnel you'd fix first
Market sizing, metrics and funnel diagnosis Practice one estimate and one "this metric dropped, why?" question out loud
Behavioral and cross-functional stories Prepare four or five STAR stories, each with a number in the result
Self-check: Your PMM onsite is one week away. What three things do you do first?

Use the product and read its reviews, so your examples are real. Write the company's positioning in your own words and check it against their homepage. Then drill one launch case and one funnel diagnosis out loud, with a timer.

Check your understanding

3 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. You're walking an interviewer through a launch you ran. What should the answer cover besides the launch plan itself?

    Show answer

    What happened after launch: adoption and measurable results. Launch day isn't the finish line. PMA's guide says the launch itself is not the end goal, so show how customers adopted it and which numbers changed.

  2. What should a 90-day plan for an interview be built around?

    Show answer

    What you need to learn first, and how the hiring manager defines success in the role. Watkins says to start by deciding what you need to learn, with milestones at one, two and three months. Asking how the manager defines success keeps the plan tied to the job.

  3. "Tell me about a launch that missed its goal" and "How would you launch our new feature?" are different question types. How should the answers differ?

    Show answer

    Tell a real story with numbers for the first, and walk through your approach step by step for the second. Google tells candidates to expect both kinds. The first is behavioral, so it's judged on what you actually did and its impact. The second is hypothetical, so it's judged on how you think through the problem.
