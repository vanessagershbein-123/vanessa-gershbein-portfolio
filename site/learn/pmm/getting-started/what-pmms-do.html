What a product marketer actually does

PMMLesson 1.18 min

Goal: Explain what a PMM owns in two sentences, and how that differs from a PM and a brand manager.

Skills: Interviews & career

Ask a few people what a product marketer does and you'll hear "launches," "sales decks" or "messaging." None of them is wrong. Each is only one piece of the job.

Product marketing is one of the harder tech jobs to pin down, because it shifts with the company and the product. That matters the moment you walk into an interview, since many open with some version of "what does a PMM do?" You want a clear, specific answer ready before anything else.

The job in two sentences

Product marketing decides who a product is for, how the market should understand it, and what it takes for those people to adopt it. The product manager, by contrast, decides what gets built1. As the product marketer, you make sure the right people understand why it matters, and you drive adoption. That takes far more than managing a launch2.

Four jobs hiding in one title

Those two sentences cover a lot of ground. Martina Lauchengco, author of LOVED (an SVPG book on tech marketing), breaks product marketing into four fundamentals2:

Job What it looks like day to day
Ambassador Connects customer and market insight, and carries it back into the company
Strategist Directs the product's go-to-market
Storyteller Shapes how buyers and the market understand the product, through positioning and messaging
Evangelist Enables others, such as press, pundits and superfans, to tell the story

Look at who actually does the work in each row. Most of the four depend on teams the PMM doesn't run, which is why learning to win people over without authority is the job.

If four roles feel abstract, try four activities instead. RocketBlocks' free guide to the role, by former Google and PayPal product marketer Anna Wiggins, groups the work into market and customer research, positioning and messaging, launch, and driving adoption after launch3.

What PMMs say they own

Definitions tell you what the job should be. Surveys tell you what people actually get handed. The Product Marketing Alliance (PMA) surveys product marketers every year, and in its 2025 data, this is the share of PMMs who own each area4:

Area Share of PMMs who own it
Positioning and messaging 91%
Launches 80.9%
Sales enablement 78.7%
Customer and market research 69.7%
Competitive intelligence 65.2%
Website 65.2%
Content 48.3%
Onboarding 37.5%
Roadmap input 28.1%

Read these as shares of people, not hours. Roadmap input lands at the bottom because PMMs feed insight into the roadmap, but PMs decide it. The row to watch is onboarding. PMA reports that the share of PMMs who own it nearly doubled from the year before. Job postings point the same way: Notion's Lead PMM role includes driving adoption of key features after launch5.

PMM vs. PM vs. brand manager: three different questions

The three titles sound alike. The fastest way to tell them apart is the question each one is trying to answer:

Product manager Product marketing manager Brand manager
Core question What should we build, and why? Who is it for, how do we explain it, and are they adopting it? What does this brand mean to people?
Owns Strategy, roadmap, prioritized features Positioning, launches, sales enablement, market insight Brand identity and consistency
Often measured on Speed to market, roadmap delivery, customer satisfaction Market growth, customer acquisition cost, lifetime value Awareness, perception, loyalty, brand equity

Sources: Pragmatic Institute, SVPG, BU Career Development. In PMA's 2025 survey, 53.2% of product marketers named revenue as one of their KPIs6.

The line between PM and PMM blurs most at launch, and some companies have PMs with no PMMs at all7. Pragmatic Institute draws the line this way. PMs focus on building the product, prioritizing features and managing the roadmap. PMMs face the market: they talk with customers and prospects, track trends and write the go-to-marketGo-to-market: the plan for how a product reaches and wins customers, covering the target, message, pricing, channels and launch. plan, positioning and messaging. How closely the two work together depends on the size of the company7.

Be careful with the brand title if you interview outside tech. In consumer packaged goodsConsumer packaged goods: everyday products like food, drinks, cleaning supplies and personal care items., "brand manager" usually means something bigger: the person who runs a brand as a business, P&LProfit and loss statement: sales at the top, costs below, and profit at the bottom. included.

The job in action: a waitlist for fitness studios

An illustration, not a real company. The numbers are made up to show the work.

You're the product marketer at a booking app used by about 2,000 independent fitness studios. The product team has just built a waitlist: when someone cancels a class, the next person on the list gets the spot and is charged automatically. Now watch the four activities play out in order.

1. Research the customers and the market. Start with your own data. Which studios have the most last-minute cancellations, and which classes sell out? The answer is boutique studios with small classes of 15 spots or fewer. Next, you interview eight of their owners. Today they handle cancellations by texting a list of regulars by hand, or by overbooking and hoping. Both cost them money, because an empty spot in a sold-out class is revenue they never get back.

2. Position and message. Your target is boutique studios with small, full classes. Their real alternative isn't another app. It's the hand-texted list. So the difference to stress is that the spot fills itself and the new client pays without the owner lifting a finger. The message: "Every canceled spot, filled and paid for." The proof: a pilot studio that filled most of its late cancellations in the first month.

3. Launch. This is a feature for existing customers, so it gets a mid-size launch, not a big public one. You write an in-app announcement and an email to owners of studios with sold-out classes. You publish a help article, a one-page guide so support can answer questions, and a 60-second demo. Before launch, you and the PM agree on two goals: the share of eligible studios that turn the waitlist on within 30 days, and the number of spots filled each week.

4. Drive adoption. Two weeks in, something's off. Studios are turning the feature on, but few clients are joining waitlists. You dig into the data and the support tickets and find two problems. The "Join waitlist" button is easy to miss on phones, and clients don't know the feature exists. You take the button problem to the PM with the evidence, and it moves up the roadmap. You also send studios a ready-made email and front-desk sign that tells their clients about the waitlist. A month later, you update the messaging with real results and a quote from a studio owner.

What this shows. Each step fed the next. Research picked the target, the target shaped the message, the launch set goals you could measure, and watching adoption sent a fix back to product. You owned none of the engineering, yet the product still changed because of your work.

Common traps

  • "PMM is the marketing person for the product team." That covers the storyteller job and skips the other three.
  • "PMMs own the roadmap." They shape it with customer insight. The PM makes the call.
  • Treating launch day as the finish line. Adoption after launch is where much of the value shows up.

In an interview

Expect "What is product marketing?" or "How is PMM different from PM?" early on. Give the two-sentence definition, name the four jobs, then ground it all in one real launch you know well. Spend most of your time on that launch. Anyone can memorize a definition.

Self-check: A PM tells you, "PMM just makes the launch deck." Which two parts of the job is that missing?

The deck covers only the storyteller part. Any two of the other three count: bringing customer and market insight back to the product team (ambassador), directing the go-to-market (strategist), and enabling press, pundits and superfans to tell the story (evangelist).

Check your understanding

3 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. A PMM spends a week on sales calls, then writes up what customers keep asking for and shares it with the product team. Which of Lauchengco's four fundamentals is that?

    Show answer

    Ambassador. The ambassador connects customer and market insight and carries it back into the company. The storyteller shapes how buyers understand the product.

  2. A software company has a brand manager, a PM and a PMM. Which project most likely belongs to the PMM?

    Show answer

    Writing the pitch and comparison page for a new analytics feature. The PMM owns positioning, launches and sales enablement for a product. The brand manager owns how the brand looks and what people think of it, and the PM decides what gets built.

  3. A launch is two weeks out. Who usually decides which features make the cut, and who decides how to explain them to the target buyer?

    Show answer

    The PM decides scope; the PMM owns the audience and the message. The PM decides what gets built. The PMM decides who it's for and how the market should understand it.
