Customer proof: case studies, references and demos

PMMLesson 6.412 min

Goal: Pick the right proof for each moment in a deal, and use it in ways buyers believe and regulators accept.

Skills: Writing & storytelling

Ask B2BBusiness-to-business: selling to companies rather than to consumers. buyers who they trust, and the answers form circles. In Forrester's research, the most trusted sources are buyers' own coworkers and managers, at 82%, followed by vendors they already work with, at 79%. Next come independent voices, such as industry peers, analysts, vendor executives and customers, trusted by 66% to 72%. Other outsiders rank lowest1.

A new vendor's salesperson starts in that outer circle. Customer proof is how you borrow trust from someone closer to the middle.

Three kinds of proof

Each kind does a different job at a different moment:

Proof What it is When it helps most
Case study A published story of a customer's results Early, while buyers research on their own
Reference A live conversation with a current customer Late, when a buyer needs to hear it from a peer
Demo The product shown solving the buyer's problem In the middle, once you know what they need

Proof matters most where nobody knows you yet. Risto Salminen and Kristian Möller, reviewing how references are used in business marketing, note that many smaller firms lean on them to overcome a "liability of anonymity" when they enter new overseas markets2.

What makes a case study work

Buyers read case studies looking for themselves. The ones that persuade usually have four things:

  • A customer like the buyer: same industry, similar size, a similar problem.
  • A specific number: "cut onboarding from three weeks to four days" beats "improved efficiency."
  • The before: the problem and what they'd already tried, so the result means something.
  • A real person: a named customer with a title makes it believable.

The best story for a deal isn't always your biggest logo. A 40-person company may trust a story about another 40-person company more than one about a household name.

References without burning out your best customers

Your happiest customers get asked constantly, so treat references as a scarce resource:

  • Match on industry, size and use case, not just on who's available.
  • Brief the customer on who's calling and what they care about.
  • Track how often you ask each one, and spread the load.
  • Thank them in ways that matter to them, like early access or a speaking slot, and know that some perks have to be disclosed.

Demos: show the result first

Peter Cohan's Great Demo! method starts with a reversal. Show the most valuable result first, which he calls "Do the Last Thing First," then show how you got there3.

Before a demo, Cohan recommends filling in a Situation Slide with six items: the person's job title, their critical business issue, the problems making it hard to solve, the specific capabilities they need, the "delta" (the tangible value of making the change) and the critical date they need a solution by. The specific capabilities tell you exactly what to show and what to leave out3.

A demo that tours every feature makes the buyer figure out what matters. A demo built from a Situation Slide does that work for them.

The rules

Published customer proof, like case studies and testimonials on your website, is advertising. The FTC's Endorsement Guides explain how it applies the law to endorsements, and two points matter most for PMMs4:

  • Typical results. If a featured customer's results aren't representative of what customers generally get, the ad should clearly disclose the generally expected performance, and you need evidence to back that figure.
  • Hidden connections. If an endorser has a connection to you that the audience wouldn't expect and that could affect how much they trust the endorsement, it has to be disclosed clearly and conspicuously.

A discount given in exchange for a case study is the kind of connection worth checking with your legal team.

Reviews have their own rule. Since October 21, 2024, the FTC's rule on consumer reviews and testimonials has let courts impose civil penalties for knowing violations involving fake or deceptive reviews and testimonials5.

Common traps

  • Leading with your biggest logo when the buyer looks nothing like it.
  • Vague results. "Improved efficiency" proves nothing.
  • The feature-tour demo. If it isn't tied to their problem, it's noise.
  • Overusing your happiest customer. References get tired too.

In an interview

If asked how you'd build a customer proof program, start with where deals stall and what proof would get them moving. Then cover how you'd find and document stories, how you'd match references to prospects, and how you'd keep every claim accurate.

Self-check: One customer cut costs 60%, but most customers see about 15%. Marketing wants to headline the 60%. What do you advise?

Feature the story if you like, but say clearly that it isn't typical and what customers generally achieve, around 15%. Without that, the headline implies 60% is normal, which the FTC's guides warn against. Better yet, lead the main campaign with a story closer to typical, and save the 60% for buyers whose situation actually matches.

Check your understanding

4 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. A 40-person accounting firm is evaluating your software. Which case study is most likely to persuade them?

    Show answer

    A 50-person accounting firm's story with a specific result. Buyers look for customers like themselves. A similar company with a real number beats a famous logo.

  2. You have 30 minutes to demo for a CFO whose biggest problem is a slow month-end close. Where do you start?

    Show answer

    The finished close report, then how it got there. Great Demo! shows the most valuable result first, so the CFO sees why each later step matters.

  3. Your best reference customer got a 30% discount in exchange for doing case studies. What does that mean for your marketing?

    Show answer

    The connection may need to be disclosed, since the audience wouldn't expect it. The FTC's guides require clear disclosure of unexpected connections that could affect how much people trust an endorsement.

  4. A buyer is in the final stage of a deal and nervous about risk. Which proof helps most?

    Show answer

    A live call with a current customer in a similar situation. Late in a deal, hearing it from a peer carries the most weight. Buyers trust independent voices more than a vendor they don't know yet.
