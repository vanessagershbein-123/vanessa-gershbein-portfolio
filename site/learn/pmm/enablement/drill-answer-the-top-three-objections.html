Answer the top three objections

PMMDrill 6.212 minIntermediate

Skills: Writing & storytelling

The prompt

The facts.

  • Microsoft Bookings comes with many Microsoft 365 and Office 365 plans, including Microsoft 365 Business Basic, Business Standard and Business Premium and the E3 and E5 enterprise plans. It offers personal booking pages that anyone can use to book time, plus shared booking pages for teams1. A Bookings page supports up to 100 staff members1.
  • Calendly's Standard plan costs $10 per seat a month and Teams costs $16, billed yearly. Teams adds round-robin and team scheduling, lead qualification and routing, sending meetings to Salesforce and an optional single sign-on add-on. Seats are needed only for people who host meetings, and invitees don't need one. Enterprise starts at $15,000 a year2.
  • Calendly lists SOC 2 Type 2, SOC 3, ISO/IEC 27001 and other certifications, and offers documentation for customers running security reviews or procurement assessments2.

Your task: You're the PMM supporting Calendly's sales team. A 300-person company on Microsoft 365 Business Premium wants scheduling for its 40-person sales development team, which books meetings with prospects and routes them to account executives. Three objections keep coming up:

  1. "We already get Bookings with Microsoft 365. Why pay for Calendly?"
  2. "$16 a seat for 300 people? No way."
  3. "Security will never approve another vendor."

For each, write the question a rep should ask first, a short answer and the proof to use.

Your earlier answer

Sample answers

1. "We already get Bookings."

  • Ask first: "What does your team need to happen after a meeting is booked?"
  • Answer: Bookings covers plenty of simple scheduling, and the rep shouldn't pretend otherwise. The case for Calendly rests on what this team does all day: routing leads to the right rep, round-robin across 40 people and sending meetings to Salesforce. Check what Bookings offers today before claiming any gap.
  • Proof: a short trial with five reps, measured on meetings booked and time spent routing.

2. "$16 a seat for 300 people."

  • Ask first: "Who actually hosts the meetings?"
  • Answer: Only people who host meetings need seats: the 40 SDRs plus the account executives who take the routed meetings, still far below 300. With 20 account executives, that's 60 seats × $16 × 12 = $11,520 a year. Compare that with the value of one extra qualified meeting per rep each month.
  • Proof: the seat rule on the pricing page, and a simple cost-per-meeting calculation.

3. "Security won't approve it."

  • Ask first: "What does your security team usually need to see?"
  • Answer: Send the certifications and trust documentation before they're requested, and offer single sign-on through the Teams add-on.
  • Proof: the security documentation itself, plus a reference customer in a similar industry.

Why these work

  • Each starts with a question, which turns a flat no into a problem you can solve.
  • The seat math reframes price around the people who actually need seats.
  • Nobody badmouths Microsoft. The case rests on this team's workflow.

What interviewers push on

  • "What if Bookings really does cover their needs?" Then say so, and offer Calendly only for the team where it clearly wins. Credibility pays off in the next deal.
  • "Where does PMM come in?" Turn these three answers into a battlecardA short internal guide that helps sales handle a specific competitor: where you win, where you lose and how to answer objections. section with proof attached, and update it as reps hear new versions.

Practice twist

The buyer's IT lead says, "Let's wait and see what Microsoft adds next year." How do you answer a "wait for the bundle" objection?
