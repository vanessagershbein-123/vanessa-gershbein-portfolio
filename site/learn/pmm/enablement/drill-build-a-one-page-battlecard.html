Build a one-page battlecard

PMMDrill 6.115 minIntermediate

Skills: Writing & storytellingPositioning & strategy

The prompt

The facts.

  • Toast describes itself as a cloud-based, all-in-one platform built for the entire restaurant community, which it calls the restaurant operating system. It had about 164,000 locations at the end of 2025, up 22%, and estimates that its US restaurants make up about 20% of the US restaurant market. It's starting to expand into food and beverage retail. Its annual report doesn't name rivals. It says it competes on an all-in-one software and payments platform with partner integrations, on ease of use and reliability, on brand and customer satisfaction, on support and onboarding, and on low-cost integration1.
  • Toast's Starter Kit costs $0 a month for single-location restaurants and is paid for through a 3.09% card processing fee. Its traditional option has restaurants buy hardware up front and pay monthly for software, with a 2.49% processing fee2. The Point of Sale plan starts at $69 a month, and contracts typically run 12 to 36 months, then renew automatically one year at a time2.
  • Square for Restaurants has a free plan charging 2.6% plus 15 cents per in-person payment, Plus at $49 a month per location (2.5% plus 15 cents) and Premium at $149 (2.4% plus 15 cents). Plus and Premium come with a 30-day free trial, and hardware starts at $593.
  • Block, Square's parent company, says it differentiates on its broad commerce ecosystem, transparent pricing and no mandatory long-term contracts. Square's payment volume grew 10% in 2025, driven mainly by food and beverage sellers, and Block has been building a field sales team to win larger sellers4.
  • CNBC reported in May 2025 that Block faces growing competition from rivals including Toast and Fiserv's Clover, though Square still gained share in food and beverage5.

Your task: You're the PMM at Toast. Sales keeps running into Square at small, independent restaurants opening their first location. Build a one-page battlecardA short internal guide that helps sales handle a specific competitor: where you win, where you lose and how to answer objections.: why Toast wins, where Square is strong, the questions reps should ask, quick answers to what Square's reps will say about Toast, and the proof you'd want.

Your earlier answer

Sample battlecard

Square in one line: an easy, low-commitment way to start taking payments, with a free plan, a 30-day trial on paid plans and no mandatory contract.

Why Toast wins

  • Built for restaurants first, so kitchen, menu and online ordering tools fit how restaurants actually run.
  • One system for front and back of house, with a large partner ecosystem.
  • Restaurant-specific onboarding and support, which its annual report lists among the ways it competes.

Where Square is strong, and reps should say so

  • No mandatory long-term contract, and a free plan.
  • Simple, published processing rates.
  • A familiar brand for owners who already use Square somewhere else.

Questions to ask

  • "How many orders do you expect on a Friday night, and how will they get to the kitchen?"
  • "Are you planning a second location?"
  • "How will you handle online orders and delivery?"

Quick answers

  • "Toast locks you into a contract." Be straight: terms often run 12 to 36 months. Then show what the term buys, such as restaurant-specific onboarding and support, and compare total cost over the same period.
  • "Square is cheaper." Toast has a $0-a-month Starter Kit too, so compare the full monthly cost at the restaurant's real volume: processing, including any per-transaction fee, plus software and hardware, not just the plan price.

Proof to get: a story from a single-location restaurant that switched from Square, with a number that matters to owners, like faster ticket times or fewer order errors.

What the public record shows

Both companies are reaching for the same small restaurant. Toast leans on being built for restaurants. Block's annual report leans on transparent pricing and no mandatory contracts, and food and beverage sellers drove Square's growth in 2025. Toast's own pricing shows its answer to "cheaper to start": a $0 Starter Kit for single locations.

Why this card works

  • It concedes Square's real strengths, which makes the rep believable.
  • The questions steer the conversation to restaurant operations, where Toast is strongest.
  • The answers compare total cost and value instead of attacking.

What interviewers push on

  • "Isn't the contract a real weakness?" For some owners, yes. The card should help reps qualify: a restaurant unsure it'll last a year is a poor fit for a long term.
  • "How would you keep this current?" Recheck both pricing pages every month, and update the card after each round of win/loss interviews.

Practice twist

Suppose Square launched a 12-month price lock for new restaurants, still with no contract. What would change on your card?
