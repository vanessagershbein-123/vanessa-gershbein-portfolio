Running competitive intelligence

PMMLesson 6.312 min

Goal: Learn what rivals are doing from sources you can stand behind, and turn it into something sales and product can use.

Skills: Research & insight

In March 2025, the HR software company Rippling sued its fiercest rival, Deel, with an allegation straight out of a spy novel. According to the lawsuit, Rippling suspected an employee of passing information to Deel. So it created a fake Slack channel, told key Deel executives its name along with a hint that it held embarrassing details about Deel, and waited to see whether the employee would search for it1. The filing says the employee had viewed and downloaded information on how to compete against Deel, and had also seen material on sales calls and demos. Deel denied all legal wrongdoing2.

Then the accusations ran the other way. In a June 2025 filing, Deel alleged that a Rippling employee whose job title was Competitive Intelligence had spent six months impersonating a Deel customer to study its products. Rippling said it was looking into the allegations and that it's committed to fair competition3. The case was still going in September 2026, when a federal judge rejected Deel's bid to strike the key witness's testimony and sent Deel's most serious counterclaims to arbitration. Deel has denied the spying claims4.

Look at what was allegedly worth taking: material on how to win deals against a rival. A legitimate competitive intelligence program produces exactly that every day, from sources nobody has to sneak into.

Where good intelligence comes from

Competitive intelligence, or CI, is the work of learning what rivals are doing and turning it into decisions your company can act on. Most of it is public. The FTC's antitrust guidance answers the obvious question directly: collecting price and other competitive information from public sources, such as trade journals, securities filings and press releases, and circulating it inside your company is fine5.

Each source tells you something different:

Source What it tells you Watch out for
Win/loss interviews Why buyers actually chose you or a rival Small samples, and buyers being polite
Rivals' annual reports Strategy, segments and how they say they compete Written for investors, so it's polished
Pricing pages and release notes What they charge and what they just shipped Pages change, so save dated copies
Job postings Where they're investing next One posting isn't a strategy
Your own sales calls What buyers are hearing from rivals Reps remember losses better than wins
Trade shows Demos and how they pitch Ask openly, as yourself

Annual reports are underrated. SEC rules ask public companies to describe their business, and that description may cover trends in market demand and competitive conditions6. That's why many 10-Ks include a section on who the company competes with and on what basis, in its own words.

Where the line is

The lines are clearer than the Rippling and Deel fight makes them look. SCIP, the professional association for intelligence work, publishes a seven-point code of ethics. Two points carry most of the weight: comply with all applicable laws, and disclose your identity and organization before every interview7.

Pragmatic Institute's guide to CI guardrails shows what that means in practice. At a trade show, walking up to a rival's booth and asking questions is fine. Pretending to be someone else, like a customer, while you do it is not. Fraud generally means misrepresenting yourself or your purpose to get information, and trade secret violations carry steep penalties. For gray areas, its rule of thumb is "when in doubt, leave it out"8.

There's a line running the other direction too. The same FTC guidance warns that your company's future plans, such as an upcoming price increase, should be closely guarded. Sharing them outside the company, even with a consultant or analyst who also talks to your rivals, could change competitors' decisions and raise antitrust concerns5.

Before you gather anything, a quick test:

  • Would you be comfortable if the rival knew exactly how you got it?
  • Did you say who you are and who you work for?
  • Is it public, or does someone owe their employer confidentiality?
  • Are you giving away anything about your own future prices or plans?

Turning intel into something people use

A folder of screenshots helps nobody. Intelligence matters when it changes what someone does:

  • For sales: an updated battlecardA short internal guide that helps sales handle a specific competitor: where you win, where you lose and how to answer objections., or a heads-up the day a rival changes its pricing.
  • For product: patterns from win/loss interviews about why deals are lost.
  • For leadership: a short, regular summary of what moved and what it means.

Keep a steady rhythm, like a monthly digest, plus quick alerts for big moves such as a price change or an acquisition. Separate what you know from what you're guessing, and say which is which.

Common traps

  • Collecting everything, using nothing. Start from a decision someone needs to make.
  • Watching only the famous rival. Buyers often compare you with a spreadsheet or the way they work today.
  • Treating rumors as facts. Label how confident you are.
  • Crossing the line for an edge. Misrepresentation can cost far more than it gains.

In an interview

If asked how you'd set up competitive intelligence, start with the decisions it should feed, such as battlecards, pricing or the roadmap. Name your sources, starting with win/loss interviews and public filings, explain how you'd share what you find, and mention the ethical lines you'd hold.

Self-check: A teammate suggests signing up for a rival's free trial under a made-up company name, "just to see the product." What do you say?

Don't. Using a fake identity to get information is misrepresentation, the line Pragmatic draws on posing as someone else, and it may break the rival's terms of service. It's also close to what Deel accused a Rippling competitive-intelligence employee of doing. Sign up as yourself if the rival allows it, or learn the product from public demos, documentation and buyers who've used it.

Check your understanding

4 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. You want to know how a public rival describes its own competitive strengths. Where's the best first place to look?

    Show answer

    The competition section of its annual report. Public filings often describe competition in the company's own words, and reading them is fair game.

  2. At a trade show, how should you learn about a rival's new product?

    Show answer

    Ask questions at their booth as yourself. Asking openly is fine. Pretending to be a customer is misrepresentation.

  3. An industry consultant asks about your company's plan to raise prices next quarter. What do you do?

    Show answer

    Decline, because future pricing shared outside the company can reach rivals and raise antitrust concerns. The FTC warns that future plans should be closely guarded, including from go-betweens like consultants and analysts.

  4. Your monthly CI digest runs 30 pages and nobody reads it. What's the best fix?

    Show answer

    Cut it to what changed and what each team should do about it. Intelligence matters when it changes a decision. A short summary tied to actions gets used.
