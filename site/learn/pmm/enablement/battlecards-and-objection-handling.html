Battlecards and objection handling

PMMLesson 6.212 min

Goal: Build a battlecard reps actually use, and answer objections with proof instead of pressure.

Skills: Writing & storytellingPositioning & strategy

This might surprise you: the US Federal Trade Commission encourages companies to name their competitors. Its policy on comparative advertising, on the books since 1979, says that disparaging a rival is permissible as long as it's truthful and not deceptive1. A rival can still challenge a comparison it thinks is false, so have the proof in hand before the claim goes out.

Permission isn't the same as good strategy, though. Wharton's Pinar Yildirim, with Gorkem Bostanci and Kinshuk Jerath, studied what negative advertising does to a market. The threat of attacks pushes companies toward safer, more similar products, which cuts variety and can slow innovation. Yildirim also points out the boomerang: if your product is much like a rival's and you highlight its weakness, you probably share the same flaw2.

A good battlecardA short internal guide that helps sales handle a specific competitor: where you win, where you lose and how to answer objections. strikes that balance. It helps a rep win against one specific rival, honestly, without turning the call into a takedown.

What goes on a battlecard

A battlecard is a one-page brief on a single competitor, written for a rep in the middle of a deal. It answers what a rep needs to know the moment a buyer says, "We're also looking at X." A good one fits on a screen and covers six things. The example lines are illustrative:

Section What it answers Example line
Who they are Who they sell to and how they win "Wins small teams on price and a fast setup"
Why we win Your two or three strongest differences, each with proof "Customers like Y cut setup from six weeks to two"
Where they're strong What to concede honestly "Their mobile app gets better reviews"
Questions to ask Discovery questions that bring out your strengths "How will approvals work once you pass 50 users?"
Quick answers Responses to what they say about you "They'll call us expensive. Here's the three-year cost."
Proof Customers who switched, with numbers A reference customer in the buyer's industry

The "where they're strong" row is the one teams often skip. A rep who admits a rival's real strength sounds believable when making the case for everything else.

Objections aren't FAQs

PostHog's public handbook draws a line worth borrowing. An FAQ is a question about the product, such as how pricing works. An objection is a reason a customer gives not to buy, such as "We can build this ourselves for less." Objections are the valuable part. The handbook tells product marketers to collect them from customer calls and the sales team, keep updating battlecards as new ones appear, and answer honestly, even when the honest answer is "not yet"3.

Most objections belong to a handful of families:

Objection What's often behind it What answers it
"It's too expensive." The value isn't clear yet, or the budget sits with someone else The cost of the problem next to the price
"We're fine with what we have." Switching feels riskier than staying What staying costs, and a low-risk way to start
"We can build it ourselves." The upkeep is underestimated The full cost to build and maintain it
"Your competitor says..." A rival planted a doubt Facts and proof, not a counterattack
"Security won't approve it." A real requirement, or a polite no Certifications and documents ready before anyone asks
"Not this quarter." No urgency, or no budget yet A reason the timing matters, or a smaller first step

Handling one in the moment

Clever comebacks rarely win. A short routine works better:

  1. Hear the whole thing. Cutting in tells the buyer you weren't listening.
  2. Ask what's behind it. "Expensive compared with what?" turns a wall into a problem you can work on.
  3. Answer with proof. A customer result or a document beats an adjective.
  4. Check that it landed. "Does that cover it?" An objection left unresolved comes back later, usually at the worst time.

Teach, don't just defend

The strongest objection handling starts before any objection comes up. In "The End of Solution Sales," Brent Adamson, Matthew Dixon and Nicholas Toman of the Corporate Executive Board describe star reps who get in early with provocative ideas about what the customer should do, instead of waiting for the buyer to define the problem. They seek out skeptical change agents over friendly informants, and they end up selling insights, not just solutions4.

For a battlecard, that means going beyond rebuttals. Give reps one insight that reframes the buyer's problem in a way that plays to your strengths, so the comparison happens on your ground.

Common traps

  • The takedown card. Attacks make a rep sound defensive, and claims you can't back up can get you in trouble.
  • The novel. If it doesn't fit on a screen, nobody reads it mid-call.
  • The stale card. Rivals ship features and change prices. A six-month-old card can be flat wrong.
  • Rebuttals without proof. "That's not true" convinces nobody. A customer's number does.

In an interview

If you're asked how you'd help sales beat a competitor, start with where deals are lost and why, using win/loss interviews and call recordings. Then describe the battlecard, how you'd keep it current and how you'd know it's working, such as win rate against that rival.

Self-check: A rep wants a battlecard line saying the rival's product "crashes constantly." You've seen a handful of complaints online. What do you do?

Leave it out unless you can prove it. A claim about a rival has to be truthful and not deceptive, and a handful of complaints doesn't show "constantly." Even a true attack can backfire, especially if your product shares the same weakness. Give the rep a discovery question about the buyer's reliability needs instead, plus proof of your own uptime.

Check your understanding

4 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. A buyer says, "Your rival told us you're twice their price." What's the best first move?

    Show answer

    Ask what they're comparing, then show the full cost and what it buys. Asking what's behind an objection turns it into a problem you can solve. Proof works better than a counterattack.

  2. Which line belongs in a battlecard's "where they're strong" section?

    Show answer

    "Their mobile app gets better reviews than ours.". Conceding a real strength makes the rep credible. Unsupported attacks do the opposite.

  3. Which of these is an objection rather than an FAQ?

    Show answer

    "We can build this ourselves for less.". An FAQ asks about the product. An objection is a reason not to buy, and it's where reps need the most help.

  4. A battlecard is nothing but rebuttals to a rival's claims. Based on research on star reps, what's it missing?

    Show answer

    An insight that reframes the buyer's problem in a way that plays to your strengths. Star reps get in early with ideas about what the buyer should do. Rebuttals only defend, while an insight shapes the comparison.
