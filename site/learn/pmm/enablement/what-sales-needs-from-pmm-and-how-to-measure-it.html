What sales needs from PMM, and how to measure it

PMMLesson 6.112 min

Goal: Give reps what they need at each stage of a deal, and show that it changed how they sell.

Skills: Writing & storytellingData & charts

In a 2026 Gartner survey of 646 B2BBusiness-to-business: selling to companies rather than to consumers. buyers, 67% said they'd prefer to buy without a sales rep at all1. So the time a rep does get with a buyer is scarce, and it has to count. Reps don't have much time to spare either. In Salesforce's 2026 State of Sales survey of 4,050 sales professionals, the average seller spent 40% of their time actually selling2.

That's the real brief for sales enablement: make the few minutes a rep gets with a buyer count, and take enough busywork off the rep's plate that they get more of those minutes.

What reps need, stage by stage

Sales enablement covers everything that helps reps sell better, from onboarding and training to content and tools. Product marketing usually owns the product and market side: what to say, who to say it to, how to beat competitors and what proof to show. The easiest way to see what reps need is to walk through a deal:

Stage What the rep is trying to do What PMM gives them
Finding accounts Pick who to call The customer profiles that fit, and signals that an account is ready
First call Earn a second meeting A short talk track: the problem, who it's for, why now
Discovery Learn what the buyer needs Questions that surface the problems your product solves
Demo Show it working A demo flow that opens with the result the buyer cares about
Evaluation Survive the comparison Battlecards, objection answers and proof
Decision Help the buyer sell it internally A business case, pricing guidance and references

Notice how little of that is a slide deck. Most of it is an answer to a question a buyer asks at a specific moment.

Worked example: PostHog's playbook

PostHog, which makes product analytics and other tools for software teams, publishes its company handbook online, including how its product marketers do enablement. There, product marketers make the sales material for the products they own. A few of their rules3:

  • Start with the hardest product to sell. They ask the go-to-marketGo-to-market: the plan for how a product reaches and wins customers, covering the target, message, pricing, channels and launch. team to rank products from easiest to hardest to sell, then work on the hardest first. Visual products like session replay are easier to pitch. Products with little to show on screen, like a data warehouse, need the help.
  • Give signals, not call lists. Reps know their accounts better than marketing does. So instead of telling them who to call, PMMs explain which customer profiles fit and which signals show an account matches: rising use of a related product, a fresh funding round, a new hire in the right role.
  • Explain why it's worth selling. A rough number for what a customer might spend helps reps see how the product gets them to quota.
  • Demo the result, not the setup. For a product that's hard to show, demo the answer it gives. Watching data sync convinces nobody.

Training follows the same logic. One PostHog session had 15 to 20 minutes of content and 45 minutes of questions, and the questions turned out to be the most useful part, because they showed what reps didn't understand and what to write next. The handbook even asks PMMs to write material that the AI agents reps use to prepare for calls can read3.

Partners need enablement too

Sometimes the people selling your product don't work for you. A 2026 Stanford GSB case on Cumulus Networks, a networking software start-up, shows the price. Partners could close the gap between Cumulus's software and what corporate IT teams could set up on their own, but each partner cost a share of every sale plus months of training, joint selling and engineering support from a small team4.

A partner channel multiplies your enablement work. Each partner's reps need the same talk tracks, demos and objection answers your own team gets, while they juggle other vendors' products at the same time.

How to tell if it's working

Enablement is easy to do and hard to prove. In a 2023 Gartner survey, 77% of sellers said they struggle to complete their assigned tasks efficiently, even at companies investing in enablement. Gartner's advice is to measure which specific behaviors enablement changed, then tie those changes to sales goals5.

Think of it as three questions, each harder to answer than the last. The examples use illustrative numbers:

Question What to measure Example
Did reps use it? Views, and which assets reps send to buyers 70% of reps opened the new battlecard in its first month
Did it change how they sell? What happens on calls Call recordings show reps asking the new discovery questions
Did it move deals? Win rate, deal speed, deal size, how fast new reps close a first deal Win rate against the main rival rose from 30% to 38% over two quarters

The first row is the easiest to measure and tells you the least. The third is what leadership cares about, but deals take months and plenty besides your battlecardA short internal guide that helps sales handle a specific competitor: where you win, where you lose and how to answer objections. moves a win rate. Usage and behavior are your early signals, and results confirm them later.

Common traps

  • A content library nobody opens. If reps can't find it mid-call, it might as well not exist.
  • Training once, at launch. Objections change as rivals respond, so the material has to keep up.
  • Counting downloads as success. Usage shows interest, not impact.
  • Writing for marketers. Reps need the answer to the buyer's question in a line or two, not your positioning document.

In an interview

For "How would you enable sales for this launch?", walk a deal from first call to close and name what reps need at each stage. Then say how you'd train them, how you'd collect what they hear from buyers, and the one or two numbers you'd watch to know it worked.

Self-check: Your team built 40 assets for a launch. Three months later, reps have opened four of them and win rates haven't moved. What would you do?

Ask reps which buyer questions they can't answer, and sit in on a few calls. The four assets they use show what's valuable, so improve those and retire most of the rest. Then rebuild around the stage where deals stall, and look for behavior change on calls before expecting win rates to move.

Check your understanding

4 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. A rep says, "Just tell me which accounts to call." Following PostHog's approach, what do you give them?

    Show answer

    The customer profiles that fit, and the signals that show an account is ready. Reps know their accounts better than marketing does. Profiles and buying signals help them spot the right moment themselves.

  2. You're running a one-hour training on a new product. How should you split the time?

    Show answer

    Keep the content short and leave most of the time for questions. Reps' questions reveal what they don't understand and what to write next. PostHog's session ran 15 to 20 minutes of content and 45 of questions, and the questions were the most useful part.

  3. Which result best shows that a new battlecard is working?

    Show answer

    Reps who use it win more often against that rival than reps who don't, and call recordings show it in use. Downloads and ratings show interest. A change in behavior followed by a change in results shows impact.

  4. Your company starts selling through three resellers. What happens to your enablement work?

    Show answer

    It grows, because each partner's reps need training, talk tracks and support too. Partners extend your reach, but each needs the enablement your own reps get, often while selling other vendors' products too.
