Packaging: good-better-best, freemium and usage-based

PMMLesson 5.212 min

Goal: Decide what goes in each plan, when a free plan pays for itself, and when to charge for usage instead of seats.

Skills: Pricing & promotion

When Dropbox filed to go public in 2018, it had more than 500 million registered users in 180 countries. Only about 11 million of them paid. The free version, Dropbox Basic, wasn't a leak in that funnel. It was the funnel: Dropbox called it "a major funnel" for its paid plans, and more than 90% of its revenue came from people who bought through the app or website on their own1. The gap held: by the end of 2025, Dropbox served more than 700 million registered users, and 18.08 million of them paid1.

That split between free and paid was designed. So is every pricing page you've ever scrolled. Deciding what goes in each plan, what's free and what you charge for is called packaging. Price is the number. Packaging is everything the number buys.

Why three plans usually beat one

Rafi Mohammed opens his Harvard Business Review case for tiered pricing with car insurance. The industry long assumed drivers just wanted the cheapest plan. Allstate's research in the early 2000s found something more interesting: price mattered, but many drivers worried about their premium jumping after an accident, and drivers with clean records wanted credit for it2. One price can't answer all of those worries. A few tiers can.

That's the idea behind good-better-best. Instead of one product at one price, you offer a basic version, a fuller one and a premium one, so each kind of buyer finds a plan built for them.

Three also works because of how people choose. Researchers call it the compromise effect: an option gains share when it sits in the middle of a set instead of at one end3. Shown three plans, plenty of buyers skip the cheapest because it feels risky and the priciest because it feels like too much. So build the middle plan as the one you most want people to buy, and let the top plan make it look reasonable.

What goes in which plan

Naming three plans is easy. Deciding which features sit behind which price is the real work. A useful rule: put a feature in a higher plan when it's worth a lot to the customers who'd pay more and very little to everyone else. Those features work as fences. They keep the customers who need them from buying down.

Zapier, the automation tool, shows the pattern4:

Plan Price (billed yearly) Built for What it adds
Free $0 Anyone trying their first automations Two-step Zaps
Professional From $19.99 a month Solo builders Multi-step Zaps, premium apps, webhooks
Team From $69 a month Small teams 25 users, shared Zaps and folders, shared app connections, SAML SSO
Enterprise Contact sales Large organizations Unlimited users, advanced admin controls, a technical account manager

Look at what Team adds. Nothing on that list helps one person automate more. It's all sharing, security and control, which only matter once several people depend on the same workflows. Single sign-on is the classic fence: the IT team at a 200-person company insists on it, and a freelancer never thinks about it. It's also a common complaint, sometimes called the "SSO tax," so weigh the extra revenue against annoying security-minded smaller customers.

Zapier also prices along a second line. Every plan comes in task tiers, from 100 tasks a month up to 2 million. Hit your limit and you switch to pay-as-you-go, unless you turn that off or move up a tier, and the higher the tier, the less each task costs4. The plan sets what you can do. The tier sets how much of it.

Free: a funnel or a giveaway?

Every free plan raises the same worry: will people who would have paid just stay free? Deng, Lambrecht and Liu tested that on game apps in Apple's App Store that added a free version next to an existing paid one. Apple's review process made each free launch date hard to predict, so the timing worked like a natural experiment. In that market, the free version didn't eat the paid one. It let people sample the game first, and demand for the paid version went up, with its daily ratings rising 8.9% at the average5.

Dropbox's free plan worked for a similar reason. It was useful on its own, and its limit grew with use: the more you stored and shared, the closer you got to needing more space. A good free plan passes three tests:

  • It's useful by itself, so people keep it and bring others in.
  • Its limit grows with value, such as storage, tasks or users, so the people who hit it are the ones getting the most out of the product.
  • It's cheap to serve, because most free users will never pay.

Usage-based: charge for what customers use

Some products don't fit neat plans at all. Twilio sells the building blocks for texts, calls and email inside other companies' apps, and the majority of its revenue is usage-based. Most of those customers have no long-term commitment and can cut back or stop at any time without penalty. That cuts both ways. When customers grow, revenue grows with them, no sales call needed. Twilio tracks this with a dollar-based net expansion rate, which compares what existing customers spend now with what they spent a year earlier, and for its Communications business the rate was 105% in 2024. When customers shrink, revenue shrinks too, and Twilio lists exactly that among its risks6.

The big decision in usage pricing is the value metric, the unit you charge for. A good one:

  • Grows as the customer gets more value, like messages sent, tasks run or records stored.
  • Is easy for a buyer to understand and estimate before the bill arrives.
  • Tracks your own costs closely enough that heavy users don't lose you money.

AI features push many products toward meters, because every request costs something to run. Many companies settle on a hybrid: a plan with an included amount, plus pay-as-you-go above it. Zapier's task tiers are exactly that.

Picking a model

Treat this as a rule of thumb, not a law:

Model Works best when Watch out for
Good-better-best Buyers differ a lot in what they need and will pay Too many tiers, or fences nobody cares about
Freemium The product spreads through use and is cheap to serve A free plan so generous nobody upgrades
Usage-based Value rises with volume, and volume is easy to measure Bills nobody can predict, and revenue that drops when customers cut back
Hybrid (plan plus usage) You want predictable revenue with room to grow Two price dimensions for buyers to understand

Common traps

  • Fences nobody needs. If the premium plan's extras don't matter to big customers, they'll happily buy the cheaper plan.
  • A free plan that's too good. If the free version covers what most paying customers need, upgrades stall.
  • A meter customers can't predict. When buyers can't estimate next month's bill, finance pushes back, or customers cap their own usage.
  • Copying a competitor's page. Their tiers fit their customers and their costs, not necessarily yours.

In an interview

Packaging questions often arrive as "Should X add a free plan?" or "How would you package X's new AI feature?" Start with who the customers are and how they differ. Then pick the model that matches those differences, and name the number you'd watch to know it's working, such as free-to-paid conversion or expansion revenue.

Self-check: A project-management tool's middle plan adds 40 features over its basic plan, but almost everyone buys basic. What's likely wrong, and what would you check?

The fences probably aren't fences. If basic covers what most teams need, piling features into the middle plan won't push anyone up. Check which features paying customers actually use, then move the one or two that matter most to bigger teams, such as admin controls, permissions or SSO, into the middle plan so it becomes the obvious choice for a growing team.

Check your understanding

4 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. A tool sells Basic at $10 and Pro at $30, and most buyers pick Basic. It adds an Enterprise plan at $90 and leaves the other two alone. What does the compromise effect predict?

    Show answer

    More buyers choose Pro, now that it's the middle option. An option gains share when it sits in the middle of a set. Part of the top plan's job can be making the middle one look reasonable, though in B2B the top plan is often a big revenue line in its own right.

  2. Zapier's Team plan adds 25 users, shared Zaps, shared app connections and SAML SSO. Why put those features there?

    Show answer

    They matter a lot to teams and little to individuals, so teams can't get by on the cheaper plan. Good fences are features whose value rises with the customer's size or complexity.

  3. Your team sells a paid app and fears a free version will steal sales. What's the strongest case for launching one anyway?

    Show answer

    It lets people sample the product, and some who like it go on to buy the full version. When Deng, Lambrecht and Liu studied games that added a free version, demand for the paid version went up, not down. Sampling worked as marketing.

  4. Most of Twilio's revenue is usage-based. What risk comes with that?

    Show answer

    Revenue falls when customers cut back, since most have no long-term commitment. Usage pricing grows with customers, and it shrinks with them too. Twilio lists lower usage as a risk to its revenue.
