Pricing research and price changes

PMMLesson 5.312 min

Goal: Pick the right way to research willingness to pay, and plan a price increase customers can live with.

Skills: Pricing & promotionResearch & insight

Ask streaming subscribers whether a $5 price hike would make them cancel their favorite service, and 60% say they'd likely do it, according to Deloitte's Digital Media Trends survey. The average subscribing household already pays for four services1. Stated answers like that aren't a hard ceiling, but they're a clear warning about how much patience customers have left.

Price changes are where pricing theory meets real customers, and they go better when the research comes first and the change arrives in a shape people can absorb.

Why "what would you pay?" fails

Kellogg researchers Sharlene He, Eric Anderson and Derek Rucker point out that many studies have measured willingness to pay by simply asking people what they'd spend, with no details about the situation or the alternatives. Their fix, the Comparative Method of Valuation, measures value against the options a buyer would actually weigh. A KitKat in a vending machine competes with other candy bars. In a grocery store, it also competes with apples and crackers. The researchers found that conjoint analysis, another popular method, is an efficient way to explore many contexts at once, while their comparative method does better when the alternatives are clear2.

The takeaway: research price in context, against the alternatives buyers really have.

Ways to research willingness to pay

Van Westendorp's price sensitivity meter. Peter van Westendorp introduced it in 1976, and it's been a market research staple ever since3. It asks four questions:

  • At what price would this be so expensive you wouldn't consider it?
  • At what price would it be so cheap you'd doubt the quality?
  • At what price is it getting expensive, but you'd still consider it?
  • At what price is it a bargain?

Plot everyone's answers, and the points where the curves cross mark out a range of acceptable prices. Researcher Mike Pritchard warns against treating any single crossing as the "optimal" price, since pricing research is usually just one input to the decision. He also contrasts this direct approach with indirect ones like conjoint, which show people whole packages of features and prices and work out from their choices how much the price matters3.

Testing in the market. The strongest evidence is what people actually pay. Offer a new price to new customers in one region or channel, then compare conversion and retention with everyone else.

Each method answers a different question:

Method What it tells you Watch out for
Asking directly A rough gut check People answer without alternatives in mind
Van Westendorp A range of acceptable prices Stated answers, with competitors left out of the frame
Conjoint What each feature and the price are worth, relative to each other Needs a careful design and a decent sample
Comparative method Value against the specific alternatives buyers weigh You have to know the real alternatives first
Market test What people really pay Slower, and customers notice when prices differ

Raising prices without a revolt

Kellogg's Suraj Malladi explains why so many companies raise prices in small steps. Firms act as if demand is "kinked": cutting prices barely brings in more customers, but raising them can drive customers away fast. For businesses that set one price and change it rarely, he says, the order of price changes matters4.

A price increase plan answers six questions:

Question What good looks like
Why now? New value customers can see, or costs they'd recognize
How much? Small enough to absorb; big jumps invite comparison shopping
Who's affected? New customers first, or existing customers with time to adjust
Where can they land? A cheaper plan to step down to instead of canceling
How will they hear? Early, plain notice from you, not a surprise on a bill
What will you watch? Cancellations, downgrades, support tickets and win rates

Worked example: Disney+ in 2025

On September 23, 2025, Disney announced price increases starting October 21. The ad-supported Disney+ plan went up $2 to $11.99 a month, and the ad-free Premium plan rose $3 to $18.99, or $30 a year to $189.99. Bundles rose $2 or $3, and NFL+ prices stayed put. Disney had hinted at the increases on its earnings call, and its previous round, in October 2024, raised most plans by $1 to $2. The timing was rough: the news arrived in a week when some fans were already posting about canceling over an unrelated programming decision5. A year later, Disney did it again: in September 2026, ad-free Disney+ rose $2.50 to $21.49, its sixth US increase in six years6.

Run it through the six questions:

  • How much: every increase stayed at $3 a month or less, below the $5 that most surveyed subscribers said would likely make them cancel. Treat that survey as a rough guide, not proof, since stated answers aren't the same as behavior. In percentage terms, though, the Premium increase was still about 19%.
  • Where they land: the ad-supported plan stayed much cheaper than Premium, so price-sensitive subscribers had somewhere to go besides the exit.
  • Cadence: about once a year, in steps, which fits the gradual approach Malladi describes. Disney did it again in 2026.
  • Timing: some of it is out of your hands. A good plan includes what you'll do if an increase hits during bad news.

Common traps

  • Trusting stated answers. What people say they'd pay and what they actually pay often differ.
  • Researching price without alternatives. Value only makes sense next to the options buyers weigh.
  • An unexplained big jump. A large increase with no new value attached invites customers to rethink the whole purchase.
  • Raising prices with nothing new to point to. Customers accept increases more easily when they can see what changed.

In an interview

For "How would you raise prices for X?", walk through why now, how much, who's affected, where customers can step down to, how you'll tell them and what you'll watch. Then name the research you'd run first, and be honest about what it can't tell you.

Self-check: A B2B tool plans to raise prices 40% for every customer next month because a survey said customers would pay more. What would you challenge?

Three things. The evidence: survey answers are stated, not real purchases, so test the price with new customers or try a smaller step first. The size: 40% in one jump invites customers to reconsider the whole purchase, so consider steps. The rollout: one month's notice is short for business budgets, so start with new customers, give existing ones time to adjust and tie the change to new value they can see.

Check your understanding

4 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. Your team plans a survey with one question: "What's the most you'd pay for this?" What flaw would the Kellogg researchers point out?

    Show answer

    They answer without thinking about the alternatives they'd actually weigh. Kellogg researchers found many studies asked without describing the situation or the alternatives, which misses how buyers really judge value.

  2. Your Van Westendorp survey's curves cross at $42, and your CEO wants to set the price at exactly $42. What would you say?

    Show answer

    It gives a range of acceptable prices from what people say, so treat it as one input, not the answer. Mike Pritchard warns against treating any single crossing as the optimal price. The meter shows where prices start to feel too cheap or too expensive, and the decision needs other inputs too.

  3. Your company acts as if demand is "kinked." How should that shape a plan to raise prices 20%?

    Show answer

    Break it into smaller steps and watch where demand starts to fall. In Malladi's explanation, cutting prices barely brings in more customers, but raising them can drive customers away fast. Small steps show you where demand starts to drop before you've lost too many people.

  4. In Deloitte's survey, most streaming subscribers said a $5 hike would likely make them cancel their favorite service. If you ran pricing at a streaming service, how would you use that?

    Show answer

    Treat it as a warning: test smaller steps, and keep a cheaper plan for people to step down to. Stated answers aren't a hard ceiling, but a clear majority naming $5 is a warning. Smaller steps and a cheaper plan give price-sensitive customers somewhere to go besides the exit.
