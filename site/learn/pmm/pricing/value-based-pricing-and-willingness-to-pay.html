Value-based pricing and willingness to pay

PMMLesson 5.111 min

Goal: Set a price from what the product is worth to customers, starting from their alternatives, and see why different customers will pay different amounts.

Skills: Pricing & promotion

Here's a number that should make any PMM care about pricing. In a 2003 analysis, McKinsey modeled the income statement of an average S&P 1500 company, a 1% price increase, with volume holding steady, raised operating profit by 8%. That's nearly 50% more than a 1% cut in variable costs, and more than three times the effect of selling 1% more1. McKinsey's more recent work puts the typical gain from a 1% price increase at 6% to 14% of operating profit1.

Even so, most companies don't price from value. As IMD professor Stefan Michel puts it, practitioners and researchers broadly agree that value-based pricing earns higher profits than pricing from costs or competitors, yet study after study finds those simpler methods are still the norm2.

Three ways to land on a price

OpenStax's marketing textbook describes three starting points3:

  • Cost-based: add up what it costs to make and sell the product, then add a margin. It's simple, but customers don't know or care what your costs are, so the price may have nothing to do with what they think it's worth.
  • Competition-based: price at, below or above rivals. The textbook's example is a retailer that tracks competitors' prices and sets its own just below them.
  • Value-based: start from the benefits customers get, learned through research, and set the price from there.

Each one answers a different question. Your costs tell you the lowest price you can survive on. Competitors tell you what buyers are used to paying. Value tells you the most a customer would pay. Your price lives somewhere between that floor and that ceiling. Value-based pricing means starting from the ceiling and working down, instead of starting from costs and working up.

What a product is worth depends on the alternative

Kellogg professor Eric Anderson explains it with a beer. You might pay $10 or more for your favorite beer at a fancy hotel bar and only $5 for the same beer from a beachside vendor, and that can be completely rational, because the alternatives in front of you are different. Simply asking people what they'd pay, he argues, misses the comparisons they're making4.

Willingness to pay is the most a customer would pay, and it's never a fixed property of the product. It moves with whatever else the buyer could do instead. For software, that alternative might be a rival product, a spreadsheet, an agency, an extra hire or doing nothing at all.

That gives you a simple way to estimate value:

Value to the customer = what the next-best alternative costs + what your differences are worth to them

Differences can add or subtract. Saving a team five hours a week adds value. A painful migration takes some away.

Here's how the math might look, with illustrative numbers. A support team pays $15 per agent a month for its current tool. Yours saves each agent two hours a month, and the company values an agent's hour at $30. The alternative is worth $15, and the difference is worth $60, so the ceiling is about $75 per agent a month. You wouldn't charge all of it. Customers need a reason to switch and switching takes effort, so a price of $30 to $40 leaves them a clear gain. One caution: finance teams often discount saved hours, so hard savings, like a tool retired or a hire avoided, make a stronger case.

Michel also names the catch. The hardest part of value-based pricing is measuring that difference: customers have to understand their own processes well enough to see what one option saves compared with the next-best alternative, and the research to find out costs money2.

Different customers, different prices

If willingness to pay varies from customer to customer, one price leaves money on the table with some and scares others away. Spotify's US plans show one way around that5:

Plan Price a month Who it's for
Individual $12.99 One account
Student $6.99 Verified students at accredited colleges
Duo $18.99 Two people at the same address
Family $21.99 Up to six accounts at the same address

The music is the same in every plan. What changes is who's paying and how much they're likely to afford. Students usually have less money, so they pay about half, but only after verification. Duo and Family let a household pay more in total while each person pays less. Student verification and the shared address are fences: conditions that stop everyone else from claiming the cheaper price.

Worked example: GoToMeeting's flat rate

Before launching GoToMeeting, Jim Semick's team ran dozens of interviews with potential customers. What they heard shaped the price as much as the product. They launched at $49 for all-you-can-meet, a flat rate that was unusual in conferencing, because the interviews showed customers valued a price structure that was easy to understand3.

Read it as a value-based decision:

  • The research came first. The team didn't pick a number and write messaging around it. It learned what customers valued, then built the price to match.
  • Predictability had value of its own. A bill that never surprised anyone was part of what customers were buying.
  • How you charge is part of the price. Flat, per seat or per use can add value or destroy it, separately from how much you charge.

Common traps

  • Pricing from cost by default. Your costs set the floor, not the price.
  • One price for everyone when customers differ a lot in what the product is worth to them.
  • Charging the whole ceiling. If you capture all the value, customers have no reason to switch.
  • Ignoring the do-nothing option. For many products, the real competitor is the spreadsheet or the old process.

In an interview

For "How would you price X?", start with the customer and their alternative, not your costs or your competitors. Estimate the value of the difference in rough numbers, say where you'd set the price between the floor and the ceiling and why, and explain how you'd check it with research.

To hear a full pricing case worked through out loud, here's a mock interview with consultants from McKinsey and EY. It's consulting style, so expect more math than most PMM loops ask for.

Self-check: A startup's analytics tool costs $8 per user a month to run. The founder wants to charge $10 to "keep a healthy margin." Customers currently pay an agency $2,000 a month for the same reports. What's wrong with that reasoning, and where would you start?

The founder is pricing from cost, which is only the floor. The ceiling comes from the alternative: the $2,000 agency bill, plus or minus what the tool does better or worse, such as faster reports but more work for the team. Start there, estimate the value per customer and price well below that ceiling, which is likely far above $10 per user. Then test it with real customers.

Check your understanding

4 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. Your CFO can raise prices 1% or cut variable costs 1%, with sales volume holding steady. Which usually helps operating profit more?

    Show answer

    The price increase, because almost all of the extra revenue goes straight to profit. In McKinsey's model of an average S&P 1500 company, a 1% price rise lifted operating profit about 8%, nearly 50% more than a 1% cut in variable costs.

  2. The same bottle of water sells for $1 at a grocery store and $5 inside a stadium, and both sell out. What explains it?

    Show answer

    Willingness to pay depends on the alternatives available in that moment. Inside the stadium, there's no cheaper option a few steps away. The same logic is behind Kellogg's hotel-bar and beach beer: the gap can be completely rational because the alternatives differ.

  3. Three teams price the same tool. One adds 30% to its costs, one prices $5 under the market leader, and one estimates what the tool saves customers compared with their spreadsheet. What does the third team learn that the others don't?

    Show answer

    The most a customer would pay. Costs set the floor, and competitors show what buyers are used to paying. Value, measured against the customer's next-best alternative, sets the ceiling.

  4. A design tool wants to offer teachers half off. What has to be true for the discount to work without everyone claiming it?

    Show answer

    There's a fence, like verifying a school email address, so other customers can't claim the price. Different prices only work when conditions stop customers who'd pay more from claiming the lower price, like Spotify's student verification.
