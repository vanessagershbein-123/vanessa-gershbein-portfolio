Positioning starts with the alternatives

PMMLesson 3.19 min

Goal: Find what customers would really do without your product, and use it as the starting point for positioning.

Skills: Positioning & strategy

Your toughest competitor might be a spreadsheet. Buyers never judge a product on its own. They judge it against whatever they'd do instead, and that's often not a competitor in the usual sense.

That's why positioning so often goes wrong at the very first step. The team starts from its features and only later asks what customers compare them with. Get the alternatives wrong and everything you build on top, from the positioning statement to the message house, inherits the mistake.

Positioning is the frame buyers use

Tell a buyer your product is a "CRM" and watch what happens. In an instant they've guessed who you compete with, what features you have and roughly what you cost. You haven't said a word about any of it.

That reaction is positioning at work. It's the place a product holds in a buyer's mind compared with the alternatives, an idea Al Ries and Jack Trout made famous in their 1981 book Positioning1. April Dunford, a widely cited voice on B2BBusiness-to-business: selling to companies rather than to consumers. positioning, frames it as context: the frame a buyer uses to make sense of a product2. Good positioning sets off assumptions that are true about your product. Bad positioning sets off ones that aren't, because the category you picked promises things you don't do.

The five parts, and why the order matters

Dunford breaks positioning into five components that depend on each other2:

  1. Competitive alternatives: what customers would use if you didn't exist
  2. Differentiated capabilities: what you have that those alternatives lack
  3. Value: what those capabilities do for the customer
  4. Target customers: who cares most about that value
  5. Market category: the frame that makes the value obvious

The order isn't arbitrary. Each step uses the answer to the one before it. You can't say what's different about you until you know what you're compared with. So you start with the alternatives, and with Dunford's core question: what would a customer do if you didn't exist3?

This can look like it clashes with advice to pick your target customer first. It doesn't, quite. Dunford's order assumes you already have customers who love the product, because they're the people you ask about alternatives, so it fits repositioning something that exists. When you're entering a brand-new market with no customers yet, choosing a segment first comes naturally.

How to find what customers would really do

The alternatives are often not your named competitors. They can be a spreadsheet, a manual process, an intern, or doing nothing at all. That last one deserves special respect. Harvard's John Gourville found that buyers overvalue what they already have while sellers overvalue what's new, a mismatch he puts at about nine to one4. So the status quo starts every deal with a head start. Newer research points the same way. In a study of more than 2.5 million recorded sales conversations, Matthew Dixon and Ted McKenna found that 40% to 60% of deals are lost to buyers who intend to purchase but never act5.

To build the real list, start with your happiest, best-fit customers and ask what they would do if your product didn't exist6. Expect their answers to be humbler than the competitor slide in your sales deck. Group them into a few alternatives and see which come up most.

Once you know where you're strong against those alternatives, you pick a strategy. Dunford describes three7:

Strategy When it fits
Head to head You claim the leader's category with no qualifiers. It works best if you are the leader, or a strong challenger when the leader is weak
Big fish, small pond You win a sub-segment of an existing category, one that cares most about what you do best. Most tech companies start here
Create a new category No existing category explains your value. The hardest route, so use it only when nothing else fits

Worked example: how Janna Systems stopped losing to Siebel

Dunford told this story from her own career in a 2019 talk6. She doesn't name the company there, but the details match Janna Systems, the CRM maker Siebel bought in 2000. Here's how it went, by her account:

  • Before: Janna sold itself as enterprise CRM and kept losing to Siebel, which had 8,000 employees and $2 billion in revenue. By Dunford's account, her company had 30 people and about $1.3 million in revenue.
  • The real strength: Janna could model relationships between people across organizations. For most CRM buyers, that didn't matter much.
  • The insight: bankers at Goldman Sachs and Merrill Lynch lit up in demos, because banks run on relationships.
  • After: Janna repositioned as CRM for investment banks. Dunford says revenue reached a little under $80 million in about 18 months, and that Siebel paid $1.3 billion for the company.

Before you repeat those numbers, check them against the press. Computerworld's coverage of the deal in September 2000 gives smaller ones: $13 million in revenue for 1999 and $12.6 million for the first half of 2000. It valued the deal at $975 million in stock, below Dunford's figure, and named customers including Merrill Lynch and Lehman Brothers8. The press figures are the safer ones to quote, since they were reported at the time, and the positioning point holds either way.

Notice what didn't change: the product. Janna changed its context. It picked the customers who valued its difference and named a category they already understood. That's the big fish, small pond strategy from the table above.

Common traps

  • Listing every possible competitor instead of what customers actually do today.
  • Fixating on the rival your team talks about most. Check which alternatives actually come up in won and lost deals, because the one you lose to most is often the status quo.
  • Assuming you need a new category. Most products win by framing themselves inside one buyers already know, since a new category means teaching the market from scratch.

In an interview

"How would you position X?" is a classic PMM question. The tempting move is to start listing features. Resist it, and open with the alternatives instead: "Before I talk about what's special, here's what this customer does today without it."

Self-check: A startup sells software that schedules shifts for restaurants. The founder says, "We have no competitors." What are its likely real alternatives?

Paper schedules or a whiteboard, a spreadsheet, group texts, the scheduling module inside the restaurant's payroll or point-of-sale system, and the manager simply spending Sunday night on it. When a team says "no competitors," it usually hasn't looked at the status quo.

Check your understanding

3 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. You're asked to position a new product. Which positioning component should you pin down first?

    Show answer

    What customers would do if your product didn't exist. Each part of positioning builds on the one before it, and the alternatives come first. You can't say what makes you different until you know what buyers compare you with.

  2. Your team keeps worrying about a well-known rival that rarely shows up in actual deals. What should you check first?

    Show answer

    Which alternatives actually come up in your won and lost deals. Positioning has to beat what customers really compare you with, and that's often the status quo.

  3. A small software company keeps losing head-to-head deals to a much bigger rival. What does the Janna story suggest it try?

    Show answer

    Focus on the segment that values its difference most, in a category those buyers already understand. Janna kept the same product and won by changing the context to CRM for investment banks. That's the big fish, small pond strategy.
