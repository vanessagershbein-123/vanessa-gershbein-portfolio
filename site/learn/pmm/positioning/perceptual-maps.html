Perceptual maps: seeing the market the way buyers do

PMMLesson 3.310 min

Goal: Build a perceptual map from real buyer data, and read what it says about where to position.

Skills: Positioning & strategyData & charts

Ask developers which code editor they want to use next year, and Neovim trails far behind VS Code. Ask the people who already use Neovim whether they want to keep it, and it beats every editor on the map below, VS Code included. A feature list would never show you that. Two numbers on one chart show it at a glance.

That chart is a perceptual map. Positioning work leaves you with two answers: who buyers compare you with, and on what. A perceptual map puts both on one page: two attributes buyers care about, and where each product sits in their minds. It's a fast way to check whether buyers see you the way you think they do, and whether there's open space worth claiming.

What a perceptual map shows

OpenStax's Principles of Marketing describes a perceptual map as a diagram of how the average target customer perceives your product next to competitors, plotted on two attributes that drive the decision. Marketers use it for three jobs: checking whether customers see the product as intended, finding the closest rivals, and spotting empty areas that might be opportunities1. None of this is new. Researchers have built these maps from survey data for decades, and Hauser and Koppelman were already comparing mapping techniques in the Journal of Marketing Research in 19792.

The key word is perceives. Two products with the same feature list can sit far apart on the map if buyers see them differently.

How to build one

The steps are simple. The discipline is in where the data comes from.

  1. Pick the buyer and the frame. Use the real alternatives customers consider, not the rivals you'd like to have.
  2. Choose two attributes buyers use to decide. Take them from interviews and win/loss notes, not your feature list, and make sure they don't move together. If every fast product is also cheap, one axis would do.
  3. Place each product with buyer data. Surveys, review scores and the reasons deals were won or lost all work. Your team's opinions don't.
  4. Read it. Where are you, who's closest, and where is the empty space?
  5. Ask why a space is empty before you move into it. Sometimes nobody wants that combination.

Worked example: mapping code editors with real survey data

Here's a map built from what users say, not what a product team believes. Stack Overflow's 2026 Developer Survey drew 30,903 responses from 169 countries3. For development environments, 13,193 developers answered, and the survey reports two numbers for each tool. Desired is the share of respondents who want to use it next year. Admired is the share of its current users who want to keep using it3. That gives a real two-axis map: how many developers are drawn to a tool, and how loyal its users are.

Editor Desired Admired
Visual Studio Code 40.6% 55.7%
IntelliJ IDEA 14.5% 53.3%
Neovim 12.9% 69.9%
Zed 9.7% 57.3%
PyCharm 8.2% 49.7%
Cursor 7.9% 33.0%
Kiro 1.7% 31.2%
Windsurf 1.7% 22.7%

Now read it:

  • VS Code owns the right side alone. It's the default choice, wanted by far more developers than anything else, with solid loyalty.
  • Neovim is the opposite. Few developers overall want to use it next year, but the people who use it love it. That's a small, loyal niche, a classic big fish, small pond position.
  • Cursor, Kiro and Windsurf sit low. All three score lower on admiration than the established editors on this map, which suggests their users are still shopping around. If you were the PMM at one of them, your gap to close would be loyalty, not awareness.

Strictly speaking, desire and admiration are outcomes rather than the attributes buyers decide on, so this is a popularity-versus-loyalty map. A classic perceptual map would plot attributes, such as speed against extensibility. One more caution: the survey reflects Stack Overflow's own audience, not every developer, so the map shows how that group sees the tools.

Analyst maps aren't perception maps

If you work in tech, you'll also run into two-by-two charts that look a lot like perceptual maps but come from analysts. Gartner's Magic Quadrant places vendors on completeness of vision and ability to execute, sorting them into leaders, challengers, visionaries and niche players, and buyers use it to narrow their shortlists4. Forrester's Wave launched in 2002, plotting a vendor's current offering against its strategy, with bubble size showing market presence5. Since late 2024, customer feedback has replaced market presence on that graphic5.

These charts show what analysts judge, not what buyers feel. Buyers still read them, though, so where you land shapes perception too. That's why analyst relations shows up in so many PMM roles.

Common traps

  • Picking axes that flatter you. If the axes are your two strongest features, the map can't tell you anything new.
  • Using attributes that move together. Price and a premium feel often rise together, which squeezes the map into a single line.
  • Chasing every empty space. A gap can mean nobody wants that mix.
  • Placing products from your team's opinions. Without buyer data, the map just shows what your team believes.

In an interview

Asked to position a product? Reach for the whiteboard. A quick sketch of two axes and three or four rivals shows you understand the market. Say where the axes come from, meaning what buyers actually decide on, then say where your product sits and where it should move.

Self-check: You're the PMM for Zed on this map. Where should it aim, and what's the risk?

Zed's loyalty is already close to VS Code's (57.3% admired against 55.7%), so the gap is desire: few developers overall want to use it next year. The goal is to move right without losing admiration, for example by finding the developers most like its current fans. The risk is chasing VS Code's whole audience and diluting what current users love.

Check your understanding

3 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. Two note-taking apps have almost identical feature lists, but buyers call one "for students" and the other "for executives." Which tool would show that difference?

    Show answer

    A perceptual map. A perceptual map plots how buyers see products on attributes they care about. On a feature table, these two apps would look the same.

  2. Which pair of axes makes the most useful map?

    Show answer

    Two attributes buyers use to decide that don't move together. Axes have to come from how buyers decide, and they need to vary independently, or the map collapses into one line.

  3. Your product is a Leader in a Gartner Magic Quadrant, but buyers in win/loss interviews call it "the expensive, complicated option." Whose view belongs on your perceptual map?

    Show answer

    The buyers', because a perceptual map plots how buyers see you. The Magic Quadrant is analysts' judgment of vision and execution. Buyers do read it when they shortlist, but your positioning has to deal with how they actually see you.
