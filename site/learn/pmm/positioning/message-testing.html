Testing messages before launch

PMMLesson 3.68 min

Goal: Pick the right way to test a message, given how much traffic and time you have.

Skills: Research & insightWriting & storytelling

Every message gets tested eventually. The only question is when. Skip it before launch, and the first real test happens on launch day, in front of buyers, when changing anything is slow and public.

A few days of testing beforehand shows whether people understand the product, which words stick and which claims they doubt. Which test you run depends on how much traffic and time you have.

Why your team can't judge its own copy

Read your own homepage and it makes perfect sense. Of course it does: you already know what the product does. Your team fills in the gaps without noticing, so it can't judge whether a message is clear. Testing with your target audience catches that before launch.

Four ways to test, and when each fits

Pick by what you need to learn:

Method What it tells you What it needs Use it when
Qualitative message test Why copy works or fails: clarity, relevance, value, difference A panel of about 12 to 13 people from your audience Before launch, especially in B2B
Five-second test What people notice and remember at first glance A screenshot and a small panel Checking a homepage or ad
A/B test Which version converts better Enough traffic, often 500+ conversions a month After launch, on high-traffic pages
Interviews and sales feedback How real buyers react in context Access to customers and the sales team All the time

Sources: Nielsen Norman Group, Kuric et al. 2023.

The fine print:

  • Qualitative tests explain why. For a typical qualitative usability study of one user group, Nielsen Norman Group recommends five participants to uncover most common problems1. Message tests work on similar logic: a small panel of the right people tells you why a message lands or doesn't, while an A/B test needs far more traffic and still can't tell you why.
  • Five-second tests check first impressions. Show a design for five seconds, then ask people to describe what they saw2. A 2023 peer-reviewed study showed web pages for 2, 5 or 10 seconds and found that people who saw complex pages too briefly struggled to say what the page was for, so match the exposure to the page3.
  • Positioning reviews don't stop at launch. April Dunford held a standing positioning check-in about every six months with sales, product and the CEO, to ask what had changed among competitors4.

Common traps

  • Testing on coworkers. They already know what the product does, so they can't tell you whether the page explains it.
  • Running an A/B test on a page with little traffic. You'll wait months for a result you can't trust.
  • Testing a headline without saying who should read it. The right panel matters more than the method, because a clear message for the wrong reader still fails.

In an interview

"How would you know the messaging works?" The reflex answer is "A/B test it," which often won't work on a low-traffic B2BBusiness-to-business: selling to companies rather than to consumers. site. Name a method that fits the situation instead: a qualitative test before launch, then conversion and win rates after.

Self-check: Your team loves a clever headline, but in a message test, most of your target readers can't say what the product does. What do you do?

Side with the readers. Rewrite the headline in plain words that say what the product does and for whom, then test it again. The team already knows the answer, so it can't judge whether the headline is clear.

Check your understanding

3 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. Your B2B site gets 300 visitors a month. Which test fits best?

    Show answer

    A qualitative message test with about 12 people from your target audience. At that traffic an A/B test could take months, and it wouldn't tell you why a version won.

  2. You want to know whether first-time visitors can tell what your homepage is about at a glance. Which test fits?

    Show answer

    A five-second test. A five-second test shows what people notice and remember at first glance. An A/B test shows which version converts, not what people understood, and your own team already knows the answer.

  3. Why is testing messaging on your own team a trap?

    Show answer

    They already know what the product does, so they can't judge whether it's clear. Clarity has to be judged by people who don't already know the answer.
