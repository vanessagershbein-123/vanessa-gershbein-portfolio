The positioning statement and the message house

PMMLesson 3.49 min

Goal: Capture positioning in a statement, then turn it into a message house that teams can reuse.

Skills: Positioning & strategyWriting & storytelling

Ask the sales team, the homepage and the latest ad campaign what your product does. If you get three different answers, your positioning never made it out of the room where it was decided.

Two tools carry it out. A positioning statement records the choices for your own team. A message house turns them into language that sales, the website and campaigns can all share. Skip them, and every team ends up writing its own version of what the product does.

The positioning statement, and its catch

The best-known template comes from Geoffrey Moore's Crossing the Chasm. Here are its slots1:

Slot What goes there
For The target customers (for Moore, one beachhead segment)
Who are dissatisfied with The current market alternative
Our product is a The new product category
That provides The key problem-solving capability
Unlike The product alternative
We have assembled The key features for this customer's specific use

The appeal is obvious: it forces every part of positioning into one sentence. April Dunford pushes back hard anyway. She argues you should never write a positioning statement at all, because the blanks assume you already know each answer. Once the work is done, she records the results in a dated positioning template so the team shares one agreed version2. If your team still wants one, treat the statement as a receipt for decisions you've already made, written after the thinking is done. That's a compromise Dunford herself wouldn't bother with.

The message house: from choices to words

Nobody puts a positioning statement on a homepage. It's internal. The message house turns it into language that sales, web and campaign teams can reuse, and the name tells you the shape. Atlassian's template has these parts3:

Part What goes there
Roof The top-line message and who it's for
Pillars Value themes that hold the roof up
Proof points Evidence under each pillar: numbers, customer stories, features
Boilerplate Standard descriptions in a few lengths

Pragmatic Institute's guide to message maps describes the same structure: three message pillars (four at most), proof points under each ranked by their impact on revenue, and positioning statements in a few lengths. Preparation starts with gathering product information and defining the target buyers4.

Interviews rarely leave time for the whole house. RocketBlocks' guide to PMM hard skills offers a lighter three-step version of the same idea: why (the customer's pain), how (the value proposition and what makes it different), and what (about three benefits that act as reasons to believe)5.

Worked example: Mailchimp outgrows email

On May 13, 2019, Mailchimp's CEO, Ben Chestnut, announced that the company was moving from email marketing to an all-in-one marketing platform for small businesses. Landing pages, ads, social posting and CRM tools came into one app, with new plans6. TechCrunch reported about 11 million active customers, projected 2019 revenue of $700 million, and a focus on businesses with fewer than 100 employees6. Mailchimp also offered an Essentials plan designed for customers who mainly use email, and existing customers could stay on their current plans6.

That's a lot to explain to customers who came for email. Here's how it could look as a statement and a message house. It's an illustration built from the public facts, not Mailchimp's internal document.

Statement: For small businesses that market themselves without a marketing team, Mailchimp is an all-in-one marketing platform that runs email, ads, landing pages and customer data from one place. Unlike stitching together separate tools, Mailchimp keeps your audience and results in one view.

Pillar 1: One place Pillar 2: Know your customers Pillar 3: Grow at your pace
Message Run every channel from one app Your audience data, shared across channels Start with email, add more when you're ready
Proof Landing pages, ads, social and CRM in one app One audience view for every campaign A plan built for email-first customers; current customers keep their plans

Common traps

  • Pillars that are features. "Landing pages" is a feature. "Run every channel from one app" is value.
  • No proof. A pillar with nothing under it is just a claim.
  • Too many pillars. Past four, nobody remembers any of them.

In an interview

If an interviewer asks you to "write the messaging" for a launch, don't start with taglines. Sketch the house: one roof line, three pillars, and one proof point each. It shows you think in structure before you think in slogans.

Self-check: Sales asks for a fourth and fifth pillar so they can mention every feature. What do you say?

Keep three, four at most, and put the extra features under the pillar they prove. A pillar for every feature turns the house back into a feature list, and nobody remembers five messages.

Check your understanding

3 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. When should you write the positioning statement?

    Show answer

    After the positioning work, to record what you decided. The blanks assume you already know each answer. April Dunford argues against writing one at all, so if you use the template, write it after the work, as a record.

  2. Which of these belongs under a pillar that says "Close the books faster"?

    Show answer

    "Customers closed their monthly books three days sooner on average". Proof points are evidence, such as numbers, customer stories and features. "Built for scale" is just another claim, and a pillar with nothing under it is a claim too.

  3. Which roof line is strongest?

    Show answer

    "Run every channel from one app, built for small teams without a marketer". It names a value and an audience. The first could belong to any competitor, and the last is a feature.
