Messaging by audience and funnel stage

PMMLesson 3.58 min

Goal: Adapt one core message for different people and different moments, without changing what you stand for.

Skills: Writing & storytelling

Here's the meeting where a software purchase gets decided. The CFO asks what it costs. The IT lead asks what it might break. The person who'll use it every day asks whether it's any good. You walked in with one message.

One message rarely works for all of them, and a buyer who has just noticed a problem needs something different from one comparing vendors. The skill is changing the framing for each audience and stage without changing what you stand for.

Same value, different framing

Pragmatic Institute draws the line clearly: positioning keeps every team on the same playbook, while the messaging built on it speaks to each type of buyer, such as functional, financial and technical buyers1. In B2BBusiness-to-business: selling to companies rather than to consumers., split the buyer from the user, because the person who signs off on the purchase is often not the person who uses it every day. Then ask what each one needs to see.

A buying group, moving in three stages

That meeting tends to be crowded, because in B2B the buyer is usually a group. Gartner's survey of 632 B2B buyers found buying groups of five to 16 people from as many as four functions. In 74% of them, members showed "unhealthy conflict," such as clashing goals or being overruled by someone outside the group, and groups that reached consensus were 2.5 times more likely to call their deal high quality2. So your B2B messaging has two jobs: speak to each member, and give the whole group a shared reason to agree.

To plan which message goes where, it helps to compress the buying stages. OpenStax's Principles of Marketing lists eight for B2B, from problem recognition through supplier selection3. Marketers often fold the early ones together and work with three:

Stage What the buyer is doing What helps
Awareness (need recognition) Naming a problem Educational content with no pitch
Consideration (search and evaluation) Comparing ways to solve it Case studies, testimonials, demos
Decision (purchase, or supplier selection in B2B) Comparing vendors Objection handling, sharp differences, trials

Don't expect anyone to march through that table in order. Real buyers rarely move in a straight line, as Martina Lauchengco notes4, and each member of a buying group can be at a different stage.

Worked example: one house, two people

Take an illustrative Mailchimp message house aimed at small shops. Same shop, different worries:

Owner of a small shop Part-time marketer at that shop
Cares about Time and money Getting campaigns out without juggling tools
Awareness message "Marketing shouldn't eat your weekend." "Stop copying lists between five tools."
Decision message One plan, one bill, and you can start with email only Your audience data in every channel, with no re-uploading
Proof A plan for email-first customers; current customers keep their plans Landing pages, ads and CRM in the same app

Common traps

  • Changing the core value per persona. If every audience hears a different product, you have no positioning.
  • Pitching at the awareness stage. People naming a problem want help first, so save the demo for later.
  • Writing only for the buyer. Users have a big say in whether a B2B product gets renewed.

In an interview

If an interviewer asks you to "write messaging," answer with two questions of your own: for whom, and at what stage? Then give two versions and point out what stayed the same. That's how you show the core value held.

Self-check: A CFO is in the decision stage and an IT lead is in consideration for the same security product. What does each need?

The CFO needs cost, risk and proof of return, plus answers to objections. The IT lead needs to compare approaches: a technical demo, case studies from similar companies, and how it fits their stack. The core value stays the same for both.

Check your understanding

3 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. When you adapt messaging for different personas, what should stay the same?

    Show answer

    The core value. Framing, proof and order can change by persona. If the core value changes too, you have no positioning.

  2. A buyer is still naming their problem. What helps them most?

    Show answer

    Educational content with no pitch. That's the awareness stage. Demos and trials fit later, at consideration and decision.

  3. In B2B, why split the buyer from the user when you write messaging?

    Show answer

    The person who signs isn't always the daily user, and users affect whether it gets renewed. Writing only for the buyer is a common trap.
