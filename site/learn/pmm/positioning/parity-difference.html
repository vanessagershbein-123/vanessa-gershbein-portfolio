Points of parity and points of difference

PMMLesson 3.28 min

Goal: Separate what you must match to be considered from what makes you the better choice.

Skills: Positioning & strategy

A feature can go from a reason to buy to something buyers simply expect in about two years. Basic AI assistants in work software just made that trip, while more advanced AI moved into pricier plans.

Teams also tend to pitch only their differences, then lose deals over a missing basic. So once you know the alternatives, ask two separate questions. What do you have to match? What makes you the better choice? Keeping them apart, and watching how the frame you pick changes the answers, keeps your positioning honest.

Three parts of a position

The framework comes from brand management. Kevin Lane Keller, Brian Sternthal and Alice Tybout laid it out in a 2002 Harvard Business Review article1. They argued that positioning tends to focus only on differences, and neglects two other parts that matter just as much. It works the same way for software, where parity points usually go by "table stakes".

Tybout later boiled a position down to three parts2. Each one answers a question a skeptical buyer might ask:

Part Question it answers
Frame of reference What are we compared against?
Point of difference Why are we the better choice within that frame?
Reason to believe Why should anyone believe that claim?

Then there are points of parity: what you have to match to belong in the frame at all1. A CRM has to import your contacts and sync with your email. Nobody picks one for that. Leave it out, though, and you're off the shortlist.

The frame decides what counts as a difference

A feature doesn't carry its status around with it. The same feature can be a difference in one frame and parity in another, and in software the status can flip fast, because rivals can ship a copy within months.

  • AI assistants, 2023 to 2025. In 2023, AI was something you paid extra for. Notion AI launched as an add-on at $10 per member a month3, and Microsoft 365 Copilot went on sale to large companies at $30 per user a month4. By 2025, rivals were folding it into their plans. Google stopped selling its Gemini add-ons and put its AI features into Workspace Business and Enterprise plans5, and Atlassian made its Rovo assistant part of every paid plan at no extra cost6. In about two years, basic AI assistance went from a reason to buy to table stakes, while premium AI features moved into higher tiers.
  • Database or BI tool? April Dunford describes a database product whose prospects kept saying they already had Oracle. Same product, new frame: once it was positioned as a business intelligence tool, buyers compared it with different products, and the conversation changed7.

Worked example: the difference Adobe tried to buy

In 2022, Adobe agreed to buy Figma for about $20 billion and described it as a "web-first collaborative design platform"8. Why pay that much for a rival? The UK's Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) reviewed the deal, and its findings lay out both sides of the table. It treated Figma Design and Adobe XD as the same kind of product: all-in-one design tools for professionals who design apps and websites9. Same frame, very different results:

Figma Design Adobe XD
Where it runs In the browser A desktop app
Share of revenue in that market Over 80% 5 to 10%
  • Parity: designing screens and building prototypes. Both tools did it, so neither won on it.
  • Difference: Figma runs in the browser, so a whole team can work in one file at the same time. Adobe planned a web tool, Project Spice, to match Figma's real-time collaboration, then canceled it in September 2022, days before the deal was announced. The CMA judged XD a weaker product, and Adobe told it XD had been in maintenance mode since early 20229.

The CMA warned that the deal would remove the competition XD still gave Figma9, and the companies called it off in December 20238. Real-time collaboration was a difference big enough that the leader in creative software tried to build it, then tried to buy it.

Build your own parity table

Put your product next to the main alternative, one attribute per row, and be honest in the last column:

Attribute Us Main alternative Parity or difference? Does the target care?
Example: real-time co-editing Built in Export the file and email it Difference Yes, a lot
Example: single sign-on (SSO) Yes Yes Parity IT expects it; not a reason to buy

Two rules keep the table useful. A difference only counts if the target customer values it. Competitors will copy your differences over time, so revisit the table.

Common traps

  • Claiming a difference the customer doesn't care about. "First to use technology X" means nothing if the outcome is the same.
  • Ignoring the frame. Your differences change depending on what you're compared against.
  • Skipping parity. Missing a must-have kills deals before your difference even gets heard.

In an interview

When you position anything, say the frame out loud first. A difference only means something next to what you're compared with: "In the project-management tool frame, our parity points are A and B, and our difference is C."

Self-check: An expense app built for small businesses starts selling to large companies. What happens to "set up in an afternoon"?

It stops being much of a difference. Large companies expect a longer rollout and judge vendors on approval workflows, audit trails and links to their finance systems, so those become the parity points to match. The difference has to move to something they value, such as how quickly employees actually start using it.

Check your understanding

3 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. Your team wants to lead the homepage with "SOC 2 compliant," which every rival on the shortlist also is. What's the problem?

    Show answer

    It's a point of parity: buyers need it to consider you, but nobody picks you for it. Missing a parity point rules you out, but having one doesn't win the deal. Put it where buyers check for it, like a security page, and give the headline to what sets you apart.

  2. Your project-management app was the only one with built-in time tracking. Now every major rival has it. What is time tracking for you today?

    Show answer

    A point of parity: you need it to be considered, but it no longer sets you apart. A difference turns into parity once every rival in the frame offers it. That's what happened to AI assistants in work software between 2023 and 2025.

  3. Your point of difference is "the fastest sync of any file-sharing app." Which of these is the reason to believe?

    Show answer

    A third-party benchmark comparing your sync times with the top three rivals. The reason to believe is the evidence that makes the difference credible. The other options are a claim, a target and a frame.
