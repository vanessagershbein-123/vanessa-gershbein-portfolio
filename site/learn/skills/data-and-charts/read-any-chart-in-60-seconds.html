Read any chart in 60 seconds

SkillLesson 110 min

Goal: Talk through any chart out loud in about a minute: units and axes, one data point, the biggest movement, so what.

Skills: Interviews & career

Here are ten months of one number: the average price of a pound of ground coffee in US cities, as tracked by the Bureau of Labor Statistics. In April 2026 it was $9.72. By October it was $9.251. Drawn one way, that's a crash. Drawn the other way, it's a flat line.

Both charts plot the same accurate numbers. The first starts its vertical axisThe ruler along the side or bottom of a chart that says what each position means. at $9.20, so a 5% dip fills the whole frame, and its title calls that a tumble. The second starts at zero, so the same dip almost disappears. You only know which story is fair if you read the axis before the line, and the title last.

That's the whole skill in miniature. An interviewer who hands you a chart wants to see whether you read it carefully, say what matters and stop there.

Words to know

Term What it means A quick example
Axis The ruler along the side or bottom of a chart Dollars per pound, from $0 to $10
Baseline Where the value axis starts Zero, or $9.20 on a cut-off chart
Unit What one step on the axis measures Dollars, percent, thousands of stores
Data point One value on the chart $9.25 in October 2026
Series One set of points that belong together Coffee prices, month by month
Legend The key that says which color is which series Blue is grocery, orange is club
Source Who collected the data, and when Bureau of Labor Statistics, monthly
So what What the chart means for the decision in front of you "Our cost per bottle went up"

The 60-second routine

Say these four things, in this order, out loud:

  1. Units and axes (about 10 seconds). What is measured, in what unit, over what period, from what source? Where does the value axis start?
  2. One data pointOne value shown on a chart, such as one month's price. (about 10 seconds). Read a single value. It proves you can read the chart, and it gives everyone the same reference point.
  3. The biggest movement (about 20 seconds). The largest change, gap or outlierA data point far from the rest., with a rough size: doubled, fell by a third, twice the others.
  4. So what (about 20 seconds). What it means for the question you were asked, and what you'd want to know next.

The order matters. Units first, because every other sentence depends on them. One point next, because it's the easiest thing to get right and it calms you down. The movement third, because that's the story. The so-what last, because that's the part the interviewer is grading.

The routine on a longer chart

Here's how it sounds:

  • Units and axes: "This is the average retail price of a pound of ground coffee in US cities, in dollars, each September from 2015 to 2025, from the Bureau of Labor Statistics. The axis starts at zero, and 2019 is missing."
  • One point: "In September 2020, a pound cost $4.49."
  • Biggest movement: "The price stayed between about $4.30 and $4.75 from 2015 to 2021, then jumped twice: to $6.17 in 2022 and to $9.14 in 2025. It roughly doubled in five years."
  • So what: "Retail coffee prices, a rough sign of what beans cost, have about doubled since 2020, so if I'm pricing a coffee drink my costs have likely climbed too. I'd want to know whether shelf prices for coffee drinks kept up, and how shoppers reacted."

Every number in that answer comes off the chart. The interpretation is yours, so say it as one.

Why this is harder than it looks

Researchers who study data visualization literacy argue that reading and building charts matters as much in the information age as reading and writing text2. Plenty of people struggle with it. In a study of national samples in the United States and Germany, about a third of the population had both low graph literacy and low numeracy3. An interviewer can't assume you read charts well. You have to show it, out loud.

Common traps

  • Starting with the so-what. If you misread the units, a confident conclusion makes it worse.
  • Reading every point. One point gives the room a reference. Ten points is a recital.
  • Skipping the baselineWhere a chart's value axis starts. For bars it should be zero.. A cut-off axisA value axis that starts above zero, which makes differences look bigger. turns a 5% dip into a cliff.
  • Taking the title on trust. A title is someone's opinion of the chart. Check it against the data.
  • Stopping at "it went up." Give the size of the change and what it means.

In an interview

Ask for ten seconds to look before you speak. Then run the four steps and stop. If the interviewer pushes, they'll ask about the part they care about. A short, accurate read beats a long one.

RocketBlocks walks through how to interpret the charts you get in case interviews.

Self-check: Pennywhistle, the made-up cold-brew brand from the drills, shows you a chart titled "App orders are taking off." Weekly orders rise from about 9,100 to 9,600 over two months, on an axis that starts at 9,000. Say your first two sentences.

Something like: "This is Pennywhistle's weekly app orders over two months, and the axis starts at 9,000, not zero, so small changes look big. Orders went from about 9,100 to 9,600 a week, up about 5%." Then compare those weeks with the same weeks last year before you agree that orders are taking off.

Check your understanding

4 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. An interviewer slides you a chart of a snack brand's quarterly sales and says "go." What do you say first?

    Show answer

    What's measured, in what units, over what period, and where the axis starts. Units and axes come first because every later sentence depends on them. A wrong unit makes a confident conclusion wrong.

  2. A chart shows a brand's market share going from 12.1% to 12.6%, and the bars look like they doubled. What's the most likely reason?

    Show answer

    The value axis starts well above zero. A cut-off axis stretches small differences. Read the baseline before you react to the shapes.

  3. You've described the units, one point and the biggest movement. The interviewer is quiet. What's missing?

    Show answer

    The so-what: what the chart means for the question, and what you'd check next. The so-what is the part that shows business judgment. Say it, then stop.

  4. Which "one data point" works best when you're reading a chart of weekly app downloads?

    Show answer

    "In the week of March 3, downloads were about 40,000.". One specific value with its date and unit shows you can read the chart and gives everyone the same anchor.
