Charts that mislead, and the one-message chart

SkillLesson 713 min

Goal: Spot the visual and reasoning tricks that make honest-looking charts mislead, then build one chart with one clear message.

Skills: Writing & storytelling

When Maxim Lisnic and colleagues at the University of Utah studied Twitter posts with COVID-19 charts, they found that 84% of the posts that offered an interpretation of a chart were misleading. Only 11% of those misleading posts broke a common chart rule, such as a truncated axisA value axis that starts above zero, which makes differences look bigger., a dual axisTwo different value scales on one chart, one on each side. or a pie chart1. Their conclusion: breaking design guidelines isn't the main way people mislead with charts, and even faithfully plotted data can be used to support misinformation1.

So learn the visual tricks, but don't stop there. The bigger risk is an honest chart with a dishonest story.

Visual tricks: what to check first

Truncated axes. Bars that don't start at zero exaggerate differences. Experiments by Michael Correll, Enrico Bertini and Steven Franconeri found that the exaggeration persisted even when the chart carried an explicit visual cue that the axisThe ruler along the side or bottom of a chart that says what each position means. was cut. They also note that advice for other chart types is less clear-cut, since a cut axis can help or harm depending on what the chart is meant to show2.

In the first chart, Verizon's bar looks more than five times as tall as AT&T's. In the real numbers, it's about 1.26 times. Bars encode size by length, so cutting the axis changes the message. A line chart can sometimes zoom in honestly, as long as the axis is labeled and the change you're showing is one that matters.

Dual axes. Two scales on one chart let the author line up any two seriesOne set of values that belong together on a chart, such as one channel's sales by quarter. to look related, because they choose both scales. If you need to compare two measures in different units, indexA series rescaled so a chosen starting point equals 100. An index of 120 means 20% above the start. both to 100 or use two charts side by side.

Area and 3D. Bubbles, pictograms and 3D slices make readers judge area or volume, which people do poorly. Stick to length and position for anything you want compared.

Reasoning tricks: the bigger problem

Cherry-pickingChoosing the time window or subset that supports the story you want. the window. Guess first: since 2020, has online shopping's share of US retail grown or shrunk? Here is the seasonally adjustedA series with its usual calendar pattern removed, so the underlying trend shows. e-commerce share of US retail, from the second quarter of 2020 to the second quarter of 2022.

The share fell from 16.0% to 13.7%3. Every number is real. Now widen the window.

The short window started at a one-time spike. The long window shows a share that dipped after spring 2020 and then climbed past it. Ask why a chart starts and ends where it does.

Here's the routine on the long-window chart, out loud:

  • Units and axes: "E-commerce as a share of all US retail sales, seasonally adjusted, each quarter from 2019 through the third quarter of 2026. The axis starts at zero."
  • One point: "The spring 2020 peak was 16.0%."
  • Biggest movement: "After that peak the share slid for two years, to 13.7% in the second quarter of 2022, then climbed to 17.9%."
  • So what: "Online didn't give back its 2020 gains for good; it passed them. A chart that started in 2020 and stopped in 2022 told the opposite story."

Other reasoning tricks to watch for:

  • CorrelationHow tightly two measures move together in a straight line, from −1 to 1. sold as cause: two lines that move together, presented as one driving the other.
  • Missing denominatorThe number you divide by when you turn a total into a rate or an average.: totals compared across groups of very different size.
  • A missing comparison: "sales rose 8%" with no word on the category, last year or the plan.

The one-message chart

The fix for most bad charts is to decide on one message and design everything around it.

  1. Say the message in the title. The storytelling with data team recommends an active takeaway titleA chart or slide title that states the message, not just the topic. that states the key message, and notes that making a key observation primes the viewer to look for evidence of it in the chart4.
  2. Make the title true to the data. Titles are powerful. In a 2019 study of charts with slanted titles, people's recall of a chart's message more often matched its title than the chart itself5. Use that power honestly.
  3. Highlight the series that carries the message, and gray the rest.
  4. Label directly at the end of the line or bar, so nobody hunts through a legendThe key that says which color or mark stands for which series..
  5. Cut everything else: gridlines you don't need, extra series, decimals nobody reads.

The second e-commerce chart above follows this recipe: a takeaway title, one series and an annotation on the moment that matters.

Common traps

  • Hunting only for chart crimes. Most misleading charts look fine; check the reasoning too.
  • A question as a title. "Are our campaigns working?" makes the reader do your job.
  • A title the data can't support. A strong claim on a weak chart costs you trust.
  • Five messages in one chart. Split it into small charts, one message each.

In an interview

When you're shown a chart, check the axis, the window and the comparison before you accept its title. When you present one, open with your takeaway title as a sentence: "Online's share is back above its 2020 peak, and here's the trend." Then point to the line that proves it.

Alberto Cairo, author of How Charts Lie, shows how to read charts with a critical eye, including how accurate charts can still steer you wrong.

Self-check: Pennywhistle's app team (Pennywhistle is the made-up brand from the drills) titles a slide "Q3 subscription results." It shows six lines: new subscribers, cancellations, active subscribers, revenue, price and discount rate. The point is that cancellations doubled after the price increase. Fix it.

Make one chart of cancellations by month with the price increase marked, titled something like "Cancellations doubled after the July price increase." Gray or drop the other five lines, and put them in an appendix if someone asks. Before you present it, check whether anything else changed in July, so the title doesn't claim more than the data shows.

Check your understanding

3 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. A slide shows your brand's share in bars with an axis from 18% to 20%, and your share bar looks twice the competitor's. Actual shares are 19.6% and 18.8%. What do you do?

    Show answer

    Redraw the bars from zero, or show the numbers, so the gap looks as small as it is. Bars encode size by length. A cut axis makes a 0.8-point gap look like a landslide.

  2. An agency's chart starts a brand's sales trend in the month of its biggest-ever promotion and shows a steady decline since. What's your first question?

    Show answer

    What does the trend look like over a longer window that doesn't start at the promotion?. Starting at a spike guarantees a decline. Widen the window before you trust the trend.

  3. Which title best follows the one-message rule for a chart of channel sales?

    Show answer

    The app tripled its sales while grocery stayed flat. A takeaway title states the message the reader should leave with, so the chart becomes the evidence.
