Change over time and parts of a whole

SkillLesson 415 min

Goal: Read a trend without being fooled by the calendar, choose between a stacked and a 100% stacked bar, and walk a bridge from one number to the next.

Skills: Business math

Did online shopping lose ground at the start of 2026? One Census seriesOne set of values that belong together on a chart, such as one channel's sales by quarter. says it did: e-commerce fell from 18.9% of US retail sales in the last quarter of 2025 to 17.3% in the first quarter of 20261. Another says it gained, from 17.3% to 17.7%1. Both are right. The first is the raw number, and holiday shopping inflates it every fourth quarter. The second is seasonally adjustedA series with its usual calendar pattern removed, so the underlying trend shows.: the usual calendar pattern is taken out, so what's left is the trend plus any unusual moves.

Charts of change over time hide traps like that one, and so do charts that split a whole into parts. Here's how to read both.

Words to know

Time series The same measure recorded at regular intervals Online's share of retail sales, every quarter
Seasonally adjusted A series with its usual calendar pattern removed, so the trend shows The holiday bump taken out of each fourth quarter
Step change A jump to a new level that doesn't fully go back Online shopping in spring 2020
Stacked bar A bar split into parts, so you see the total and its pieces Sales by segment, one bar per year
100% stacked bar Every bar is the same height, so you only see each part's share Each segment's share of sales
Mix Each part's share of the total One segment is 18% of sales
Waterfall (bridge) A chart that walks from one number to another in steps that add up Volume, price and mix taking growth from 0 to 6.4%
Percentage point The plain difference between two percentages 17.3% to 17.7% is 0.4 points
Cohort A group that started at the same time Business locations that opened in the same year
Retention curve The share of a cohort still around as time passes Half of a year's new business locations still open after five years

Lines: one measure, period after period

When the horizontal axisThe ruler along the side or bottom of a chart that says what each position means. is time, draw a line. A line asks your eye to follow a path, and a trend is a path. Here are both versions of the Census series on one chart.

Read it out loud. Units: percent of all retail sales, by quarter. One point: in the third quarter of 2026, online sales were 17.9% of the total, seasonally adjusted. Online grew 10.4% from a year earlier while all retail grew 5.6%, which is why the share keeps creeping up2. Biggest movement: the jump in spring 2020, a partial fall back, then a climb that never returned to the old, lower path. So what: compare a seasonal series with the same quarter a year earlier, or use the adjusted line.

Two habits keep line charts honest. Compare like with like: a fourth quarter with a fourth quarter. Keep the lines few: past about four, highlight the one that matters and gray the rest.

Parts of a whole: stacked or 100% stacked?

Procter & Gamble reports sales for five segments, from Fabric & Home Care, the biggest, to Grooming, the smallest. In fiscal 2026, net sales rose 3% to $87.0 billion3. The same three years of segment sales make two different charts. Guess first: total sales grew over these three years. Did the mix of segments change much?

Each chart answers a different question. The stacked chart answers "how big is the whole, and is it growing?" The 100% chartA stacked bar where every bar is the same height, so only each part's share shows. answers "did the mix change?" For P&G, the second answer is "barely." When the parts grow at similar rates, the shares hold still even as the total grows, and a flat 100% chart is a finding in itself.

Stacked barsA bar split into parts, showing both the total and its pieces. have one weakness. Only the bottom segment and the total sit on a shared baselineWhere a chart's value axis starts. For bars it should be zero.. The segments in the middle float, so comparing them from bar to bar is guesswork. If the middle segment is the story, give it its own chart or a table.

Waterfalls: walk from one number to the next

A waterfallA chart that walks from one number to another in steps that add up, such as last year's sales to this year's., also called a bridge, starts at one number and adds and subtracts the pieces until it lands on another. Finance teams use it for profit; marketers use it for sales growth, split into volume (how many units), price and mix (selling more of the pricier or cheaper items).

In the quarter that ended September 30, 2026, two P&G segments sold about the same amount more than a year earlier, measured in units. Yet Beauty's sales grew 6.4% and Baby, Feminine & Family Care's grew 2.9%. P&G notes that these percentages are approximations from formulas4. That's why the pieces in the waterfalls below can miss the total by a tenth, shown as a rounding step.

Same volume, very different growth. Of the 3.5-point gap between the two segments, price made 1.9 points, foreign exchange 1.2 and mix 0.7. Read a waterfall in three moves: where it starts, the biggest step and its sign, where it ends. Then ask the question the chart raises, such as why one segment could raise prices while the other cut them.

Retention curves: what's left of a group over time

A retention curveA chart of the share of a cohort still active as time passes. follows one cohortA group that started at the same time, such as customers who signed up in the same month. and shows the share still around at each age. The Bureau of Labor Statistics tracks this for new business locations. Of the 677,876 private-sector establishments, meaning single business locations, that opened in the year to March 2015, 79.6% were still open a year later, 50.2% after five years and 32.6% by March 20265.

Read a retention curve in three moves: how steep the first drop is, where the curve flattens and whether newer cohorts sit above or below older ones. The 2022 cohort kept 76.3% after one year, below the 79.6% of the 2015 cohort and the 80.9% of the 2020 cohort5. Compare cohorts at the same age. A young cohort's line is shorter, not lower.

Subscription businesses read their customers the same way: a cohort per signup month, the share still paying at each age, and the hope that newer lines sit higher.

Common traps

  • Comparing back-to-back seasonal periods. A fourth quarter will beat a third quarter for most retailers. Compare the same quarter a year apart.
  • Reading the middle of a stack. Only the bottom segment and the total are easy to compare.
  • Forgetting what a waterfall's ends are. The first and last bars are totals (or zero, for a growth bridge). Everything between is a change.
  • Judging a young cohort by its length. Line cohorts up by age, not by calendar date.

In an interview

When you're handed a time chart, say whether it's adjusted before you say anything about the trend. With a stacked chart, read the total first, then the bottom segment, and ask for the numbers if the middle matters. With a bridge, name the biggest step and its sign, then ask why it moved. Each habit shows you know where these charts mislead.

Self-check: Pennywhistle, the made-up cold-brew brand from the drills, reports December sales up 30% on November. Its brand manager calls it a breakout month. What do you ask first?

How December compared with last December. Cold brew is seasonal in both directions, and December has holiday traffic and gift packs. A 30% jump on November could be normal, or even weak. Ask for the same month a year ago, or a seasonally adjusted series, before calling it a breakout.

Check your understanding

4 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. A sunscreen brand's unadjusted quarterly sales fell 45% from the third quarter to the fourth. Your manager asks if the brand is in trouble. What do you say first?

    Show answer

    Compare the fourth quarter with the fourth quarter a year earlier before deciding. A seasonal product always drops after summer. The useful comparison is the same quarter a year apart, or a seasonally adjusted series.

  2. Your director wants one chart to show that the app grew from a small slice to a big slice of sales. Which fits best?

    Show answer

    A 100% stacked bar chart of each channel's share by year. The message is about mix, so make every bar the same height and let the shares do the talking. Put the total's growth in the title or a second chart.

  3. A sales bridge goes from $200M to $203M: price +$9M, volume −$12M, new items +$6M. What's the headline?

    Show answer

    Sales grew only because price and new items covered a $12M volume loss. Name the biggest step and its sign first. Volume is the largest move, and it's negative, so the growth hides a shrinking base.

  4. Your June signups have one month of data and 74% are still subscribed. March signups were at 81% after one month. What can you say?

    Show answer

    June signups held on worse after one month, so check what changed in June. Comparing two cohorts at the same age is fair. Saying more than that needs more months of data.
