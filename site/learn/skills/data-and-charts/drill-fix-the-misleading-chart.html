Fix the misleading chart

SkillDrill 38 minIntermediate

Skills: Writing & storytellingBusiness math

The prompt

Hypothetical scenario: Pennywhistle Cold Brew is a made-up brand, and every number here is invented for practice.

Pennywhistle's ad agency sent this slide for the retailer meeting on Thursday. The footnote reads: "Data: grocery scanner data, 4 weeks ending June 7, during Pennywhistle's Summer Starter promotion."

You also know that the category leader, Brand A, sold 12.4 units per store per week in the same four weeks. In normal weeks, Pennywhistle sells about 9.6 units per store per week.

Find everything misleading about the slide, then say how you'd redraw it and what title you'd give it.

Your earlier answer

  1. Hint 1 of 3 Start with the vertical axisThe ruler along the side or bottom of a chart that says what each position means.. Where does it begin, and what does that do to the bar lengths?
  2. Hint 2 of 3 Then look at the footnote. What was happening in those four weeks?
  3. Hint 3 of 3 Finally, ask who's missing from the comparison, and write a title the numbers can back up.

Sample answer

What's misleading

  1. The axis starts at 10, not zero. Pennywhistle's bar is 1.8 units tall above the cut and the category's is 0.9, so it looks twice as big. The real gap is 11.8 against 10.9, about 8%.
  2. The window is a promotion. All four weeks were during Pennywhistle's own Summer Starter deal. In normal weeks it sells about 9.6 units per store, which would sit below this category average. That isn't quite like-for-like, since the category average was measured during the promotion weeks, so ask for normal-week category data too.
  3. The leader is missing. Brand A sold 12.4 units per store per week. Leaving it out makes Pennywhistle look like the top seller.

The redraw

Bars from zero, sorted, with Brand A included, and a note that the four weeks were promoted for Pennywhistle.

Better still for the retailer: show Pennywhistle's promoted weeks next to its normal weeks, because the lift from the deal is the real story.

Show the math

How big the bars look = (value − where the axis starts) ÷ (other value − where the axis starts).

  • Apparent: (11.8 − 10) ÷ (10.9 − 10) = 1.8 ÷ 0.9 = 2.0, which is where "twice as fast" came from.
  • Real: 11.8 ÷ 10.9 = 1.08, so Pennywhistle sold 8% more per store.

Why: a bar's length runs from the axis start. Cut the axis and you cut off the same amount from every bar, which shrinks the small one most.

Promotion lift = promoted rate ÷ normal rate − 1: 11.8 ÷ 9.6 − 1 = 23% more units per store while on deal.

Common mistake: fixing the axis but keeping the title. "Twice as fast" was never true.

Why it works

  • It catches all three problems: one visual (the axis), one reasoning (the window) and one omission (the leader).
  • Every fix is quantified, so the agency can't argue it away.
  • The new title is a claim the data can support.

What interviewers push on

  • "The retailer only cares about the promotion. Isn't the slide fine?" Then say so in the title, include the leader, and draw from zero.
  • "Is 8% meaningful?" It depends on how much it varies week to week; ask for a few months of weekly data.
  • "What would you show the retailer instead?" The promotion's lift over normal weeks, and what it did to the category.

Practice twist

The agency's next slide has a dual axisTwo different value scales on one chart, one on each side.: Pennywhistle's sales as columns on the left, its ad spend as a line on the right, and the two rise together. What do you tell them, and what do you draw instead?
