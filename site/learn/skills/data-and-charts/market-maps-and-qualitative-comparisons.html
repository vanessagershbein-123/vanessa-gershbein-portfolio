Market maps and qualitative comparisons

SkillLesson 613 min

Goal: Read a Marimekko market map, use Harvey balls and heatmaps honestly, and say where the money is.

Skills: Positioning & strategy

AT&T, T-Mobile and Verizon don't split the wireless market the same way. Which kind of plan is the bigger business, and who leads each kind? At the end of 2025, counting in thousands as the carriers report them, Verizon led in postpaid phones, with 93,868 thousand retail postpaid phone connections1. T-Mobile led in prepaid, with 25,943 thousand prepaid customers2. AT&T came third in both, with 74,214 thousand postpaid phone subscribers and 18,294 thousand prepaid3.

A market mapA chart of how a market splits by segment and by player. shows all of it, and how big each segment is, in one picture.

Marimekko charts: size and share at once

Guess first: which is the bigger pool of customers, postpaid phones or prepaid? By how much?

Read a MarimekkoA 100% stacked bar chart where each bar's width shows its segment's size, so it shows size and share at once. in three passes:

  1. Across: how big is each segment? Postpaid phone is 253,676 thousand of the 317,082 thousand total, about 80%. Prepaid is the other 20%.
  2. Up: who leads each segment? Verizon leads postpaid phones with about 37%. In prepaid, T-Mobile leads with about 41%.
  3. Area: who holds the most of the whole? Each rectangle's area is that carrier's slice of the total. Verizon's postpaid rectangle is the biggest single block. Add each carrier's two rectangles, though, and Verizon holds about 35.6% of the total and T-Mobile about 35.2%, a near tie you can't see by eye, because areas in different columns are hard to compare.

Put together out loud:

  • Units and axesThe ruler along the side or bottom of a chart that says what each position means.: "AT&T, T-Mobile and Verizon at the end of 2025, counted in thousands. Each column's width is the segment's size, and the split inside it is each carrier's share of that segment."
  • One point: "T-Mobile has about 41% of prepaid."
  • Biggest movement: "Postpaid phones are about 80% of these counts, and Verizon leads them with about 37%. Across both columns, Verizon and T-Mobile are close to tied."
  • So what: "Most of the customers are in postpaid phones, and the leaders there are close. Before calling either segment the bigger prize, I'd want revenue per customer in each."

The FT calls this kind of chart a good way to show size and proportion at the same time, as long as the data aren't too complicated4. That condition matters. With more than a handful of segments or players, the thin slices stop being readable, and the Nielsen Norman Group lists mosaic charts among the types people find harder to process5.

Two cautions before you say "where the money is." First, a market map shows customers or sales, not profit; a big segment with thin margins can be worth less than a small, rich one. Second, check that every column and every player counts the same thing. Here, the postpaid column counts phones only, while the prepaid column counts every prepaid device; one carrier reports connections, one customers and one subscribers; and Verizon's prepaid count leaves out a brand1. Say so out loud when you present it.

Harvey balls: a judgment, made visible

Harvey ballsCircles filled by quarters to show how well something meets a criterion. fill a circle by quarters to show how well something meets a criterion. Consultants use them to compare products, vendors or options on criteria that don't reduce to one number. They're honest only when the key is clear and the facts behind each ball are one click away.

An illustration: our own ratings, informed by the FT and Nielsen Norman Group guidance cited here, not a dataset.

The weakness of Harvey balls is that they hide the scale. Does three quarters mean "good" or "75% of the features"? Write the key under the chart, and keep the facts behind each rating in a table or appendix.

Heatmaps: patterns in a grid

A heatmapA grid where each cell's color shows its value. colors each cell of a table by its value, so patterns jump out before you read a number. Here is how food prices changed each year for eight grocery categories.

Read a heatmap three ways: along a row (one category over time), down a column (one year across categories), then hunt for the outliersA data point far from the rest.. Down the 2022 column, every category rose by at least 5%. Along the eggs row, 2022 and 2025 dwarf everything else. Three cells are small price declines, but you can barely see them, because the egg spike stretches the color scale.

Watch the color scale. One extreme value, like eggs in 2022, stretches the scale so that ordinary changes look pale. If the outlier isn't the story, cap the scale or note the outlier separately.

Common traps

  • Reading a Marimekko's heights as sizes. Height is share within a segment. Area is share of the whole.
  • Comparing players who count differently. Customers, connections and subscribers aren't always the same thing.
  • Harvey balls without a key. A reader can't tell what three quarters means.
  • A heatmap ruled by one outlier. Everything else fades to the same pale color.

In an interview

When you're shown a market map, name the biggest segment, the leader in each segment, then the white spaceA large part of a market where a brand is small or absent.: a big segment where your client is small. Then say what you'd need to know before chasing it, such as margins, how loyal customers are and what it costs to win them.

Self-check: Pennywhistle's agency (Pennywhistle is the made-up brand from the drills) rates three new bottle designs with Harvey balls on shelf impact, premium feel and brand fit. Design B gets three full circles. What do you ask before you believe it?

Ask for the key and the evidence behind each circle: who rated the designs, how many shoppers saw them and against what standard. A full circle from the agency's own team is an opinion. A full circle from a shelf test with a few hundred shoppers is evidence. Ask for the scores behind the circles before choosing B.

Check your understanding

3 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. In a Marimekko of a beverage market, your brand's slice in the Convenience column is the tallest in that column, but the column is very narrow. What does that tell you?

    Show answer

    You lead a small segment, so your slice of the whole market is small. Height is share within the segment, and width is the segment's size. A tall slice in a narrow column is a small rectangle.

  2. A colleague's slide rates four competitors with Harvey balls on "ease of use." The VP asks what a half-filled ball means. What should the slide have had?

    Show answer

    A key under the chart and the facts or scores behind each rating. Harvey balls are judgments. A key and the evidence behind them make the judgment checkable.

  3. In a heatmap of weekly sales by store, one store's holiday week is ten times any other cell, and every other cell looks the same pale color. What do you do?

    Show answer

    Cap the color scale or call out the outlier, so the rest of the pattern shows. An extreme value stretches the color scale. Capping it, or noting it separately, brings back the pattern in the other cells.
