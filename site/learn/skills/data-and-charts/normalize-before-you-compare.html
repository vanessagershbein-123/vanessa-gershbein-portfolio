Normalize before you compare

SkillLesson 212 min

Goal: Put numbers on a fair footing before comparing them: per store, per person, per visitor and indexed to 100.

Skills: Business math

Starbucks brought in $37.9 billion in fiscal 20261. Dutch Bros, the drive-thru coffee chain, had 1,273 shops at the end of September 2026, 960 of them company-operated2. On totals, it isn't a contest. So is a Starbucks busier than a Dutch Bros?

Totals can't answer that. To compare a giant with a challenger, divide by something they share: stores, people, visitors or a starting point. That's normalizingDivide by a shared base, such as stores, people or visitors, so things of different sizes can be compared..

Per store: the same coffee, two very different chains

Guess first: Starbucks has more than ten times as many company-operated stores in North America as Dutch Bros has in total. Does each Starbucks also take in far more?

In North America, Starbucks' company-operated coffeehouses brought in $26.4 billion in fiscal 2026. Starbucks closed more of them than it opened during the year and ended with 10,9021. That works out to about $2.4 million a store.

Dutch Bros does the division for you. It reports average unit volumeAverage unit volume: average yearly sales per store or restaurant., which it calculates by dividing shops' net sales by the number of shops, using the trailing twelve months of sales. For company-operated shops, that was $2,228 thousand in the period to September 2026. The average covers a comparable base of 724 company-operated shops, not all 960, and Dutch Bros says it uses these metrics as an indicator of shop growth and of what mature locations can expect2.

Look at the denominatorsThe number you divide by when you turn a total into a rate or an average. before you trust the comparison. The Starbucks figure counts a full year of sales, including sales from stores that closed during the year, but divides only by the stores still open at year end. The Dutch Bros figure covers only its comparable base of more mature shops, which tends to flatter an average, since new shops are still ramping up. Two "sales per store" figures can be built differently, so ask how each one was made before you call a winner.

Per person and per visitor

The same logic works for places and websites.

For example, say your cold brew sells $3 million a year in a state of 30 million people and $1 million in a state of 6 million. The big state wins on total. Per person, it's $0.10 against about $0.17, and the small state looks like the stronger market.

For example, say one retailer's site turns 20,000 visitors into 600 orders and another turns 6,000 visitors into 300. The first has twice the orders. Its conversion rateThe share of visitors or prospects who take an action, such as placing an order. is 3%, against 5% for the second. A funnel chart, which shows how many people make it from one step to the next, is a string of these rates.

Indexed to 100: a common starting line

An indexA series rescaled so a chosen starting point equals 100. An index of 120 means 20% above the start. rescales a seriesOne set of values that belong together on a chart, such as one channel's sales by quarter. so a chosen starting point equals 100. The Bureau of Labor Statistics explains it with the consumer price index: most of its series set the 1982 to 1984 average at 100, so an index of 110 means prices are 10% higher than in that base periodThe starting point an index is measured from, where the index equals 100.3.

Indexing lets you put two things measured in different units on one axisThe ruler along the side or bottom of a chart that says what each position means.. Coffee is priced in dollars per pound. The consumer price index is an index of all prices. Set both to 100 in September 2020 and you can see which grew faster.

Coffee went from $4.49 a pound in September 2020 to $9.33 in September 20264. The consumer price index went from 260.280 to 336.940 over the same six years4. To index any series, divide each value by the base-period value and multiply by 100: $9.33 ÷ $4.49 × 100 is about 208, and 336.940 ÷ 260.280 × 100 is about 129.

Here's the routine on this chart, out loud:

  • Units and axes: "Two lines, each set to 100 in September 2020: the average retail price of ground coffee and the consumer price index for all items, every September through 2026. The axis starts at zero."
  • One point: "In September 2026, coffee's index was 208, so the price was a little more than double its 2020 level."
  • Biggest movement: "The two lines moved together for the first year. Then coffee jumped in 2022 and again in 2025, while prices overall rose about 30% in six years."
  • So what: "Coffee costs ran far ahead of prices in general. I'd ask how much of that coffee brands passed on to shoppers, and what happened to the units they sold."

Common traps

  • Comparing totals across different sizes. Big beats small on totals almost every time.
  • Mixing denominators. Year-end stores, average stores and mature stores give different "per store" numbers.
  • Comparing rates without counts. A 50% conversion rate on 10 visitors means little.
  • Forgetting the base. An index of 130 says nothing until you know what equals 100.

In an interview

When you see two totals side by side, ask what you should divide by before you compare them: stores, customers, people or a starting year. Then say which denominator you chose and why. That one question often turns a wrong answer into the right one.

Robert Kosara, a data visualization researcher, explains what index charts are for and how they make series of different sizes comparable.

Self-check: Pennywhistle, the made-up brand from the drills, sells $40 million a year of cold brew through 8,000 grocery stores. A rival sells $15 million through 2,000. Which brand sells more per store, and what would you check next?

Pennywhistle sells $5,000 a store a year ($40 million ÷ 8,000). The rival sells $7,500 ($15 million ÷ 2,000), so its stores sell half again as much. Next, check the denominator: are the rival's stores bigger or in busier locations, and did both brands count stores the same way, say at year end or on average?

Check your understanding

3 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. Your brand's sales grew from $10M to $12M while the category grew from $200M to $260M. Your manager calls it a great year. What's the fairest response?

    Show answer

    Sales grew 20% but the category grew 30%, so the brand lost share. Normalize growth against the category. Growing slower than the category means losing share, even when your own sales rise.

  2. You're comparing a new product's sales in two regions. Region West has 3 times the stores of Region East and twice the sales. Where is the product selling better per store?

    Show answer

    East, because West's sales per store are only two-thirds of East's. West has 2× the sales on 3× the stores, so per store it sells 2/3 as much as East.

  3. A chart indexes two brands' sales to 100 in 2022. In 2026, Brand A is at 140 and Brand B is at 120. What can you say?

    Show answer

    Brand A's sales grew faster than Brand B's since 2022. An index shows growth from a shared starting line, not size. Brand B could still be the bigger brand.
