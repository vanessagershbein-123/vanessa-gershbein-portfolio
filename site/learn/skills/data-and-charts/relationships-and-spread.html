Relationships and spread: scatter plots, bubbles and histograms

SkillLesson 515 min

Goal: Read how two measures move together, explain r² in plain words, keep correlation and cause apart, and describe a spread by its shape.

Skills: Research & insight

eBay once tested its own ads the hard way: it ran large field experiments designed to measure what its paid search ads actually caused. Because search clicks and purchases are correlatedHow tightly two measures move together in a straight line, from −1 to 1. anyway, Thomas Blake, Chris Nosko and Steven Tadelis found that the returns from paid search were a fraction of what the usual non-experimental estimates showed. For ads on eBay's own brand name, they found no measurable short-term benefit1.

A newer study points the same way. Brett Gordon, Robert Moakler and Florian Zettelmeyer analyzed 663 large advertising experiments at Facebook. Their results suggest that state-of-the-art observational methods, the kind that study ads without a test, can't recover the causal effect of online advertising there2.

Both studies warn about the same trap. Two measures that rise and fall together, like ads and sales, don't prove that one drives the other. A scatter plotA chart with one dot per item, placed by two measures, to show whether they move together. shows you whether they move together. Only an experiment, or very careful reasoning, gets you to cause.

Scatter plots: does one thing move with another?

Guess first: do states with more college graduates have higher household incomes? How tightly would you expect the two to line up?

Each dot below is a US state. Across: the share of adults 25 and over with at least a bachelor's degree. Up: medianThe middle value when values are sorted. Half are above it and half below. household income. Both come from the Census Bureau's 2025 American Community Survey. Massachusetts has the highest degree share, 49.2%, and West Virginia the lowest, 24.7%. Nevada sits at 29.0% and Vermont at 44.5%3.

Yet Nevada's median household income, $85,198, is almost the same as Vermont's, $86,0183. Two states, 15 points apart on degrees, same income. A scatter plot shows the pattern and its exceptions at once.

Two numbers summarize the pattern. Correlation, written r, runs from −1 to 1 and measures the strength and direction of a straight-line relationship4. r²The share of the variation in one measure that a straight trend line explains, from 0 to 1. is the share of the variation in the up-axisThe ruler along the side or bottom of a chart that says what each position means. measure that the least-squares lineThe straight line that best fits the dots in a scatter plot. explains4.

For these 50 states, r is 0.85 and r² is 0.73. In plain words: the line explains about three quarters of the state-to-state differences in median income, and something else explains the rest. The line rises about $2,000 of median income for each extra point of degree share. Add Washington, D.C., which sits far to the right of every state, and r drops to 0.82. One dot can move the summary, so look at the plot before you trust the number.

Out loud, the routine sounds like this:

  • Units and axes: "One dot per US state in 2025. Across is the share of adults 25 and over with a bachelor's degree; up is median household income, starting at $50,000 rather than zero, which is fine for a scatter plot."
  • One point: "Vermont: 44.5% with a degree, and a median income of $86,018."
  • Biggest movement: "For a scatter plot, that's the pattern and its biggest exception. The dots climb from lower left to upper right, and a straight line explains about three quarters of the differences. Nevada stands out, with about Vermont's income and far fewer graduates."
  • So what: "Degree share and income travel together across states, but this chart can't say why. If I were choosing states for a premium launch, I'd use it to build a shortlist, then test."

What the chart can't tell you is why. Degrees might raise earnings. High-paying industries might attract graduates. Cost of living might lift both. That third kind of factor is a confounderA third factor that moves two measures at once, making them look linked., and a scatter plot can't rule it out.

Bubble charts: a third measure as size

A bubble chartA scatter plot where each dot's size shows a third measure. is a scatter plot with one more measure shown as dot size. P&G's five segments make a good one. In fiscal 2026, Beauty grew net sales 7%, the fastest of the five, but its net earnings were 16.7% of its sales, the lowest. Grooming, the smallest at $6.9 billion, earned the highest share, 22.1%. Fabric & Home Care was the biggest, at $30.3 billion5.

Put the two measures you most want compared on the axes, and use size for context, such as how big each business is. People judge area far less precisely than position, so nobody should have to compare two bubbles to make your point.

Histograms: the shape of a spread

A histogramBars that count how many values fall in each range of one measure. splits one measure into ranges, called bins, and counts what falls in each. The chart below regroups the Census Bureau's 2025 household income brackets into $25,000 bins.

Read a histogram for its shape. These bars add up to more than half of households by the $75,000–100,000 bin, so the median household sits in that bin. Watch the last two bars. The $150,000–200,000 bin is twice as wide as the others, and $200,000 and up has no top at all, so both look taller than a same-width bin would. Check that bins are equal before comparing heights.

A spread with a long tail of high values is called right-skewedA spread with a long tail of high values. Its average sits above its median., and in one the average usually lands above the median. When you describe a typical customer, order or household in a skewed spread, use the median.

Common traps

  • Calling a correlation a cause. Ask what else could move both measures, and whether anyone ran a test.
  • Trusting r without the plot. One outlierA data point far from the rest. can raise or lower it a lot.
  • Reading bubble sizes precisely. Put the measure that matters on an axis.
  • Comparing bins of different widths. A wider bin collects more just by being wider.

In an interview

With a scatter plot, say what each dot is and what each axis measures. Then give the direction and strength, name one outlier and say what you'd need before claiming cause: an experiment, or a holdout group that doesn't get the treatment. With a histogram, describe the shape before you quote an average.

StatQuest's Josh Starmer explains what correlation measures, and what it leaves out, with simple drawings.

Self-check: Pennywhistle, the made-up brand from the drills, finds that grocery stores running in-store sampling sell more of its cold brew than stores that don't, with r = 0.6 across 80 stores. The sales team wants sampling in every store. What do you say?

Something like: "Sampling and sales move together, but the stores that got sampling may be the busy ones that already sell well. Before we roll it out, let's run it in a random half of similar stores for six weeks and compare against the other half." The correlation is a reason to test, not proof that sampling works.

Check your understanding

4 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. A scatter of 40 markets shows ad spend against sales with r² = 0.64. Your CMO says ads explain 64% of sales. What's the most accurate reply?

    Show answer

    The line explains 64% of the variation in sales across markets, which shows association, not how much ads caused. r² describes how well a line fits. Bigger markets may simply get more ads and have more sales, so a holdout test is what measures cause.

  2. A histogram of order sizes for your app has two humps, one around 2 bottles and one around 12. What should you do before reporting an average order?

    Show answer

    Split the orders by customer type, because two groups are probably mixed together. Two humps usually mean two kinds of buyers, such as households and offices. One average would describe neither.

  3. Removing one store moves r from 0.3 to 0.7 in your scatter plot. What do you do?

    Show answer

    Look at that store, report both and explain what makes it different. An outlier is information. It might be a data error, or it might be the most interesting store you have.

  4. Your bubble chart puts growth across, margin up and sales as bubble size. Which comparison will readers get least right?

    Show answer

    Whether one unit's sales are twice another's. Readers judge positions on an axis well and areas poorly, so the sizes are the weakest part of the chart.
