Start from the question: picking a chart

SkillLesson 312 min

Goal: Pick a chart by finishing the sentence "I want to show...", and explain why a bar usually beats a pie.

Skills: Writing & storytelling

Before you pick a chart, finish one sentence: "I want to show ___." The Financial Times built a reference guide, the Visual Vocabulary, around that habit. It asks you to decide which data relationship matters most in your story, then look at the chart types in that category1. Pick the relationship first and the chart nearly picks itself.

Words to know

Term What it means A quick example
Relationship What you want the reader to see in the data Change over time, ranking, parts of a whole
Magnitude How big things are Sales by brand
Ranking Order from biggest to smallest Top five flavors
Part-to-whole How a total splits into pieces Sales by channel
Distribution How values are spread out Order sizes
Correlation Whether two measures move together Price and volume
Deviation How far values sit from a reference Sales vs. target
Encoding How a chart turns a number into something you see Bar length, line position, slice angle, bubble area

The question picks the chart

The FT's guide sorts charts into nine relationships: deviation, correlationHow tightly two measures move together in a straight line, from −1 to 1., change over time, ranking, distribution, part-to-whole, magnitude, spatial and flow1. Here's how common interview questions map onto them:

If the question is... The relationship Start with
How has it changed over time? Change over time Line (columns for a few periods)
Which is biggest? Ranking or magnitude Bars, sorted largest first
What is it made of? Part-to-whole Stacked bar; a pie only for two or three parts
How is it spread out? Distribution Histogram
Does one thing move with another? Correlation Scatter plot
How far off target are we? Deviation Bars above and below a zero line
Where is it happening? Spatial Map
How did we get from A to B? Flow Waterfall

If nine is too many to hold in your head, Andrew Abela's chart chooser boils it down to one question, "What would you like to show?", with four answers: comparison, distribution, composition and relationship2. Either way, the order is the same: the message first, the chart second.

Why a bar usually beats a pie

In 1984, William Cleveland and Robert McGill ranked the basic judgments people make when they read a graph by how accurately people make them, and advised building graphs from the tasks highest in that ranking3. In 2010, Jeffrey Heer and Michael Bostock replicated earlier studies of how people read these visual encodings, this time with crowdsourced participants4. The practical upshot, as the Nielsen Norman Group puts it, is that bar charts let people quickly and accurately see the differences between values, while charts that ask readers to judge angle or area, such as pies and bubbles, are harder to process5.

Try it. Here's how Procter & Gamble's five segments split their fiscal 2026 sales, first as a pie, then as bars. Guess first: from the pie alone, is Beauty closer in size to Health Care or to Baby, Feminine & Family Care?

In the pie, you can tell Fabric & Home Care is the biggest. Is Beauty closer to Health Care or to Baby, Feminine & Family Care? You have to squint. In the bars, the answer is on the page, because every bar starts from the same line and you're comparing lengths. The FT's notes make the same point about pies: they're common for parts of a whole, but it's hard to compare the sizes of the segments accurately1.

The routine works on a ranking too. Read the bars out loud:

  • Units and axesThe ruler along the side or bottom of a chart that says what each position means.: "Each of P&G's five segments as a share of their combined net sales in fiscal 2026, sorted from largest, with the axis starting at zero."
  • One point: "Beauty is 18.6%."
  • Biggest movement: "For a ranking, that's the biggest gap: Fabric & Home Care is about a third of the total, more than 11 points ahead of the next segment, and Grooming is the smallest at 8%."
  • So what: "More than half of P&G's sales come from household and family products. A 10% swing in Fabric & Home Care moves the company about four times as much as the same swing in Grooming."

A pie can still work when there are only two or three parts, one share clearly dominates, and the reader needs only a rough sense of the split.

Sometimes the answer isn't a chart

If the point is one number, say the number. If the reader needs a handful of exact values, a small table beats a chart. Use a chart when the shape of the data is the message: a trend, a gap, a cluster.

Common traps

  • Picking the chart first. Starting from a favorite chart type bends the message to fit it.
  • Unsorted bars. When the question is "which is biggest," sort the bars.
  • A pie for close values. If the slices are within a few points of each other, use bars.
  • Charting one number. A single bar or a two-slice pie is a sentence in disguise.

In an interview

When a case asks you to sketch or choose a chart, say the message first ("I want to show that the app is growing fastest"), then the relationship ("that's change over time across channels"), then the chart ("a line chart with the app highlighted"). Saying all three shows the interviewer how you think.

In this talk at the OutlierA data point far from the rest. conference, Steve Franconeri explains what our eyes do quickly, and what they do slowly, when we read a chart.

Self-check: Pennywhistle's brand director (Pennywhistle is the made-up brand from the drills) asks, "Which of our five flavors sold the most units last quarter, and by how much?" Which chart do you build, and how do you set it up?

Horizontal bars, one per flavor, sorted from most to fewest units, with the value printed at the end of each bar. The question is a ranking with a size gap, so a shared baselineWhere a chart's value axis starts. For bars it should be zero. and sorted order answer both parts at a glance.

Check your understanding

3 questions. Pick an answer to see if you're right.

  1. The VP wants to see how each of four channels' shares of sales shifted over five years. Which chart fits best?

    Show answer

    A 100% stacked bar per year, or a line per channel's share. Change in parts of a whole over time is best read with the parts lined up across years. Pies side by side make readers compare angles across circles.

  2. A teammate's draft shows eight competitors' market shares in a pie, several of them between 9% and 12%. What's your first suggestion?

    Show answer

    Switch to bars sorted by share. Close values are exactly where pies fail. Sorted bars on a shared baseline make small gaps readable.

  3. You need to show that 72% of surveyed shoppers prefer your new package. What's the best form?

    Show answer

    Just the number, stated plainly, perhaps with the question asked and the sample size. One number is a sentence, not a chart. Give the number its context and move on.
